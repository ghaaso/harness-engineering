Before you start
How to read this
This is the one place where everything we have learned about our build harness sits together. I wrote it so that anyone on the team, including people who were not in the working sessions this record comes from, can pick a piece of work, see why it matters and see what has already been tried. Throughout, “I” is me and “we” is the team.
Every number says how sure we are of it
 | M | Measured | Counted or timed on our own machine or in our own records.
 | E | Estimated | Worked out from measured data with a stated assumption.
 | G | Guess | A judgement, written down so a measurement can replace it.
 | S | Setting or outside figure | A configured value, or a number from an outside study or vendor.
 | T | Target | A bar we set for ourselves to clear.
 | I | Illustrative | An example that shows the arithmetic. It is not a result.
Every idea shows how far it has got
 | Idea | Idea. Proposed, or picked out of an outside source to try. Not yet designed.
 | Designed | Designed. Written into a design or plan with its test, or chosen for adoption. Not built yet.
 | Built | Built. It exists, but has not been measured or is not switched on yet.
 | Tested | Tested. Measured, trialled or replayed, with results in. The results may be strong, modest or a lesson.
 | In use | In use. Part of how we work today.
 | Parked | Parked. We chose not to pursue it for now, or a better idea replaced it. It stays in the record with the reason, so nobody has to rediscover it.

Ideas are numbered by theme: MAC-03 is the third idea in the machine theme. The twelve theme codes are listed on the first page of Part 3.

R2, R4 and B3-LOOP are loops of harness v2 (our 11 November design for the whole harness, set out in Part 1) that are bottlenecks in their own right. W1 to W5 (weaknesses) and L1 and L3 (two of its three levers, not the four token levers of Part 2) come from the Dex Horthy sweep, our card-by-card study of Dex Horthy’s talks and writing, which scored every outside idea against them. Ids that start with TK (tokens), LIB (the library, the knowledge store and the graph test, our held-out test of a typed code store), MC (the machine and the check) or PR (process; not a pull request) were added in this compile for bottlenecks that had no id yet.

The short version
I build software with AI agents. The harness is everything around them: how I split work into sessions, the checks that decide whether work is right, and the memory that carries what we learn. I judge it by seven metrics: throughput, efficiency, token count, token cost, time taken, quality of output and hallucinations. This reference edition says what limits each one, what we have measured, and every idea we have against it, including the ones we only talked about.

1
The window keeps growing, and that is the first bottleneck. Every call re-reads the whole window, so each step costs more than the last, and the orchestrator’s window reached 574k to 974k tokens M before it compacted. Both summaries we checked were then wrong about the latest state M. My proposal is to run orchestration as a B+ tree spread over sessions, as in a distributed system: a session past about 40 % S of its window (set per model) splits, small ones merge, and we may never have to compact. WP12 wrote the rule and replayed it on our record: tokens are not settled against compacting earlier, and its first aim, fewer hallucinations, needs a live trial. I said yes to one trial wave at 300k, ahead of most of our compactions.
2
Hallucinations and quality have catches, not yet a rate. We recorded 20-plus overstated reports, with no denominator M, and those two wrong summaries M. 64 % M of reviews sent a slice back, and an Opus reviewer found a serious defect in 9 of 14 M Opus-built slices. Two things should bring the numbers down: claims that a script rechecks instead of a model, and windows that stay small (not yet measured). A script rechecked 1,265 M paths, commits and pull requests in FixFlow’s summaries: 16 M were not borne out, no more than in maintained documents; the latest state needs a reader. WP8 counts reports next.
3
One orchestrator and one usage limit cap throughput. Review, merging and checks all pass through one session, and when the five-hour limit hits, every lane stops: ten were stopped within about 15 minutes M. The testbed had about three lanes worth running M but ran six to nine M. The planner, the merge queue and the governor are designed to answer this.
4
Every agent pays a fixed start, often on the most expensive model. About 70k tokens M per clinic agent before any work, 27 % E of all our tokens. A short tool list should shrink it; model tiers by role are in use, and raising only the stuck problem to the top model is designed but not yet measured.
5
Most steps only find and re-read. 64 % M of FixFlow’s calls and 52 % M of the clinic’s only read or searched, each one a full re-read. A typed code store found more of what a change touched than a scripted search did (+0.076 overall M, the gain in the mobile app). The test with an agent in the loop is next.
6
What one session learns, the next does not have. The library and the knowledge store are designed, not built. The ensemble of simulated runs I suggested on 12 November helped: it showed which unknowns matter most. The same model’s corner settings (MEM-15) show the library pays only when work comes back, reaches the cheap model and is right, all together. Neither can pick a path, because the inputs are still guesses G.
7
A lot already works, and the work splits cleanly. The clinic’s full check is 3.0× faster in parallel M and 1.6× faster in a Linux container M. Sixteen packages each have a goal, a first deliverable and a “done when”: one has begun, six can start today, six start on my word and three follow another package.
What I need from you. Read this page and Part 4, take a package (two people can share one) and write your name on its card. Parts 2 and 3 are the reference for your package: the bottlenecks it answers and every idea we already have for it.
Part 1
What we are building
FixFlow is the product. The harness is how we build it. This part says what the harness is, where our ideas came from, and the design that ties them together.
What I mean by the harness
When I say harness I mean everything we wrap around the AI models to build software with them:
Sessions and agents. A session is one conversation with Claude Code, Anthropic’s coding tool, which reads and edits code and runs commands on our PC. A session can start subagents (helpers that each begin with a fresh context) and workflows (scripts that run many subagents to a fixed plan).
Checks and gates. The test runs and reviews that decide whether a piece of work is good enough to merge into the main line of code.
Memory. Whatever carries what we learned from one session to the next: notes, the library we have designed, the knowledge store.
The token budget. A token is the unit a model reads and writes in, a short piece of text: a common word is often one token, a long word several. The context window is everything the model holds in mind for one call, and it re-reads the whole window on every call. Our Claude plan allows a limited amount of use in each five-hour window, so tokens are the resource every part of the harness shares.
The machine. The one PC everything runs on: its power, memory, disk and Docker (the tool that runs our databases and Linux test runs in containers).
How results reach me. Reports, visual pages and decision forms.
The word comes from software testing, where a test harness is the code that runs the tests. Teams that build with AI agents now use it for the whole system around a model.
Where the ideas came from
Two of our own projects feed this record:
FixFlow, the workshop management system we are building for a home-appliance repair shop in Karachi: a web app for the manager, an Android app for technicians and a backend in FastAPI (a Python web framework). Its build is laid out in the ship plan, the document that orders FixFlow’s work into phases (P0a, P0b and on).
The clinic testbed, an earlier large build on this same PC that we used to push the harness hard: 432 commits and 65 merges in eight working days M. Most of our measured bottlenecks come from its record.
Two outside sources were studied card by card: talks and writing by Dex Horthy of HumanLayer, author of “12-Factor Agents” and of “Advanced Context Engineering for Coding Agents”, and BAML, BoundaryML’s language for typed model calls. The glossary says more about each.

How it fits together: harness v2
On 11 November we put what we had learned into one design, harness v2. It treats the harness as one system with four scarce things: time on the check, review, the one-at-a-time merge, and tokens per five-hour window. Each scarce thing gets a part that controls it.
Speed = lanes × what one lane delivers, until the lowest limit. A lane is one session building one slice of work. More lanes help only until the first of the four limits is reached. Past that point, the knee, they add queues, tokens and collisions, and no speed.

The loops
The loop labels come from system dynamics, the method Jay Forrester began at MIT in the 1950s for studying systems as stocks, flows and feedback. A reinforcing loop (R) feeds itself: more of it makes more of it. A balancing loop (B) pushes back toward a limit.
LoopNameWhat happensWhat we saw in the testbed
 | R1 | The fix spiral | A fix adds new stored state (a hold, lock, marker or cutoff), and one of the ways that state can end is never tested. That becomes a new defect, which needs another fix and another check. | 2.8 M review rounds per slice; 64 % M of reviews sent the slice back.
 | B1 | Check capacity | Every round needs the check, and the check can run only as many rounds at once as it has slots, each taking a full run time. Past that, lanes queue. | One slot in practice (a second opened only with 8 GiB S free, which never happened) and 367 s M per run on Windows. Longest wait 2,030 s M.
 | B2 | The serial gate | Review and merging pass through something that takes one at a time. Nothing leaves faster than that. | The orchestrator briefed reviewers, read the last diff, ran the check twice and edited the wave board by hand.
 | B3 | The limit stop | Tokens rise with the lanes working and the context each carries. At the five-hour window’s limit every lane stops at once. | 14 M limit episodes in October; ten lanes run at once hit the limit within about 15 minutes M.
 | R2 | Context growth | Every call re-reads the whole context and adds to it, so each call costs more than the one before. | Sessions on Sonnet ran to 963k tokens M; sessions on Opus compacted near 367k M.
 | R4 | The pile-up | A waiting lane still holds its context. Waiting costs tokens, tokens bring the stop sooner, and after a stop everything restarts at once and waits again. | All sessions were released together on 9 November; one check then waited 2,030 s M.
 | R3 | The library compounds | Each merged slice writes what it learned. The next session asks the library before it explores, so it reads less, finishes sooner and writes more. | Not built yet. This is the loop we most want to start.

 | B5 | Lanes at the knee | More lanes help only until the lowest limit. Past it they add queues, tokens and collisions, and no speed. | Lanes were chosen by feel and nothing checked for collisions; for the first two slices, the first check on merged main failed on hand edits to shared documents M.

1The window keeps growing until it must compact or clear
LimitsToken countHallucinationsToken costQuality8 register rows · 30 ideas
What happens. Every call re-reads the whole window and adds to it, so our orchestrator and long-lived sessions accumulate tokens until they must compact or clear. The summary that then replaces the history can misstate the latest state.
What we measured. Cache reads are 96.8 % M of 7.35 B M context tokens, output 0.75 % M. The root compacted at 574k, 974k, 967k and 970k tokens M, though a second count of the same transcripts finds three compactions M. 2 of 2 summaries were wrong on the latest state M; the verbatim tail corrected both. Vendor figures, not reproduced here, put native compaction at 48.7 against 69.3 for the full history S (TOK-41). Our snapshot-and-restore trial matched native compaction on recall and task success but used 17.0 % more tokens M (TOK-12). Not yet measured. At what window fill a session’s judgement starts to fall, for any model, and whether a brief written at a split is right more often than a compaction summary.

Where the tokens go
We counted every model call on this PC for both projects: 35,834 calls M and 7.35 billion context tokens M. The finding is simple and useful. Token use is calls × window size. 97 % M of all tokens are the model re-reading a window it has already seen; what it writes is 0.75 % M. So the way to spend fewer tokens is fewer calls and smaller windows, not shorter answers.
Each bar is one project’s context tokens split by what they were. Wider = more of the tokens.
