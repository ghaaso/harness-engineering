1 The window keeps growing, and that is the first bottleneck. Every call re-reads the whole window, so each
      step costs more than the last, and the orchestrator's window reached 574k to 974k tokens M before it
      compacted. Both summaries we checked were then wrong about the latest state M . My proposal is to run
      orchestration as a B+ tree spread over sessions, as in a distributed system: a session past about 40 % S of its
      window (set per model) splits, small ones merge, and we may never have to compact. WP12 wrote the rule and
      replayed it on our record: tokens are not settled against compacting earlier, and its first aim, fewer
      hallucinations, needs a live trial. I said yes to one trial wave at 300k, ahead of most of our compactions.

2 Hallucinations and quality have catches, not yet a rate. We recorded 20-plus overstated reports, with no
      denominator M , and those two wrong summaries M . 64 % M of reviews sent a slice back, and an Opus
      reviewer found a serious defect in 9 of 14 M Opus-built slices. Two things should bring the numbers down:
      claims that a script rechecks instead of a model, and windows that stay small (not yet measured). A script
      rechecked 1,265 M paths, commits and pull requests in FixFlow's summaries: 16 M were not borne out, no
      more than in maintained documents; the latest state needs a reader. WP8 counts reports next.

3 One orchestrator and one usage limit cap throughput. Review, merging and checks all pass through one
      session, and when the five-hour limit hits, every lane stops: ten were stopped within about 15 minutes M . The
      testbed had about three lanes worth running M but ran six to nine M . The planner, the merge queue and the

PART 1

What we are building

FixFlow is the product. The harness is how we build it: everything we wrap around the AI models to build
software with them. The word comes from software testing, where a test harness is the code that runs the
tests; teams that build with AI agents now use it for the whole system around a model.

What the harness is made of

    Sessions and agents. A session is one conversation with Claude Code, Anthropic's coding tool, which reads and
    edits code and runs commands on our PC. A session can start subagents (helpers that each begin with a fresh
    context) and workflows (scripts that run many subagents to a fixed plan).

    Checks and gates. The test runs and reviews that decide whether a piece of work is good enough to merge into the
    main line of code.

    Memory. Whatever carries what we learned from one session to the next: notes, the library we have designed, the
    knowledge store.

    The token budget. A token is the unit a model reads and writes in, a short piece of text. The context window is
    everything the model holds in mind for one call, and it re-reads the whole window on every call. Our Claude plan
    allows a limited amount of use in each five-hour window, so tokens are the resource every part of the harness
    shares.
    The machine. The one PC everything runs on: its power, memory, disk and Docker (the tool that runs our databases
    and Linux test runs in containers).

    How results reach me. Reports, visual pages and decision forms.

Where the ideas came from

Two of our own projects feed this record. FixFlow is the workshop management system we are building for a home-
appliance repair shop in Karachi: a web app for the manager, an Android app for technicians and a backend in FastAPI
(a Python web framework). Its build is laid out in the ship plan, the document that orders FixFlow's work into phases
(P0a, P0b and on). The clinic testbed is an earlier large build on this same PC that we used to push the harness hard:
432 commits and 65 merges in eight working days M . Most of our measured bottlenecks come from its record. Two
outside sources were studied card by card: talks and writing by Dex Horthy of HumanLayer, and BAML, BoundaryML's
language for typed model calls. This edition also draws on what I typed in 59 M saved sessions since 6 October,
because some ideas were only ever said in chat.

1 The window keeps growing until it must compact or clear

What happens. Every call re-reads the whole window and adds to it, so our orchestrator and long-lived sessions accumulate
tokens until they must compact or clear. The summary that then replaces the history can misstate the latest state.

What we measured. Cache reads are 96.8 % M of 7.35 B M context tokens, output 0.75 % M . The root compacted at 574k,
974k, 967k and 970k tokens M , though a second count of the same transcripts finds three compactions M . 2 of 2 summaries
were wrong on the latest state M ; the verbatim tail corrected both. Vendor figures, not reproduced here, put native
compaction at 48.7 against 69.3 for the full history S (TOK-41). Our snapshot-and-restore trial matched native compaction on
recall and task success but used 17.0 % more tokens M (TOK-12). Not yet measured. At what window fill a session's
judgement starts to fall, for any model, and whether a brief written at a split is right more often than a compaction summary.

How this compile was made

The reference edition came first, following our orchestration design: Sonnet agents extracted every idea, bottleneck
and term from the sources with a citation each, fresh-context Opus agents checked the records and every number, and
nine fresh readers read the draft. On 14 November I changed the lens to the seven metrics and asked for the ideas that
lived only in chat. Agents then read all 376 M messages I typed in 59 M saved sessions and matched 113 M ideas,
observations and requests against the records: 5 M were in no record, and the earlier short edition had cut 31 M
more. Eight classifier agents sorted all 606 M earlier records by metric, and a blind second reading agreed on the
main metric for 80 of 101 M . Each block of Part 2 was written from its records and checked number by number by a
second agent. Four fresh readers then read this edition, one of them against my own message line by line, and skeptics
kept 49 M of their 73 M findings. A final check, against my message and against the records, kept 6 M of its 10 M
findings, four M separate errors, all corrected here. The same day WP12 wrote the split rule and replayed it over our
record, with two rounds of independent checking, and this edition carries the result.
