Part 1

What we are building

FixFlow is the product. The harness is how we build it: everything we wrap around the AI models to build software with them. The word comes from software testing, where a test harness is the code that runs the tests; teams that build with AI agents now use it for the whole system around a model.

What the harness is made of

  • Sessions and agents. A session is one conversation with Claude Code, Anthropic’s coding tool, which reads and edits code and runs commands on our PC. A session can start subagents (helpers that each begin with a fresh context) and workflows (scripts that run many subagents to a fixed plan).
  • Checks and gates. The test runs and reviews that decide whether a piece of work is good enough to merge into the main line of code.
  • Memory. Whatever carries what we learned from one session to the next: notes, the library we have designed, the knowledge store.
  • The token budget. A token is the unit a model reads and writes in, a short piece of text. The context window is everything the model holds in mind for one call, and it re-reads the whole window on every call. Our Claude plan allows a limited amount of use in each five-hour window, so tokens are the resource every part of the harness shares.
  • The machine. The one PC everything runs on: its power, memory, disk and Docker (the tool that runs our databases and Linux test runs in containers).
  • How results reach me. Reports, visual pages and decision forms.

Where the ideas came from

Two of our own projects feed this record. FixFlow is the workshop management system we are building for a home-appliance repair shop in Karachi: a web app for the manager, an Android app for technicians and a backend in FastAPI (a Python web framework). Its build is laid out in the ship plan, the document that orders FixFlow’s work into phases (P0a, P0b and on). The clinic testbed is an earlier large build on this same PC that we used to push the harness hard: 432 commits and 65 merges in eight working days M. Most of our measured bottlenecks come from its record. Two outside sources were studied card by card: talks and writing by Dex Horthy of HumanLayer, and BAML, BoundaryML’s language for typed model calls. This edition also draws on what I typed in 59 M saved sessions since 6 October, because some ideas were only ever said in chat.

How it fits together: harness v2

On 11 November we put what we had learned into one design, harness v2. It treats the harness as one system with four scarce things: time on the check, review, the one-at-a-time merge, and tokens per five-hour window. Each scarce thing gets a part that controls it.

Speed = lanes × what one lane delivers, until the lowest limit. A lane is one session building one slice of work. More lanes help only until the first of the four limits is reached. Past that point, the knee, they add queues, tokens and collisions, and no speed.

The blue labels are the loops of harness v2: R marks one that feeds itself, B one that pushes back toward a limit. Part 2 gives each its numbers under the bottleneck it belongs to.

1The window keeps growing until it must compact or clear

What happens. Every call re-reads the whole window and adds to it, so our orchestrator and long-lived sessions accumulate tokens until they must compact or clear. The summary that then replaces the history can misstate the latest state.

What we measured. Cache reads are 96.8 % M of 7.35 B M context tokens, output 0.75 % M. The root compacted at 574k, 974k, 967k and 970k tokens M, though a second count of the same transcripts finds three compactions M. 2 of 2 summaries were wrong on the latest state M; the verbatim tail corrected both. Vendor figures, not reproduced here, put native compaction at 48.7 against 69.3 for the full history S (TOK-41). Our snapshot-and-restore trial matched native compaction on recall and task success but used 17.0 % more tokens M (TOK-12). Not yet measured. At what window fill a session’s judgement starts to fall, for any model, and whether a brief written at a split is right more often than a compaction summary.

How this compile was made

The reference edition came first, following our orchestration design: Sonnet agents extracted every idea, bottleneck and term from the sources with a citation each, fresh-context Opus agents checked the records and every number, and nine fresh readers read the draft. On 14 November I changed the lens to the seven metrics and asked for the ideas that lived only in chat. Agents then read all 376 M messages I typed in 59 M saved sessions and matched 113 M ideas, observations and requests against the records: 5 M were in no record, and the earlier short edition had cut 31 M more. Eight classifier agents sorted all 606 M earlier records by metric, and a blind second reading agreed on the main metric for 80 of 101 M. Each block of Part 2 was written from its records and checked number by number by a second agent. Four fresh readers then read this edition, one of them against my own message line by line, and skeptics kept 49 M of their 73 M findings. A final check, against my message and against the records, kept 6 M of its 10 M findings, four M separate errors, all corrected here. The same day WP12 wrote the split rule and replayed it over our record, with two rounds of independent checking, and this edition carries the result.
