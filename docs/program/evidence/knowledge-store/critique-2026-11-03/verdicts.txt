- [supported] (6) tree-sitter recovers from syntax errors with ERROR and MISSING nodes, so a probe on a half-broken file still gets a tree. The implication is that such a probe cannot be treated as authoritative.
    The tree-sitter intro lists as a design goal 'Robust enough to provide useful results even in the presence of syntax errors'. The queries doc says 'When the parser encounters text it does not recognize, it represents this node as (ERROR) in the syntax tree'. MISSING nodes 'appear as seemingly normal nodes in the tree, but they are zero tokens wide', and 'these missing nodes are not captured by (ERROR) queries'. api.h says 'Missing nodes are inserted by the parser in order to recover from certain kinds of syntax errors'; ts_node_has_error is 'a syntax error or contains any syntax errors'. ast-grep's core-concepts page says 'Tree-sitter also handles syntax errors gracefully'. In ast-grep, 'kind: ERROR' matches parse-error nodes (doc comment in kind.rs: 'we can use kind: ERROR in YAML to find invalid syntax'; ERROR is builtin symbol 65535). Implications, as inference: a parse never fails outright. Patterns still match in the intact parts of the file. Inside or next to a broken region the structure may be mis-grouped, so a probe can read a wrong absent, or even a spurious present if the recovered tree resembles the pattern. Neither the ast-grep docs nor the pages I read say what the CLI does or what its exit code is when a scanned file has parse errors. A safe checker should run a 'kind: ERROR' search over the file (or at least over the anchored span) and return inconclusive if it hits. That catches ERROR nodes but not zero-width MISSING nodes, which keep their normal kinds. The napi SgNode exposes no isMissing or hasError.

- [partly_supported] Elsewhere in the test-selection / change-impact literature, limiting selection to direct dependents (one hop) or a small depth is a recognised way to bound over-selection.
    Yes, but as a precision-versus-safety trade, not a free bound. (1) Beszedes et al., 'The Dynamic Function Coupling Metric and Its Use in Software Evolution' (Univ. of Szeged; dynamic call traces; the year is not shown in the extracted text): a cut-off depth d gives infinite d as safe but imprecise, and direct coupling only (d=1) as precise and small but unsafe. At d=1 the impact set averages 13-15% of the safe set's size, at d=2 about 25-35%. Recall reaches near 100% only around d=5-15. They note regression 'firewalls' typically involve only the closest dependents, so d=1 can substitute there. (2) Google, Memon et al., 'Taming Google-Scale Continuous Testing' (ICSE-SEIP 2017; build-target graph): a MinDist cutoff of 10 edges. Executing only targets within MinDist 10 or 6 saved about 42% or 55% of resources, and breakages and fixes sat mostly at MinDist 5-10. That is evidence a one-hop cut would miss most real failures at that scale. They also report affected-target sets as large as 1.6 million. (3) The regression-test firewall idea (Leung and White) retests changed modules plus direct callers and callees; I saw it only in search-result summaries, not the original paper. I found no study that measures a one-hop caller bound on recall in a small codebase.

## Verifying the critique's two literature numbers: '19–23% of duplicate bug reports are worded differently' and 'keyword search finds 10–18 points fewer of them', plus what Zhang et al. (TOSEM 2023) concluded on BM25-style retrieval vs deep learning
- [partly_supported] '19–23% of duplicate bug reports are worded differently' (the range exists in the literature and measures wording difference)
    Primary source: Jahan and Rahman, 'Towards Understanding the Impacts of Textual Dissimilarity on Duplicate Bug Report Detection' (arXiv 2212.09976, Dec 2022; the arXiv listing says SANER 2023). It appears once, in the Introduction, hedged as 'according to our investigation, 19%-23% of the duplicate bug reports could be textually dissimilar'. There is no method, no citation and no per-dataset breakdown behind it. What the paper operationalises is different: 'textually dissimilar' = a duplicate pair whose n-gram (n=1,2,3) cosine similarity is below the lower quartile (25th percentile) of its own dataset, repeated for uni/bi/trigrams and intersected (Eclipse 662, Firefox 1,455, Mobile 131 pairs). That is a relative cut, 25% by construction, not a measured absolute rate of different wording. Data: 92,854 Bugzilla reports (2017–2022) from Eclipse, Firefox, and a pooled 'Mobile' group. So the range is real and attributable, but it is an unexplained, hedged estimate, and it describes public-tracker duplicates filed by different reporters, not an agent writing its own store entries. Nothing in the paper says the share varies with store size, so the critique's point that wording gaps do not depend on store size is consistent with it.

- [partly_supported] Related, closest primary measurement of 'duplicates share no words': '19.2% of duplicate bug report pairs share no terms' (Chaparro et al. 2016), which may be the origin of the 19% end of the range
    Chaparro, Florez and Marcus, 'On the Vocabulary Agreement in Software Issue Descriptions' (ICSME 2016, pp. 448–452; PDF read in full). Data: 8,572 duplicate bug-report pairs from 159 Apache Jira projects plus 5,058 duplicate Stack Overflow pairs, with code, stack traces and code references stripped and stopwords removed. Table II, share of duplicate bug-report pairs with zero shared terms: 33.6% using titles only, 19.0% using descriptions only, 5.1% using title+description. The paper's 19.2% is the mean of those three field choices. Mean of the Stack Overflow rows (27.2, 6.6, 2.8) = 12.2%. On the realistic full-text case (title+description) only 5.1% of duplicate bug-report pairs share nothing. Duplicates that do share words share about 31% of their vocabulary on average. A 2025 student review (arXiv 2507.14396, which I read) repeats the 19.2%/12.2%/31% figures second-hand and cites Chaparro with 'Year' left blank. I cannot confirm the 19.2% is Jahan's source; the match is numerical only, since Jahan cites nothing. Companion paper (Chaparro et al., SANER 2019, abstract read): a basic Lucene detector failed to put a duplicate in the top 5–30 for 34–50% of reports across 20 projects. That is a retrieval-failure rate, not a wording rate, and it involves no embedding comparison.

    The number comes from the same Jahan and Rahman paper, Section III-A / Table IV. It is the BM25 Recall-rate@100 gap between its 'textually similar' and 'textually dissimilar' duplicate subsets: Eclipse 62.78 vs 52.58 (10.20 points), Firefox 57.58 vs 46.72 (10.86), Mobile 78.00 vs 59.55 (18.45). The text says 'ranges from 10.20% to 18.45%'; the Introduction prints '10.02%' (an internal typo). Caveats read from the tables: (1) only k=100. (2) Table V: Eclipse BM25 gap is not statistically significant (p=0.2865, medium effect); Firefox and Mobile are. (3) The dissimilar set is defined by a lexical n-gram cosine score, so a lexical retriever scoring lower on it is close to circular. (4) The paper says it contrasts each technique across the two sets and does not rank techniques. - [contradicted] The same 10–18 points taken as evidence that keyword retrieval is that much worse than embeddings, so embeddings are needed for the wording gap
    No keyword-vs-embedding comparison appears in the paper. ## What the retrieval literature establishes about lexical (BM25) vs dense retrieval at tiny scale: a check of design §4.4 (line 313) against the critique's point 3
- [partly_supported] BEIR (Thakur et al. 2021) supports 'BM25 is the robust baseline' (design §4.4, line 313).
    Abstract: 18 public datasets, zero-shot; BM25 is 'a robust baseline'; re-ranking and late-interaction models give the best average zero-shot results at high compute cost; dense and sparse retrievers are cheaper but often trail. The fetched Table 2 (averages computed by the fetch summariser, not printed in the paper) puts BM25 at about 0.423 nDCG@10, DPR 0.237, ANCE 0.389, TAS-B 0.415, GenQ 0.410, docT5query 0.434, ColBERT 0.431, BM25+CE 0.476. The dense models are 2020-21 vintage. Dense models beat BM25 on NQ (ANCE 0.446 vs 0.329), DBPedia, ArguAna and Quora (ANCE 0.852 vs 0.789; Quora is duplicate-question retrieval). BM25 trails neural models by roughly 7-18 points in-domain on MS MARCO. The authors attribute BM25's edge largely to out-of-distribution shift and note that many BEIR datasets have a lexical bias in their annotation pools. Adding missing judgments on TREC-COVID raised ANCE from 0.654 to 0.735, above BM25's 0.668. Corpora run from 3,633 to about 15M documents, so BEIR has no tiny-store setting. Newer evidence cuts against the design: the E5 abstract says E5 'is the first model that outperforms the strong BM25 baseline on the BEIR retrieval benchmark' without labelled data. Design E-07 F22 itself lists bge-small-en-v1.5 at MTEB retrieval average 51.68. BEIR supports 'BM25 is a strong zero-shot baseline against 2021 dense models'. It does not support 'at this scale', and it does not cover the modern embedder the design would pin.

- [partly_supported] Sciavolino et al. 2021 shows dense retrievers fail on the entity-like tokens defect text is made of (design §4.4).

(d) Search-result sources on hybrid search are vendor and practitioner blogs, and they say to measure on your own queries.     Fair as a conditioned statement, an overclaim as written. - [partly_supported] Critique: ''Keyword search beats embeddings at this scale' isn't established either; it depends on the kind of query' is a refutation of the design.
    Mostly a restatement, not a refutation. The design's own wording already conditions its claim on 'entity-like tokens', and its lane evidence says dense should be added when misses come from zero vocabulary overlap. The critique is right that the sentence reads as an unconditional claim about scale, and that nothing cited establishes it at about 100 entries. Both sides lack tiny-scale evidence on defect text. The critique is also right that query type decides the winner: dense wins on paraphrase-heavy natural language (BEIR NQ/Quora/ArguAna, CoIR text-to-code) and BM25 wins on rare identifiers (Sciavolino, code-to-code). Five percent of 50 lookups is 2.5 misses, close to the critique's 3. §4.5 (line 317) already defines v_missed_existing, classified by cause (typo, vocabulary, handle gap). The only thing both camps agree resolves the question is measurement on real queries.
