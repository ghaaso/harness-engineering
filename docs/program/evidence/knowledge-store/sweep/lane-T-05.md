# lane:T-05

lane=T-05 tool_status=ok facts=27 candidates=10

## Summary
Prior art agrees on a small core. CBR (Aamodt and Plaza) gives the loop: retrieve, reuse, revise, retain. Retain happens whether the solve succeeded or failed. A case holds problem, solution and outcome, plus annotations on how the solution was derived. ITIL known-error records add symptom, root cause, workaround versus permanent fix, status and affected configuration. Practitioner KEDB templates add version scope, a status line with a review date, symptom-first titles, extend-never-sibling dedup, and retire-by-status-never-delete. Google SRE postmortems add owner, measurable action items, "what went well / poorly / where we got lucky", and metadata for trend analysis. The Learning-from-Incidents community adds that write-ups get reused when they record what was surprising or hard. Cook warns that a single "root cause" is a hindsight artefact, so entries should record validity conditions rather than one cause. Smyth and Keane show that performance-driven deletion destroys coverage in pure CBR. A store backed by a top-tier fallback is closer to their "speed-up learner", which makes deletion safer. At tens to hundreds of entries (their experiments used 50 cases), swamping is not the threat. Staleness and false positives are. Agent-memory work (ReasoningBank, Xiong et al.) shows top-1 retrieval beats more retrieval, and that agents copy near-matching records, wrong or misaligned ones included. No source found describes invalidation by code dependency; that gap has to be designed. No empirical study of KEDB rot was found, only practitioner guidance. Nothing here needs an engine. Egress risk appears only in ReasoningBank (embeddings via a Google API), which is cite-for-design-only.

## Design implications
- (strong) Entry schema: prior art converges on these field groups. Problem (symptom text verbatim, plus a symptom-first title). Context (affected versions, platform, configuration, in and out of scope). Diagnosis (root cause plus the conditions under which it manifests). Solution (workaround and permanent fix kept separate, with cost). Outcome and status. Derivation and justification (how it was found). Failed approaches and why (retained failures, 'what went poorly', 'why surprised'). Provenance (owner, dates, linked evidence). Metadata for trend analysis. This maps to CBR's problem/solution/outcome/justification and to the KEDB and SRE templates. [F3, F4, F14, F15, F17, F19, F20]
- (strong) Entry prerequisite: an entry is created only on an evidenced root cause, and a hunch goes to an unconfirmed state first. The KEDB template and Compaq's NEW-to-ACTIVE flow agree, and Xiong et al. show unvetted stored records get copied. The store needs at least a candidate state and an active state, with only active entries served to cheap agents. [F11, F15, F23]
- (strong) Retire by status, never by deletion: mark entries RESOLVED, SUPERSEDED or INVALIDATED and keep them for history, with retirement and merge recommendations going to a human. The KEDB template says retain-for-history and no bulk deletion. Smyth and Keane show deletion by performance heuristics is irreversible. Retired entries stay searchable so a session on an old branch can still find the fix. [F6, F15]
- (strong) Merge rule: same root cause with different symptom wording extends the existing entry's symptom handles (never create a sibling); similar symptoms with a different root cause create a new entry cross-referenced to its lookalike. This directly supports multiple handle types per entry and distinguishes merging from splitting. [F15]
- (moderate) Serve at most one verified hit (or at most two) per lookup, with the entry's date, status and validity conditions displayed. ReasoningBank's top-k ablation got worse as k rose, and the KEDB template says lookup output must show date and status so freshness can be judged. [F15, F22]
- (moderate) The false-positive failure mode is documented: agents copy retrieved records whose inputs resemble the new task, including wrong or misaligned ones, and a copied error can be re-stored. This supports the owner's precision-first stance, a verification gate before any action, and writing back refuted outcomes so a bad entry stops being served. Evidence is at the search-summary level for the paper itself. [F23]
- (strong) Do not copy time-based review as the invalidation mechanism. Prior art uses review dates and linked-record state changes; no source describes dependency-based invalidation (files, symbols, versions, platform). The 'Affects' version-scope field is the closest prior art and should be the seed for a machine-checkable dependency fingerprint, which has to come from another lane. [F9, F15, F16, F26]
- (moderate) Scale: Smyth and Keane's swamping analysis and competence-preserving deletion address case bases far larger than a few hundred entries, and the fallback of a top-tier session makes deletion a cost question rather than a competence question. Maintenance effort should go to staleness and duplicate control, not to retrieval-cost control or footprint modelling. Watson's field study confirms the two practical maintenance problems are redundancy and obsolescence. [F6, F8, F9, F27]
- (strong) Store failed approaches as first-class data: Aamodt and Plaza retain experience regardless of success, ReasoningBank distils lessons from failures, SRE templates have 'what went poorly' and 'where we got lucky', and Morgue has 'why_surprised'. Negative results are the expensive part to save, and nothing in this lane's sources discourages that. [F2, F17, F19, F22]
- (weak) Do not require lengthy narrative or template-heavy entries. ACL argues write-ups worth keeping are those someone will read and that record what was surprising or difficult, not template completion. Keep the structured part small enough to be written in minutes, with a link to the evidence folder for narrative. [F20]
- (weak) Action items from a postmortem need a single owner, tracking id and measurable end state. For a one-developer program, the analogue is that every entry's follow-up (for example 'add a regression test' or 'update CLAUDE.md') is a tracked item or it is not recorded as an action, because SRE treats action-free postmortems as having no effect. [F17]

## Rejected paths
- Adopt Etsy Morgue as the store — Archived since 2019-12-18, PHP stack, human-meeting oriented, no search or tagging in the README.
- Competence-preserving (footprint) deletion as the retirement policy — Built for large case bases where swamping drives deletion. Here retirement is driven by invalidity, and the entry history is worth keeping at this scale.
- Reuse ReasoningBank as built (embedding-API retrieval, add-only memory) — Sends queries to a Google embedding API (egress), has no pruning, no dedup, and no verification gate; keep only its design lessons (top-1, failure lessons).
- Time-only review dates as the invalidation mechanism — Prior art uses it, but the owner wants event-driven invalidation and no source shows time-based review preventing false positives. Retained as a fallback tripwire at most.
- Single 'root cause' line as the only diagnostic field — Cook and the Howie guide argue single-cause attribution is a hindsight artefact; store root cause plus manifestation conditions plus lucky-escape notes.
- LLM-judge as the retain gate — Xiong et al. note selective addition is limited by evaluator accuracy; the track's reproducing check is a stronger gate. Evidence on the judge is at search-summary level.

- F1 [LOAD-BEARING] (verified, paper; workflow/provenance/data_model) Aamodt and Plaza (1994, AI Communications 7(1), 39-59) define the CBR cycle as four steps: RETRIEVE the most similar case(s); REUSE the information and knowledge in that case to solve the problem; REVISE the proposed solution; RETAIN the parts of this experience likely to be useful for future problem solving.

- F2 (verified, paper; workflow/provenance/data_model) Aamodt and Plaza: in CBR the case base is updated no matter how the problem was solved, and the text discusses learning from the success or failure of the proposed solution (CHEF-style repair of failed solutions). Failed outcomes are retained, not discarded.
    https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~ian/CBR/AICom.pdf (partial local extraction, fragment around the Revise/Retain discussion)
- F3 [LOAD-BEARING] (verified, blog; data_model/provenance) A CBR case is described as a problem, its solution, and typically annotations about how the solution was derived. This is the 'justification' element that must be stored alongside problem and solution.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case-based_reasoning (read 2026-11-02)
- F4 (verified, paper; data_model/retrieval/provenance/limits) A 2025 review of CBR for LLM agents models a case as a tuple (problem features, solution actions, outcome metrics, metadata incl. temporal markers, environmental conditions and provenance). Retention is gated: a new episode is stored only if a utility score (novelty, effectiveness, generalizability) meets a threshold. Retrieval combines dense embeddings with sparse feature indices. It lists noisy/redundant cases and maintaining competence and efficiency as open maintenance needs. Its comparison with CoT/RAG is theoretical, resting mainly on cited work.
    https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06943v2 (Hatalis and Christou, arXiv 2504.06943 v2)
- F5 (verified, paper; limits/ops_cost) Smyth and Keane (IJCAI-95, pp. 377-382) define the swamping problem as the cost of searching large case bases, a form of the utility problem (Minton 1990). They distinguish selective utilization (filters/indexing hide cases from retrieval; cases may be missed) from selective retention (permanently deleting cases under a deletion policy).
    https://folk.idi.ntnu.no/agnar/CBR%20papers/smyth-keane-remembering-95.pdf (read via local stdlib text extraction); https://ijcai.org/proceedings/1995/50
- F6 [LOAD-BEARING] (verified, paper; limits/invalidation) Smyth and Keane: performance-driven deletion (random, Minton-style utility) works for speed-up learners such as Prodigy/EBL and SOAR because they can always solve from first principles, so competence is unaltered. For pure CBR systems with no first-principles solver, deleting critical cases permanently removes the ability to solve whole classes of problems. They report random and utility deletion falling to 86% and 80% of benchmark competence while footprint deletion held 100%.
    https://folk.idi.ntnu.no/agnar/CBR%20papers/smyth-keane-remembering-95.pdf (local text extraction, sections 2.2 and 4.1)
- F7 [LOAD-BEARING] (verified, paper; limits/invalidation) Smyth and Keane classify cases by coverage and reachability into pivotal (only reachable from itself; deleting it loses competence), auxiliary (coverage subsumed by another case; deletion only costs efficiency), spanning (links regions covered by other cases) and support (groups of similar-coverage cases; deleting a whole group is like deleting a pivot). Footprint deletion removes auxiliary first, then support, then spanning, then pivotal.
    https://folk.idi.ntnu.no/agnar/CBR%20papers/smyth-keane-remembering-95.pdf (local text extraction, section 3)
- F8 (verified, paper; limits/ops_cost) Smyth and Keane's experiments used an initial case base of 50 cases, 160 target problems and a swamping limit of 75 cases. This is the same order of magnitude as the owner's expected store (tens to a few hundred entries). The competence model is stated to be unreliable if the case base is not representative of the target-problem distribution.
    https://folk.idi.ntnu.no/agnar/CBR%20papers/smyth-keane-remembering-95.pdf (local text extraction, section 4.1 and the closing discussion)
