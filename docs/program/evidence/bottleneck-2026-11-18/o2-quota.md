# o2 quota: refilling quotas and shares, and routing to cheaper workers

Collected 2026-11-18. All papers were downloaded as PDF, converted with pdftotext and read in the text. Line numbers are lines of the converted text (`txt line N`); the PDFs were not paginated in the text, so page numbers are given only where the paper prints them. Two-column layout garbled some symbols; quotations were checked word by word and symbols that did not survive are written in words. Not collected here: theory of machines of different speeds, and the material already in `concurrency-lit-4-scheduling.md`.

## Gate summary
- P1 Parekh and Gallager 1993: a leaky-bucket-shaped session (sigma, rho) under GPS gets a guaranteed rate and a worst-case delay; the worst case is reached when every session is greedy from time 0 (Theorem 3). quoted, txt 74-93, 617-620.
- P2 Cruz 1991: a stream with at most sigma + rho*(length) work in any interval has a deterministic backlog and delay bound at a work-conserving server; the regulator delay is at most (sigma' - sigma)/rho. quoted, txt 14-24, 169-180, ~960.
- P3 Waldspurger and Weihl 1994: lottery tickets give shares; measured 2:1 gave 2.01:1 over a whole run; 10:1 averaged 13.42:1 over 60 s; 20:1 averaged 19.08:1 over 3 min; overhead within 2.7% of Mach. quoted, txt 298-304, 357-359, 463, 515-516.
- P4 Shreedhar and Varghese 1995: deficit round robin gives O(1) work per packet and near-fair throughput but "no latency bounds" in its basic form; the unfairness is bounded by 2*Max + quantum (a conjecture there). quoted, txt 30-33, 154-160, 962-966.
- P5 Banga, Druschel, Mogul 1999: charging work to the right principal (a container) enforced a 30% and a 10% CPU cap "almost exactly"; an uncapped flood fell to about 0 throughput; with containers 73% of maximum remained at 70,000 SYN/s. quoted, txt 640-660, 696-709.
- P6 Turner, Rao, Rao 2010: a group gets quota units of CPU time per period, then is throttled until the period refreshes; quota is handed out in 10 ms slices to per-CPU caches. quoted, txt 112-116, 189-192, 243-248.
- P7 Abeni and Buttazzo 1998: a server with budget Q per period T; when the budget is exhausted it is recharged and the deadline is pushed by T; schedulable iff Up + Us <= 1. quoted, txt 184-203, 249-252.
- P8 Chen, Zaharia, Zou 2023 (FrugalGPT): cascade with a learned scorer and thresholds; cost to match the best single model: 98.3% saving on HEADLINES, 73.3% on OVERRULING, 59.2% on COQA (best single model: GPT-4, GPT-4, GPT-3). quoted, txt 468-482.
- P9 Ong et al. 2025 (RouteLLM): learned router on preference data; up to 3.66x cost saving at 95% of GPT-4 quality on MT Bench, 1.41x at 92% on MMLU (with in-domain data), 1.49x at 87% on GSM8K; trained only on chat data the routers were near random on MMLU. quoted, txt 426, 472-478, 577-591.
- P10 Ding et al. 2024 (Hybrid LLM): learned scorer of the quality gap; with GPT-3.5 as large and Llama-2 13b as small, 20% of queries to the small model cost under 1% quality, 40% cost under 4% (BART score). quoted, txt 20-21, 377-381, 442-446.
- P11 Aggarwal et al. 2024 (AutoMix): small model checks its own answer, a POMDP decides; "reducing computational cost by over 50% for comparable performance"; designed for context-grounded tasks, not factual QA or commonsense. quoted, txt 24-25, 665-668.
- P12 Yue et al. 2024 (cascades with mixture of thought): agreement of the weak model's sampled answers decides; accuracy 0.929 against 0.931 for GPT-4 with self-consistency at 40% of its cost, six reasoning benchmarks, no trained scorer. quoted, txt 349-366.

## Papers

### P1. Parekh and Gallager, "A Generalized Processor Sharing Approach to Flow Control in Integrated Services Networks: The Single-Node Case", IEEE/ACM Trans. Networking 1(3), June 1993. Read from: https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~jiasi/teaching/cs204_spring19/papers/gpsLeakyBucket93.pdf (a course copy of the journal version). Pages read: 344-357 (all of the text; proofs of Lemmas 5-11 read for structure only).
- Result: "the use of Generalized Processor Sharing (GPS), when combined with Leaky Bucket admission control, allows the network to make a wide range of worst-case performance guarantees on throughput and delay" (txt 12-16). "A Generalized Processor Sharing (GPS) server is work conserving and operates at a fixed rate r" (txt 125). Session i, continuously backlogged, is guaranteed a rate of its weight over the sum of weights times r; "as long as rho_i <= g_i, the session can be guaranteed a throughput of rho_i, independent of the demands of the other sessions" (txt 74-90, symbols reconstructed). The leaky bucket: "Tokens or permits are generated at a fixed rate, rho, and packets can be released into the network only after removing the required number of tokens from the token bucket ... the token bucket contains at most sigma bits worth of tokens" (txt 424-433); the constraint is "A_i(tau, t) <= min{(t - tau)C_i, sigma_i + rho_i(t - tau)}" (txt 460-464). Theorem 3: "D_i* and Q_i* are achieved ... when every session is greedy starting at time zero, the beginning of a system busy period" (txt 617-621).
- Conditions: single server, fixed rate, work conserving; N sessions each bounded by (sigma, rho, C); system empty at time 0 (txt 466-470). Bounds for the multi-node case are left to the sequel.
- Measured effect: no measurement; analytic. Packet version PGPS: "Theorem 1: For all packets p, F_p - F_p(GPS) <= L_max / r" (txt 234-238), i.e. one maximum packet time later than the ideal.
- Needs to know or measure: each session's (sigma, rho), the weights, the server rate, the maximum packet size.
- Failure modes: "We have not been able to show that Theorem 3 holds when C_j < r for some sessions" (txt 941-943); the paper notes Virtual Clock "punishes" a session for earlier idle capacity use while PGPS does not (txt 404-430, summarised). The shaping bound excludes the delay inside the leaky bucket itself: "From this point on, we will not consider the delay in the leaky bucket" (txt 70).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. A refilling allowance (weekly or 5-hour) is a token bucket only if the bucket depth sigma and refill rate rho are known; then the worst case for a given agent is every other agent being greedy from the start of the busy period, which gives a delay bound without knowing their statistics. It needs the resource to be divisible and work conserving; a serial test run that one job holds is not.

### P2. Cruz, "A Calculus for Network Delay, Part I: Network Elements in Isolation", IEEE Trans. Information Theory 37(1), Jan 1991, pp. 114-131. Read from: https://webserver2.tecgraf.puc-rio.br/~vbarata/old/doutorado/MDC/Artigo%20Calculus%20-%20cruz91.pdf (a university course copy). Pages read: abstract, Sections I-II (constraints), Section IV (multiplexer delay bounds, statements), Section V (regulators); proofs skimmed.
- Result: "Instead of using a stochastic model for the entering traffic, we assume that the entering traffic is 'unknown,' but satisfies certain regularity constraints" (txt 14-18 and intro). Definition: R ~ (sigma, rho) iff for all x <= y "R(y) - R(x) <= sigma + rho(y - x)" (txt 169-178). "Given any positive number rho, there exists a (possibly infinite) number sigma_rho, such that if the traffic is fed to a server that works at rate rho while there is work to be done, the size of the backlog will never be larger than sigma_rho" (txt 36-39). Regulator: "if it is known that R_in ~ (sigma', rho) then it follows that d_j <= rho^-1 (sigma' - sigma)+ for all j" (txt ~965).
- Conditions: deterministic, no probability model; fixed routing; error-free links; each element analysed in isolation (network analysis is Part II, not read).
- Measured effect: none; analytic deterministic bounds on delay, backlog (buffer) and the burstiness of the output. Theorem 4.1 gives the FCFS multiplexer delay bound in terms of the input burst sizes and rates (statement at txt 446-450; formula not reproduced because the symbols did not survive).
- Needs to know or measure: sigma and rho of each stream (an upper envelope of demand), link rate.
- Failure modes: the bound is only as good as the envelope; a stream that breaks its (sigma, rho) must be delayed by a regulator, and "the input traffic to a (sigma, rho) regulator cannot have an average rate greater than rho without introducing infinite delays inside the regulator" (txt ~960).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. The quota as (sigma, rho): sigma is what may be spent in a burst, rho the refill rate; an agent whose planned token use fits an envelope can be given a delay bound. An agent that cannot state an envelope (unknown tool-call output sizes) has no bound.

### P3. Waldspurger and Weihl, "Lottery Scheduling: Flexible Proportional-Share Resource Management", OSDI 1994. Read from: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/publications/library/proceedings/osdi/full_papers/waldspurger.pdf. Pages read: all (sections 1-8, appendix skimmed).
- Result: "Lottery scheduling efficiently implements proportional-share resource management -- the resource consumption rates of active computations are proportional to the relative shares that they are allocated" (txt 40-48). "The expected allocation of resources to clients is proportional to the number of tickets that they hold" (txt 88). "The number of lotteries won by a client has a binomial distribution ... p = t/T. After n identical lotteries, the expected number of wins w is E[w] = np, with variance np(1-p)" and "a client's throughput is proportional to its ticket allocation, with accuracy that improves with n" (txt 92-110). "Since any client with a non-zero number of tickets will eventually win a lottery, the conventional problem of starvation does not exist" (txt ~120). Compensation tickets: a client using a fraction f of its quantum "can be granted a compensation ticket that inflates its value by 1/f" (txt 170-175). Currencies insulate groups: "inflation is locally contained within currency B" (txt ~484).
- Conditions: Mach 3.0 on a 25 MHz MIPS DECStation, 100 ms quantum; compute-bound Dhrystone, Monte-Carlo, a client-server text search, MPEG viewers; ticket allocation known to the scheduler.
- Measured effect: 2 tasks, 60 s, 1:1 to 10:1 allocations: "all of the observed ratios are close to their corresponding allocations" except the 10:1 run at 13.42:1; "the observed ratio averaged over a three minute period for a 20:1 allocation was 19.08:1" (txt 298-305). 2:1 allocation over the whole run: 25378 and 12619 iterations/s, "actual ratio of 2.01:1" (txt 357-359). Overhead: with 3 Dhrystone tasks, "2.7% fewer iterations" than unmodified Mach; with eight tasks 0.8% slower; a database server workload ran 1.7% faster (1155.5 s versus 1135.5 s) (txt 463, 473-476, 515-516).
- Needs to know or measure: the ticket count of each client; the quantum; for compensation, the fraction of the quantum actually used.
- Failure modes: "the actual allocated proportions are not guaranteed to match the expected proportions exactly" and "the variance is greater for larger ratios" (txt ~97, 301-302). The paper says accuracy over short windows needs a shorter quantum: "a quantum of 10 ms ... reasonable fairness can be achieved over subsecond time intervals" (txt ~112).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. A share is a rate, not an amount: it divides whatever the scarce resource currently gives, in proportion, and it adapts at once when a client leaves or joins. It does not by itself stop a client from exceeding a cumulative cap that refills in windows; that needs a quota mechanism (P6, P7).

### P4. Shreedhar and Varghese, "Efficient Fair Queuing using Deficit Round Robin", SIGCOMM 1995 (IEEE/ACM Trans. Networking 4(3), 1996). Read from: https://www.cs.bu.edu/fac/matta/Teaching/cs655-papers/DRR.pdf. Pages read: all.
- Result: "Our scheme achieves nearly perfect fairness in terms of throughput, requires only O(1) work to process a packet, and is simple enough to implement in hardware" (txt 30-34). Mechanism: round-robin with a quantum per queue; "if a queue was not able to send a packet in the previous round because its packet size was too large, the remainder from the previous quantum is added to the quantum for the next round. Thus deficits are kept track of; queues that were shortchanged in a round are compensated in the next round" (txt 309-323). Conjecture 6.1: "|sent_i,t - sent'_i,t| < 2 Max + Quantum_i" against bit-by-bit round robin (txt 962-964); the same-quantum difference between two flows is proven as "2 Max + Q" (txt 975-982).
- Conditions: packets of arbitrary size, flows that may or may not be backlogged, quantum at least the maximum packet size for O(1) work.
- Measured effect: simulated bandwidth offered to flows with constant, bimodal and random packet sizes was close to equal (figure at txt 954-958); no timing numbers beyond O(1) work.
- Needs to know or measure: the maximum packet (job) size Max; per-flow quantum; deficit counter per queue.
- Failure modes: "Basic deficit round-robin provides throughput in terms of fairness but provides no latency bounds" (txt 154-160); "a small packet can be delayed by a quantum's worth by every other flow", so with quanta equal to Max the delay-bound ratio against bit-by-bit round robin is Max/Min (txt 1031-1041). The paper adds a latency-critical class served separately (DRR+).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. The deficit counter is the right device when work arrives in indivisible chunks of different size (an agent turn costing an unknown number of tokens): carry the unspent or overspent part into the next round. Short jobs wait behind other agents' quanta, so interactive agents need a separate class.

### P5. Banga, Druschel, Mogul, "Resource Containers: A New Facility for Resource Management in Server Systems", OSDI 1999. Read from: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/publications/library/proceedings/osdi99/full_papers/banga/banga.pdf. Pages read: all.
- Result: "the operating system's notions of protection domain and resource principal coincide in the process abstraction. This coincidence prevents a process that manages large numbers of network connections, for example, from properly allocating system resources among those connections" (abstract, txt 47-53). A resource container is the resource principal, separate from the protection domain, and work (including kernel network processing) is charged to it.
- Conditions: Digital UNIX prototype, HTTP server (thttpd modified) with CGI requests of about 2 s CPU each; hierarchical containers with CPU limits.
- Measured effect: with 4 concurrent CGI requests unmodified, "the Web server itself gets only 40% of the CPU, and the static-request throughput drops to 44% of its maximum" (txt 650-652). With a CGI-parent container limited to 30% (and 10%) of the CPU, "Figure 13 shows that the CPU limits are enforced almost exactly" and static throughput "remains almost constant as the number of concurrent CGI requests increases from 1 to 5" (txt 672-681). SYN flood: unmodified throughput "is effectively zero at about 10,000 SYNs/sec"; with containers "even at 70,000 SYNs/sec., the useful throughput remains at about 73% of maximum" (txt 697-707). Three guest servers: "the total CPU time consumed by each guest server exactly matched its allocation" (txt ~688-690, informal experiment).
- Needs to know or measure: which activity each unit of work belongs to, so cost can be charged to it, including work done on its behalf elsewhere (misaccounting was visible: the server process got "slightly more of the CPU than does each CGI process, because of misaccounting for network processing", txt 655-657).
- Failure modes: the experiment on guest servers was informal (the paper's word); the select() scalability cost remains.
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. A quota per agent is only enforceable if tokens spent by sub-agents, tool calls and retries are charged to the agent that caused them; the principal for the allowance has to be the task, not the process.

### P6. Turner, Rao, Rao, "CPU bandwidth control for CFS", Linux Symposium 2010, pp. 245-254. Read from: https://www.kernel.org/doc/ols/2010/ols2010-pages-245-254.pdf. Pages read: all (the results figures are plots; only the text statements were read).
- Result: the problem it solves is a work-conserving scheduler giving unpredictable shares: "the actual amount of CPU time available to a group is highly variable ... The maximum amount of CPU time available to a group is not predictable" (txt 63-72, two columns). The interface: a period and a quota; "the group as a whole will be limited to cpu.cfs_quota_us units of CPU time within the period of cpu.cfs_period_us" (txt 112-116). Mechanism: "When a CFS group consumes all its runtime and when there are no other CPUs to borrow from, the group is then throttled. At the end of the enforcement interval ... the throttled group becomes eligible to run once again" (txt 149-153). Quota is distributed "in 'slices' to local per-CPU caches", default slice 10 ms (txt 126-130, 240-248). Refresh "is a periodic operation that occurs once per quota period" (txt 189-193).
- Conditions: Linux 2.6.34 patch set, 16-core AMD machine, periods of 100, 250 and 500 ms, a latency soaker and sysbench, quota of 1 to 16 cores' worth.
- Measured effect: "the average deviation from the baseline benchmark is very small ... decreases as the enforcement period increases" (txt 462-466, latency soaker); sysbench elapsed time versus number of CPUs was compared with a cpuset affinity baseline (Figures 9-11, plots only; no numbers read). The paper says wider data is still being collected.
- Needs to know or measure: the period, the quota, the slice size; per-CPU consumption to settle borrowed quota.
- Failure modes: quota left in the per-CPU caches can be used after the period ends, so consumption can exceed the quota briefly: "potential local over-commit"; stronger expiry "guarantees that quota will expire relatively promptly are desired to avoid skews" (txt 333-336, 340-358). Throttling inside the period gives a long latency tail for a group that spends its quota early (txt ~160-163).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. This is the closest classical form of a weekly or 5-hour quota: a hard cap per period, unused quota is lost at the refresh (the paper's quota "expires every cfs_period_us", txt 341), work stops when it is spent. It follows that the best use of a window is to spend it before it expires, and that a bursty agent that spends early is stopped for the rest of the window.

### P7. Abeni and Buttazzo, "Integrating Multimedia Applications in Hard Real-Time Systems", IEEE RTSS 1998. Read from: http://retis.sssup.it/~giorgio/paps/1998/rtss98-cbs.pdf. Pages read: sections 1-3 and the start of the evaluation; the proofs are cited to a report ("See [1]") and were not read.
- Result: "A CBS is characterized by a budget c_s and by an ordered pair (Q_s, T_s), where Q_s is the maximum budget and T_s is the period of the server. The ratio U_s = Q_s/T_s is denoted as the server bandwidth" ... "When c_s = 0, the server budget is recharged to the maximum value Q_s and a new server deadline is generated as d_s,k+1 = d_s,k + T_s" (txt 184-203). Theorem 1: "Given a set of n periodic hard tasks with processor utilization Up and a CBS with processor utilization Us, the whole set is schedulable by EDF if and only if Up + Us <= 1" (txt 249-252).
- Conditions: EDF with hard periodic tasks having known worst-case times and soft tasks served from the budget; the soft task's real demand may exceed its reservation.
- Measured effect: worked example in Figure 1 (server (2,7)); the paper's evaluation compares deadline miss rates in simulation (not read in detail).
- Needs to know or measure: the worst-case demand of the hard tasks and the server (Q_s, T_s).
- Failure modes: soft jobs that need more than Q_s per period are delayed (the deadline is postponed), not refused; the paper says soft tasks "cannot be guaranteed a priori" because only mean values are known (txt 78-82).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. It shows how to keep a hard reserved class (a task with a deadline) safe while a budgeted class shares the rest: the budgeted class gets a deadline pushed back each time its budget is spent instead of being cut off.

### P8. Chen, Zaharia, Zou, "FrugalGPT: How to Use Large Language Models While Reducing Cost and Improving Performance", arXiv 2305.05176 (2023). Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.05176. Pages read: all.
- Result: "FrugalGPT can match the performance of the best individual LLM (e.g. GPT-4) with up to 98% cost reduction or improve the accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% with the same cost" (txt 17-19). Mechanism: "LLM cascade sends a query to a list of LLM APIs sequentially. If one LLM API's response is reliable, then its response is returned ... The key components ... (i) a generation scoring function and (ii) an LLM router" (txt 273-280). "It returns the generation if the score is higher than a threshold, and queries the next service otherwise" (txt 285-286). The scorer is "a simple regression model that learns whether a generation is correct from the query and a generated answer" and thresholds are learned by constrained optimisation under a budget b (txt 288-298). Case study: a DistilBERT regressor is the scorer; the cascade calls GPT-J, then J1-L, then GPT-4; accept GPT-J if score above 0.96, J1-L if above 0.37 (txt 345-352).
- Conditions: 12 commercial APIs from 5 providers, prices from March 2023; datasets HEADLINES (financial news, 10000), OVERRULING (law, 2400), COQA (passage reading, 7982) (txt 335-340, 393-396); a train split learns the cascade, a test split evaluates; costs in dollars as listed by the providers.
- Measured effect: Table 3, cost to reach the same accuracy as the best single model: HEADLINES 98.3% saving (best model GPT-4); OVERRULING 33.1 versus 0.6 in the cost column, 73.3% saving (GPT-4); COQA 59.2% saving (GPT-3), cost 9.7 versus 2.6 on the dataset where the saving is shown at txt 470-476 (the table is partly garbled; the percentages are as printed). "Cost savings ... range from 50% to 98%" (txt 480-481). Case study at one fifth of GPT-4's cost: "FrugalGPT reduces the cost by 80%, while improves the accuracy by 1.5%" (txt 420, 450-451). "For about 6% of the data, GPT-4 makes a mistake but GPT-J (or J1-L or GPT-C) gives the right answer" on HEADLINES (txt 446-447).
- Needs to know or measure: labelled training queries for the scorer and thresholds; the price per model; the budget.
- Failure modes: "FrugalGPT is not perfect, and there remains ample room for cost reduction. For example ... all LLM APIs in the chain give the same answer. However, FrugalGPT is unsure if the first LLMs are correct, resulting in the need to query all LLMs in the chain. Identifying how to avoid such cases remains an open problem" (txt 603-607). Costs are 2023 API prices; savings are in dollars, not tokens (the paper's tables are in dollars; not converted).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. Needs a measure of "is this answer good enough" that is cheaper than the answer. In coding work the test result or a type check may play the part of the scorer; whether it can is not shown by this paper (its tasks were classification and reading questions).

### P9. Ong, Almahairi, Wu, Chiang, Wu, Gonzalez, Kadous, Stoica, "RouteLLM: Learning to Route LLMs with Preference Data", ICLR 2025 (arXiv 2406.18665v4). Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.18665. Pages read: all of the main text, appendix partly.
- Result: "Evaluations on public benchmarks show that our approach can reduce costs by over 2 times without sacrificing response quality" (txt 19-21). Decision rule: route to the weak model if the predicted probability that the strong model wins is below a threshold alpha, else to the strong model (txt 161-168). Routers: similarity-weighted Bradley-Terry ranking, matrix factorisation, a BERT classifier, a causal-LLM classifier, trained on Chatbot Arena preference data, optionally augmented with golden-labelled data (MMLU validation split) or GPT-4-judge labels (txt 278-300, 337-373, 388-392).
- Conditions: strong model gpt-4-1106-preview, weak model Mixtral 8x7B; benchmarks MT Bench (160 questions, LLM judge), MMLU (14,042 questions, 5-shot), GSM8K (8-shot) (txt 393-396, 400). Cost is estimated "$24.7 per million tokens and $0.24 per million tokens" for GPT-4 and Mixtral, and the saving is the inverse of the ratio of GPT-4 calls against a random router (txt 584-591).
- Measured effect: Table 6, cost saving over random routing: MT Bench 3.66x (CPT(50%), at 95% of GPT-4 quality) and 2.49x (CPT(80%)); MMLU 1.41x (92%) and 1.14x; GSM8K 1.49x (87%) and 1.27x (txt 577-581). Table 1: with GPT-4-judge augmentation the matrix-factorisation router needs 13.40% GPT-4 calls for 50% of the quality gap recovered versus 49.03% for random (txt 414-423). On MMLU, "all routers perform poorly at the level of the random router when trained only on Arena dataset" and with golden data need about 20% fewer GPT-4 calls than random (txt 472-476). Same routers, no retraining, routing between Claude 3 Opus and Sonnet or Llama 3.1 70B and 8B: about the same call savings (txt 511-540). Router cost: "$39.26 per million requests" for the similarity ranker down to "$3.19" for the causal-LLM router (Table 7, txt 600-603). Against two commercial routers: same performance "with up to 40% fewer calls routed to GPT-4" (txt 889-890).
- Needs to know or measure: preference data (or a labelled sample from the target task); the strong and weak model; a threshold chosen from the cost-performance curve.
- Failure modes: the authors name them: "real-world applications may have distributions that differ substantially from these benchmarks"; only two-model routing was studied; "performance between different routers trained on the same dataset can vary widely on the same benchmark without a clear explanation" (txt 639-648). A benchmark-dataset similarity score correlates with router performance (txt 548-560).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. A pre-generation router costs little and adds one decision before work starts, so it can be used while the allowance is the bottleneck; but with only chat data it did not carry over to a new task type, so a router for coding work needs its own labelled outcomes.

### P10. Ding, Mallick, Wang, Sim, Mukherjee, Ruhle, Lakshmanan, Awadallah, "Hybrid LLM: Cost-Efficient and Quality-Aware Query Routing", ICLR 2024 (arXiv 2404.14618v1). Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.14618. Pages read: all of the main text.
- Result: "our approach allows us to make up to 40% fewer calls to the large model, with no drop in response quality" (txt 20-21). The router predicts the quality gap H(x) = q(S(x)) - q(L(x)) between a small and a large model's responses, scored by BART score (txt 239-241), and sends the query to the small model if the predicted gap is above a tunable threshold; "the desired quality level can be tuned dynamically at test time" (abstract). Three routers: deterministic, probabilistic and probabilistic with data transformation, all trained with binary cross-entropy (txt 284, 370-371).
- Conditions: MixInstruct dataset, 10k training examples, 5k test (txt 393-400); pairs Llama-2 7b versus 13b (small gap), Llama-2 13b versus GPT-3.5-turbo (medium) and FLAN-t5 800m versus Llama-2 13b (large). Cost advantage is defined as the percentage of queries routed to the small model, not money (txt 216-226).
- Measured effect: Table 1, quality drop against all-at-large: 7b/13b at 20% cost advantage 0.1% (deterministic) and 0.0% (probabilistic transformed), at 40% 0.2% and 0.0%; 13b/GPT-3.5 at 20% 1.0/0.8/0.8% and at 40% 3.5/3.4/2.9%; FLAN-t5/13b at 20% 5.8/5.8/4.7% and at 40% 13.8/13.1/10.3% (txt 377-381). "All routers achieve 20% (40%) cost advantage with <= 1% (<= 4%) quality drop" for the medium gap (txt 453-455, summary of Table 1 in the text). Router latency is small against LLM latency (Section 4.4, txt 504-524).
- Needs to know or measure: both models' responses and a quality score on a training set; the small-large quality distribution.
- Failure modes: "Non-trivial routing is challenging" when the large model dominates; for the large gap only the transformed router worked beyond random, at 10.3% quality drop for 40% cost advantage (txt 462-470). Quality is BART score, a proxy; the paper also tests another metric in 4.5 (not read in detail).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. The gain depends on the gap between the cheap and the dear model on the work at hand: near zero loss when the gap is small, large loss when it is large.

### P11. Aggarwal et al., "AutoMix: Automatically Mixing Language Models", NeurIPS 2024 (arXiv 2310.12963v5). Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12963. Pages read: all of the main text, appendix partly.
- Result: "AutoMix, an approach that strategically routes queries to larger LMs, based on the approximate correctness of outputs from a smaller LM ... a few-shot self-verification mechanism ... given that self-verification can be noisy, it employs a POMDP based router" (txt 15-22). "AutoMix consistently surpasses strong baselines, reducing computational cost by over 50% for comparable performance" (txt 24-25). The verifier is the same small model, sampled k times, estimating "p(correct = 1 | As, C, q)" for context C (txt 224-238). Thresholding variant: return LM1's answer if verifier probability >= t (txt 260-264). The POMDP optimises a reward "R = P - lambda x C" (txt 305).
- Conditions: small models Mistral-7B, Llama2-13B, GPT-3.5, large model GPT-4; five datasets: QASPER, QUALITY, COQA, MUTUAL, DIPLOMAT (context-grounded); F1 for QASPER and COQA, accuracy otherwise; cost ratio of 1:100 between the small and GPT-4, "$0.225 for LLAMA2-13B vs $30 for GPT-4" (txt 426-440, 1200-1201); routing learned from as few as 50 examples (txt 106).
- Measured effect: the metric is IBC, the slope of performance per unit cost against the SLM-LLM line (txt 361-373). Table 1: POMDP router IBC lift over the line, Mistral-7B: DIPLOMAT 156.8, MUTUAL 46.7, COQA 12.4, QASPER 69.3, QUALITY 51.6; Llama2-13B: 58.5, 12.4, 83.1, 8.5, 10.3; FrugalGPT on the same: 16.8, 20.3, -16.7, 7.7, 8.1 (Mistral) (txt 453-476, columns partly garbled in the text, read in order). "Even for a cost ratio as low as 1:10, AutoMix begins to deliver good gains" (txt 517-520).
- Needs to know or measure: a document or context to check the answer against (grounded verification); the cost ratio; a small validation set.
- Failure modes: "AutoMix is designed with a dialogue-related or context-grounded reasoning setup in mind for effective self-verification, which does not include tasks like factual question-answering and commonsense reasoning" (txt 665-668). Self-verification "is not particularly effective for reasoning tasks and is often noisy and ill-calibrated" (txt 90-93, quoted from related work).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. Checking an answer against a given context is cheaper than generating one; a coding agent has such a context (tests, spec), but the paper did not test code.

### P12. Yue, Zhao, Zhang, Du, Yao, "Large Language Model Cascades with Mixture of Thought Representations for Cost-Efficient Reasoning", ICLR 2024 (arXiv 2310.03094v3). Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.03094. Pages read: all of the main text; appendix tables skimmed.
- Result: "our proposed LLM cascades can achieve performance comparable to using solely the stronger LLM but require only 40% of its cost" (txt 26-29). The decision uses the weaker model's answer agreement: "when the weaker LLM samples highly consistent answers ... it reveals a high 'confidence' ... otherwise ... the stronger LLM" (txt 158-162). Vote-based: accept the weaker model's majority answer if its agreement score s >= a threshold (txt 253-256); verification-based: accept if the majority answers from different prompts agree (txt 264-280). "Mixture of thought": samples from both chain-of-thought and program-of-thought prompts, "emulating how experts can provide diverse perspectives" (txt 72-74). No trained scorer.
- Conditions: GPT-3.5-turbo weaker, GPT-4 stronger; six datasets: GSM8k, ASDIV, TabMWP (math), DATE and Navigate (symbolic), CREPE (causal); K = 20 samples for GPT-3.5 and K = 3 for GPT-4, temperature 0.4, 8-shot; cost relative to GPT-4 with chain-of-thought self-consistency (txt 334-347).
- Measured effect: "the four MoT variants achieve comparable task performance (0.929 accuracy) to GPT-4-CoT-SC (0.931) while demanding only 40% of its cost. On CREPE, MoT variants even outperform GPT-4-CoT-SC (0.885 vs. 0.871) at 47% of its cost. ... CoT-2D-Vote achieved 0.924 task accuracy on average but demanded only 57% relative cost" (txt 361-366). At a relative cost of 0.4, CoT-2D-Vote beats CoT-1D-Vote by 1.4% absolute accuracy (txt 418-421). Costs include the weak-model sampling cost (the C_d term of the cost model, txt 118-125).
- Needs to know or measure: only a threshold, tuned on a small sample; no labels for a scorer (DATE and Navigate had no training set, txt 1006).
- Failure modes: with Llama-2 13B as the weaker model "doesn't yield ideal results on GSM8k and CREPE ... most of the questions in GSM8k and CREPE are excessively complex for LLAMA2-13B. Therefore, LLAMA2-13B often fails to answer the questions consistently across multiple samplings. Hence, the choice of a weaker LLM should be contingent on the task's level of difficulty" (txt 586-592). Hints from the weaker model hurt accuracy on GSM8k and CREPE (txt 611-613).
- On one machine with agents: reasoned. Agreement costs several weak-model samples per question, so it saves money (price per token) but spends tokens; under a token allowance as the bottleneck the saving at equal quality in the paper (money, relative cost) is not a saving in tokens. Not tested: code, long-horizon tool use, a weak model that is a smaller model of the same family.
