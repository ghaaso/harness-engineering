# o2 memory: what limits speed-up and scheduling when memory, not the processor, is the limit (one machine)

Two limits are kept apart: (A) memory CAPACITY (working sets that do not fit: paging, thrashing, how many jobs to
admit) and (B) memory BANDWIDTH / shared cache (co-running jobs slow each other). Papers were read as text
(pdftotext) from open copies. "txt line N" refers to the extracted text under the scratchpad folder
`papers/memory/<name>/p.txt`. Extraction of 1968 scans is noisy; quotations were checked against the text.

## Gate summary
- P1 Denning 1968, working set: run a job only if its working set fits in free memory; memory demand m = min(w/M, 1); keep the sum of demands of running jobs at a chosen fraction beta of M. quoted, CACM 11(5) pp. 329, 331.
- P2 Denning 1968, thrashing: with memory just full, adding ONE more job gives "a complete collapse of service" (a cliff, not a slope); keep n of resident jobs under n0 where the working sets sum to at most M. quoted, AFIPS pp. 919, 921.
- P3 Roofline (Williams, Waterman, Patterson): attainable = min(peak compute, peak bandwidth x operational intensity); left of the ridge point a job is memory-bound. Opteron X2: 17.6 GFlops/s, 15 GB/s, ridge at 1.0 flop/byte. quoted, pp. 1-2.
- P4 Sun and Ni 1993: memory-bounded speedup; "If G(N) < N, memory capacity is likely to be the scalable constraint". Analytic only, no measurement, weak fit to independent jobs. quoted, txt line 702.
- P5 Zhuravlev et al. ASPLOS 2010: best against worst pairing of 4 jobs: 20% better average completion time, single jobs up to 50%; solo last-level-cache miss rate predicts it; scheduler within 2% of optimal. quoted, p. 1.
- P6 same paper: for 5 of 6 hard-hit jobs, more than 50% of the slowdown came from memory controller, bus and prefetch, not cache space. quoted, Section 3 (txt lines 479-484).
- P7 Merkel dissertation ch. 5 (the EuroSys 2010 work): 4 copies of a memory-bound job on a Core2 chip run 3.9x slower (SPEC memory-heavy job 3.2x); on an Opteron 2.7x and 2.5x; memory-bound beside compute-bound: no significant slowdown. quoted, txt lines 4129-4135, 4202-4207, 3902-3904.
- P8 Tang et al. ISCA 2011 (read in the dissertation): placement alone moves performance up to 25% (web search) and up to 40% (other jobs); the best placement depends on the co-runner; adaptive placement up to 22% better, within 3% of optimal. quoted, txt lines 2151-2155, 2179-2180.
- P9 Zhuravlev et al. survey 2012: contention-aware scheduling needs a mixed workload; "completely pointless if all the threads are the same"; LLC miss rate is the usable cheap signal. quoted, pp. 4:11 and limitations section (txt lines 586-590, 895-905).
- Gap: the only capacity evidence (P1, P2) is from 1968 paged machines; the mechanism carries over, the numbers do not (reasoned). No strong measured study on a 12-core Windows desktop was found.

## Papers

### P1. Denning, "The working set model for program behavior", CACM 11(5), May 1968, pp. 323-333. Read from: https://denninginstitute.com/pjd/PUBS/WSModel_1968.pdf. Pages read: all.
- Result: "The working set of pages associated with a process, defined to be the collection of its most recently used pages, provides knowledge vital to the dynamic management of paged memories" (p. 323, abstract). Working set W(t, tau): "the collection of information referenced by the process during the process time interval (t - tau, t)" (p. 326). Memory demand m_i = min(w_i / M, 1), M the pages of main memory (eq. 17, p. 331). Memory balance: the demands of processes in the running list sum to a chosen beta (eq. 24, p. 331). "Knowledge of only w(t, tau) with demand paging suffices to manage memory well. Before running a process we insure that there are enough pages of memory free to contain its working set" (p. 329). "system performance is particularly sensitive to overcommitment of memory: when too many working sets occupy main memory, each is displacing another's pages ... This phenomenon, known as 'thrashing'" (p. 332). "It is, therefore, highly desirable first to balance memory, then to balance processor" (p. 332).
- Conditions: one machine, two-level memory, demand paging, many processes. "the operating system must determine on its own the behavior of programs it runs; it cannot count on outside help" (p. 323): neither user nor compiler advice is relied on. Each job's demand is a pair (processor share p, memory share m).
- Measured effect: none (analytic model). It reports page return traffic as a function of tau and the transfer time T (eqs. 10-13, p. 327).
- Needs to know or measure: each running job's working-set size, by sampling page-table use bits (p. 328-329), the processor time a job has used since it last blocked, and the total memory M.
- Failure modes: the four properties of working sets "are properties of typical programs and need not hold in special cases" (p. 326); the rates "are estimates" (p. 327); charging for pages shared between jobs "is still an open question" (p. 330, text noisy).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): the capacity limit is a sum, over running agents, of resident memory (the agent, its build, its test run). Admit by measured resident size against free memory before counting cores. A job waiting for a model reply still holds its memory, so the demand is resident size, not recent processor use.

### P2. Denning, "Thrashing: its causes and prevention", AFIPS Fall Joint Computer Conference 1968, pp. 915-922. Read from: https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~brecht/courses/epfl/Possible-Readings/vm-and-gc/thrashing-denning-afips-1968.pdf (a course copy). Pages read: all.
- Result: "Thrashing inevitably turns a shortage of memory space into a surplus of processor time" (p. 915). Efficiency of a job with missing-page probability m and page transfer time T: e(m) = V / (V + V m T) = 1/(1 + mT) (eqs. 6-7, p. 918). For n identical programs that just fill memory (M = ns), adding the (n+1)-st raises m by about 1/(n+1); with T >> n "The presence of one additional program has caused a complete collapse of service" (p. 919). "The sharp difference between the two cases at first defies intuition, which might lead us to expect a gradual degradation of service" (p. 919). Memory needed to keep p processors busy: Q(p) = p s (1 + mT), "almost directly proportional to the traverse time T" when mT >> 1 (eqs. 16-17, p. 920). "A shortage in one resource type inevitably results in a surplus of another": a memory shortage idles processors, a processor shortage leaves memory idle (p. 921, the text reads "shortage in memory resource ... results in idle processors").
- Prevention: "we must be sure that the number n of programs residing in main memory satisfies n <= n0", which equals the condition that the sum of the working-set sizes of active processes is at most M, "there must be space in memory for every active process's working set" (eq. 19, p. 921). Under global FIFO or LRU, n0 is "very difficult to ascertain" (p. 921).
- Conditions: identical processors, demand paging, T large against a memory cycle (T and n: "T >> n >> 1"), average-value arguments.
- Measured effect: cites Fikes et al.: on an IBM 360/67 a page transfer of 1 ms (bulk core) against 10 ms (drum) raised throughput "by a factor of approximately 10" (p. 920). This is a cited figure, not an own measurement.
- Needs to know or measure: working-set sizes (eq. 19), the page transfer time T (today: the cost of swapping, reloading or restarting a killed job), total memory.
- Failure modes: "if Q(p) = M, an unanticipated working set expansion can trigger thrashing" (p. 920): admission needs headroom (Q(p) = fM, f < 1). The argument "breaks down when it is not true T >> n" (p. 919).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): the memory limit acts as a cliff, so the scheduler must stop admitting memory-heavy jobs before the sum of resident sets reaches total memory, and keep a reserve for growth. Size of T on this PC is a fact to measure (page file speed, cost of an out-of-memory kill).

### P3. Williams, Waterman, Patterson, "Roofline: an insightful visual performance model for floating-point programs and multicore architectures", UCB/EECS-2008-134 (the CACM 2009 paper). Read from: https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2008/EECS-2008-134.pdf. Pages read: all of the main text, appendix skimmed.
- Result: "Attainable GFlops/sec = Min(Peak Floating Point Performance, Peak Memory Bandwidth x Operational Intensity)" (p. 2, txt line 108). Operational intensity is "operations per byte of DRAM traffic" counted after the caches (p. 1). "either it hits the flat part of the roof, which means performance is compute bound, or it hits the slanted part of the roof, which means performance is ultimately memory bound" (p. 2). The ridge point's x-coordinate "is the minimum operational intensity required to achieve maximum performance" (p. 2). The limits "are created once per multicore computer, not once per kernel" (p. 2).
- Conditions: floating-point kernels whose "working sets ... do not fit fully in on-chip caches" (p. 1); four multicore machines; a bound model, not a prediction.
- Measured effect: dual-socket Opteron X2: peak 17.6 GFlops/s, sustained 15 GBytes/s from their benchmark, so a kernel at intensity 2 is compute-bound and one at 1 or below memory-bound (pp. 1-2). Ridge points: Xeon 4.4, Sun T2+ 0.33, Cell 0.65 flops per byte (txt lines 329-334).
- Needs to know or measure: sustainable DRAM bandwidth of the machine by microbenchmark (not the pin figure), peak compute, and each job's operations per DRAM byte (needs DRAM traffic counters).
- Failure modes: only an upper bound; a job can sit far under the roof for other reasons (the paper adds "ceilings" for these).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): it tells which limit binds before scheduling: jobs under the ridge gain nothing from more cores once memory bandwidth is used up. Coding-agent work (compile, test, git, indexing) is not floating-point; intensity would have to be recast as DRAM bytes per unit of useful work. Not tested.

### P4. Sun and Ni, "Scalable problems and memory-bounded speedup", ICASE Report 92-59 / NASA CR-189726 (journal: J. Parallel and Distributed Computing 19, 1993, pp. 27-37). Read from: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930007365/downloads/19930007365.pdf (the preprint of the journal paper). Pages read: all.
- Result: three speedup models: fixed-size (Amdahl), fixed-time (Gustafson), memory-bounded. "The problem size scales up with memory size. The difference is that in fixed-time speedup execution time is the limiting factor and in memory-bounded speedup memory size is the limiting factor" (txt lines 398-401). Work is a function of the memory it needs, W = g(M); with N nodes the largest problem is W* = g(N M) (eq. 11, txt line 416). "If G(N) < N, memory capacity is likely to be the scalable constraint when N is large. If G(N) > N, execution time is likely to be the scalable constraint" (txt lines 702-704), G(N) being the growth of work when memory grows N times. "In the memory-bounded speedup, since in general G(N) > N, the influence of the sequential portion is reduced when the system size increases" (txt lines 603-606).
- Conditions: homogeneous nodes with fixed memory each, total memory growing with N; the problem can be enlarged to fill memory.
- Measured effect: none; analytic with matrix-multiplication examples.
- Needs to know or measure: how work grows with data size for the job (g), memory per node, serial part, communication cost.
- Failure modes: "An optimal choice of the function G(N) is both algorithm and architecture dependent and, in general, is difficult to obtain" (txt lines 699-700).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): weak fit. It is about growing one problem to fill added memory, not about several fixed-size jobs sharing one memory. Only the idea carries over: whichever of time or memory binds depends on how work grows with the memory used.

### P5. Zhuravlev, Blagodurov, Fedorova, "Addressing shared resource contention in multicore processors via scheduling", ASPLOS 2010, pp. 129-142. Read from: https://people.ece.ubc.ca/~sasha/papers/asplos212-zhuravlev.pdf. Pages read: all.
- Result: "The best schedule delivers a 20% better average completion time than the worst one. Performance of individual applications improves by as much as 50%" (p. 1; Figure 1: Intel Xeon X3565 quad core, two cores sharing a last-level cache, four SPEC CPU2006 jobs, three possible pairings). Table 1: mcf slowed 65.63% by milc, milc 24.75% by mcf (pair sum 90.4%); worst schedule average degradation 22%, best 2.3% (Section 2.1). The solo last-level-cache miss rate is "one of the most accurate predictors of the degree to which applications will suffer when co-scheduled" (p. 2). Their Distributed Intensity algorithm spreads high-miss-rate jobs over caches and "performs within 2% of the optimal" (abstract). Online version: improves performance "by up to 13% relative to DEFAULT", where worse "at most 4%"; DEFAULT "in the worst case ... does only 13% worse than DIO" (Section 5.3). "The highest impact of contention-aware scheduling techniques is not in improving performance of a workload as a whole but in improving quality of service or performance isolation for individual applications" (abstract).
- Conditions: real machines, Intel Xeon X3565 (4 cores), two-socket Xeon X5365 (8 cores), and an AMD system; SPEC CPU2006 and PARSEC; workloads of 4, 8 and 10 jobs; one job per core, bound to cores.
- Measured effect: percentages are slowdown against running alone, as above.
- Needs to know or measure: each job's last-level-cache misses per million instructions from hardware counters (the online version reads them live).
- Failure modes: when three of four jobs are memory-intensive, or none, placements are "relatively equivalent" and the default scheduler does as well (Section 5.3).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): the slowdown from sharing depends on placement and is mostly predicted by one cheap number. Agents are process trees, not single threads; counters would have to be read per tree. Not tested.

### P6. Same paper, Section 3 (which resource causes the slowdown). Pages 5-6.
- Result: "for all applications except SPHINX contention for resources other than shared cache is the dominant factor in performance degradation, accounting for more than 50% of the total degradation" (txt lines 479-484; Figure 4, six jobs with degradation of at least 45%). "cache contention is not the dominant cause of performance degradation of threads co-scheduled to the same LLC" (p. 2, wording given in the text as "we determined that cache contention is not the dominant cause"). Some jobs had "a decreased prefetching rate (up to 30%) when sharing an LLC with a memory-intensive co-runner" (txt lines 482-483).
- Conditions: two-socket Intel X5365 with a shared front-side bus and memory controller; shares split by switching prefetch on and off and moving jobs between caches and sockets; the authors say the memory-controller share is "a lower bound" (txt line 443).
- Needs to know or measure: memory traffic (miss rate) rather than cache footprint.
- Failure modes: later machines with on-die memory controllers may split differently; the survey (P9) says that if cache contention becomes more dominant, other heuristics will be needed (txt lines 595-597).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): the "shared cache" and "memory bandwidth" limits mostly move together, so one traffic counter can stand for both. This PC (Ryzen 9 7900X, two core chiplets) was not measured by the paper.

### P7. Merkel, Stoess, Bellosa, "Resource-conscious scheduling for energy efficiency on multicore processors", EuroSys 2010, pp. 153-166. Read from: the first author's dissertation "Task Activity Vectors" (KIT, 2010), chapter 5 and summary, https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000015825/1202458. Pages read: chapter 5 (about 60 pages) and conclusion; not the whole thesis. The paper itself was not opened (see Could not open); this is the author's report of the same work.
- Result: "The experiments in Section 5.3.4 indicate a huge performance penalty for memory-bound tasks running simultaneously ... The [stream] microbenchmark is slowed down by a factor of 3.9, the SPEC benchmark [a memory-heavy one] by a factor of 3.2 when four instances of the respective benchmark are running in parallel" (txt lines 4129-4132; benchmark names are lost in the extraction). "When one benchmark from the first and one from the second group are combined, neither of the benchmarks is slowed down significantly" (txt lines 3902-3904, a memory-bound with a compute-bound one). On the Opteron: "slowed down by a factor of 2.7 compared to a single instance (Core2: factor 3.9) ... a slowdown of factor 2.5 (Core2: factor 3.2)", attributed to "a better performance of the integrated memory controller ... as opposed to the front-side bus" (txt lines 4202-4207). "the slowdown introduced by contention outweighs the reduction of power consumption achievable with frequency scaling by far" (txt lines 4137-4139). Energy-delay product of memory-bound tasks "can be reduced by up to 28% compared to standard Linux scheduling just by avoiding contention" (txt line 5990). With sorted runqueues a workload of two memory-bound copies among eight tasks ran "reduction by 20%" shorter (txt line 5447).
- Conditions: quad-core Intel Core2 (L2 shared by core pairs, shared front-side bus) and quad-core AMD Opteron 2354; microbenchmarks and SPEC CPU2006; Linux scheduler modified.
- Needs to know or measure: a per-task activity vector from performance counters, mainly memory-bus transactions per time and cache use. "our experiments with the recent SPEC CPU 2006 benchmarks ... suggest that contention for memory bandwidth is becoming more important than cache contention" (txt lines 3351-3352).
- Failure modes: if only memory-bound tasks are ready, no pairing avoids contention (their fall-back is lower frequency, txt line 4196); only two shared resources (bandwidth and cache) are modelled (txt line 4247).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): gives the size of the bandwidth effect: about 3x to 4x with four memory-bound copies on a bus machine, about 2.5x to 2.7x with an integrated controller, about none beside compute-bound jobs. The figure for this PC and for 12 cores must be measured; four-core numbers cannot be reused.

### P8. Tang, Mars, Vachharajani, Hundt, Soffa, "The impact of memory subsystem resource sharing on datacenter applications", ISCA 2011, pp. 283-294. Read from: Tang's doctoral dissertation "Mitigating Resource Contention in Warehouse Scale Computers" (Univ. of Virginia, 2012), chapters 3-4, https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~soffa/research/dissertations/Lingjia-dissertation.pdf. Pages read: chapters 3 and 4, parts of 1. The ISCA paper was not opened.
- Result: "we demonstrate a performance swing of up to 25% for web-search, and 40% for other key applications, simply from remapping application threads to cores. The optimal thread-to-core mapping decision is also dynamic. It changes when the application changes, when the number of threads changes, when the architecture changes and when co-located application changes" (chapter 4 intro, txt lines 2151-2155). Single-job effect of cache and bus sharing: "up to 22% for contentAnalyzer, 18% for bigtable and 8% for webSearch" (txt line 1530). Co-runner: contentAnalyzer lost 35% sharing both last-level cache and front-side bus with the image stitcher, 22% sharing only the front-side bus (txt lines 1886-1888). "When using the adaptive approach, we observe a performance improvement of up to 22% over status quo thread-to-core mapping and performance within 3% of optimal mapping on average" (txt lines 2179-2180). "the best thread-to-core mapping for a given application does not only depend on the application's sharing and memory characteristics; it is also impacted dynamically by the characteristics of other applications that are co-running" (txt lines 1359-1363).
- Conditions: five Google datacenter applications (three latency-sensitive, two batch) on dual-socket Intel Clovertown and Westmere machines; threads mapped to cores; co-run with batch jobs.
- Needs to know or measure: the adaptive mapper tries placements and measures the application's own performance online, so it needs a progress signal per job rather than a counter model.
- Failure modes: for some applications sharing a cache helps ("constructive" sharing), so separation is not a universal rule (chapter 3 intro, txt lines 1355-1359).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): if agents report progress (tests finished, tokens produced) a try-and-measure scheme needs no counters. Not tested on this PC.

### P9. Zhuravlev, Saez, Blagodurov, Fedorova, Prieto, "Survey of scheduling techniques for addressing shared resources in multicore processors", ACM Computing Surveys 45(1), Art. 4, Nov 2012. Read from: https://people.ece.ubc.ca/~sasha/papers/zhuravlev_csur11.pdf. Pages read: classification, prediction and limitations sections (about half); the rest searched.
- Result: "contention for memory controllers and interconnects is dominant on existing systems ... a simple and easy-to-obtain LLC miss rate heuristic provides sufficient information to predict contention on existing multicore systems" (p. 4:11, txt lines 586-590). "Schedulers rely on the heterogeneity of workloads. To be truly effective, they require a workload that consists of both memory-intensive as well as compute-intensive threads ... A contention-aware scheduler becomes less effective as the workload becomes more homogeneous, becoming completely pointless if all the threads are the same. Furthermore ... even the best possible thread-to-core mapping may result in high overall contention and performance degradation" (txt lines 895-905).
- Conditions: survey of work using only operating-system scheduling, mainly multiprogrammed benchmark workloads on 2007-2011 chips.
- Measured effect: no new numbers (it collects those of P5-P8).
- Needs to know or measure: last-level-cache miss rate (cheap); stack-distance profile (not available online on Intel and AMD, p. 4:10, txt footnote 5).
- Failure modes: as quoted.
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): a scheduler can only avoid contention, not remove it; if every job is memory-heavy the only lever left is how many run at once, i.e. admission, the same lever as for capacity (P1-P2). That last step is my inference, not stated by the survey.
