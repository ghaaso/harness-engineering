## Summary
- The base rule is old and simple: utilisation of resource k = throughput x service demand at k (U_k = X D_k); the resource with the largest demand D_max saturates first and caps throughput at 1/D_max (QSP ch. 3 and 5). Everything else in this set refines or tests that rule.
- The bound needs only one assumption: demand does not depend on how many other jobs are present (QSP 5 l.102-106). That assumption is exactly what fails for memory pressure, lock waits and a shared serial step, so the law is a first filter, not a verdict.
- Utilisation alone mislead: Ousterhout 2015 found CPU near 100% and disk at most 25% busy, and that removing disk wait cut job time by at most 19% (median), network at most 2%. Time blocked, measured from the task's own thread, is the measure that predicts the gain.
- Monotasks (2017) make the bottleneck trivial to name (largest per-resource ideal time) and let a controller set concurrency per resource: up to 30% better than the best fixed Spark setting, but only after re-architecting work into one-resource units.
- Coz measures the gain of speeding one thing up by slowing everything else down (virtual speedup). It sees serial steps and lock contention that utilisation counters cannot, and shows contention as a downward-sloping profile.
- Roofline: two ceilings and one ratio (ops per DRAM byte) tell compute-bound from memory-bound per kernel. The ratio shifts with problem size, so one job can sit on either side.
- A bottleneck that moves with the job mix is solved only in closed-form for known mixes: Anselmi and Cremonesi give a linear program that lists which stations can saturate, alone or together; it needs per-class demands and fixed-rate or load-dependent stations in a product-form model.
- Gap: no source here gives an online switching rule with a measured gain on a live multiprogrammed machine. Switching is derived offline (bounds, LP) or by redesign (monotasks), not by a feedback controller.

## Papers

### P1. Lazowska, Zahorjan, Graham, Sevcik, "Quantitative System Performance: Computer System Analysis Using Queueing Network Models", Prentice-Hall 1984 (textbook, authors' free online copy), chapters 3 (fundamental laws) and 5 (bounds). Copy read: https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~lazowska/qsp/Images/Chap_03.pdf and .../Chap_05.pdf. Text: qsp3/paper.txt (744 lines), qsp5/paper.txt (1128 lines).
- Signal measured: per-resource utilisation U_k, system completions C, hence service demand D_k = U_k T / C (QUOTED qsp3 l.415-417: "we can easily obtain the utilizations of the various resources, uk, and the system-level completion count, C"; the formula itself is cut off in the scan and is REASONED from U_k = X D_k). Per visit S_k, visits V_k.
- How it is measured: from counters over an observation interval T: busy time per device, completions at system level and at device level.
- Switching or control rule: none. The result is a bound: the centre with the largest D_k is the bottleneck.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (qsp3 l.79-80): "the utilization of a resource is equal to the product of the throughput of that resource and the average service requirement at that resource." QUOTED (qsp3 l.332-333): "The Forced Flow Law: Xk = VkX" (written in the scan as "x, = v,x"). QUOTED (qsp5 l.131-136): "when any of the centers becomes saturated (i.e., has utilization one), the entire system becomes saturated ... the center that saturates at the lowest arrival rate is the bottleneck center - the center with the largest service demand." Closed-system bounds, QUOTED (qsp5 l.252-256, layout garbled): throughput lies between N/(ND+Z) and min(1/D_max, N/(D+Z)); the two lines cross at N* (qsp5 l.187, 263).
- Conditions of the experiment: analytic; worked example K=3 centres, D = 5, 4, 3 s, Z = 15 s (qsp5 l.489-493).
- What must be true for it to apply: QUOTED (qsp5 l.102-106): "The validity of the bounds depends on only a single assumption: that the service demand of a customer at a center does not depend on how many other customers currently are in the system, or at which service centers they are located."
- Failure modes: demand that grows with load (paging when memory is short, lock convoys, cache thrash) breaks the single assumption; the bound then is optimistic. Bottleneck removal helps only up to the next centre: QUOTED (qsp5 l.480-487) "the secondary bottleneck". QUOTED (qsp5 l.504-506): "the futility of improving any center but the bottleneck with respect to enhancing performance at heavy load."
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. Per-process demand on CPU, memory bandwidth and the serial step can be read from counters, and the resource with the largest summed demand per job caps throughput at 1/D_max; at fewer workers than N* the system is below its cap and adding workers still helps.

### P2. Ousterhout, Rasti, Ratnasamy, Shenker, Chun, "Making Sense of Performance in Data Analytics Frameworks", NSDI 2015. Copy read: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi15/nsdi15-paper-ousterhout.pdf. Text: nsdi15/p.txt (about 700 lines).
- Signal measured: per-task time blocked on network or disk, seen from the task's compute thread; plus machine-level CPU, disk and network utilisation to cross-check.
- How it is measured: white-box logging added to Spark; blocked time per task; a simulator replays the job's task schedule with that time removed.
- Switching or control rule: none (analysis, not control).
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.16): "(i) CPU (and not I/O) is often the bottleneck, (ii) improving network performance can improve job completion time by a median of at most 2%". QUOTED (l.29): "Optimizing or eliminating disk accesses can only reduce job completion time by a median of at most 19%." QUOTED (l.389): "CPU utilization is typically close to 100% whereas median disk utilization is at most 25%." Simulator accuracy, QUOTED (l.143): "at the median, the time predicted by our simulation is within 4% of the runtime of the original job"; 95th percentile error "at most 7% for the benchmark workloads and 27% for the production workload".
- Conditions of the experiment: QUOTED (l.145): "Amazon EC2 m2.4xlarge instances, which each have 68.4GB of memory, two disks, and eight cores"; Spark 1.2.1; 20 machines (160 cores) for two SQL benchmarks, 9 machines (72 cores) for the production workload (l.41); a 3x larger cluster gave comparable results (l.625).
- What must be true for it to apply: instrumentation inside the framework; QUOTED (l.49): "we cannot claim that our results are broadly representative."
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.256): "we cannot use blocked time analysis to understand the importance of compute time, because we cannot measure when task I/O is blocked waiting for computation." Disk and network time are mixed when remote writes are used, so the figures are upper bounds (l.183-184). QUOTED (l.141, note 3): removing one blocked time may "result in more time blocked on disk". The authors' disk:core ratio (1:4) is under-provisioned, so I/O looks more important than in the wild (l.253).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. Per-resource utilisation says who is busy; time blocked says who is limiting. For a CPU-bound batch of agents, a serial step shows up as blocked time with low utilisation everywhere, which a utilisation-only rule would miss. The method cannot attribute CPU time to a cause, so CPU must be judged from utilisation.

### P3. Ousterhout, Canel, Ratnasamy, Shenker, "Monotasks: Architecting for Performance Clarity in Data Analytics Frameworks", SOSP 2017. Copy read: http://kayousterhout.org/publications/sosp17-final183.pdf (author's site). Text: mono/p.txt (680 lines).
- Signal measured: total runtime of the monotasks of each type (compute, disk, network) per stage.
- How it is measured: each monotask uses exactly one resource and reports its own run time (l.120, 352).
- Switching or control rule: QUOTED (l.370): "the ideal stage completion time ... is simply the maximum of any resource's completion time ... In essence, the ideal stage completion time is the time spent on the bottleneck resource." Job time is the sum over stages, "since stages may have different bottlenecks" (l.369), so the bottleneck is allowed to change per stage. Concurrency rule, QUOTED (l.198-200): schedule enough multitasks that every resource can have its maximum number of concurrent monotasks "plus one additional monotask"; per-resource schedulers queue the rest (l.122).
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.54): "MonoSpark provides job completion times within 9% of Spark for typical scenarios". QUOTED (l.57): "For most 'what-if' questions, the monotasks model provides estimates within 28% of the actual job completion time". QUOTED (l.59): automatic concurrency "improves job completion time by 30% relative to configuring a job's resource use." QUOTED (l.349): bottleneck CPU "average utilization is over 92% for all machines" in one map stage.
- Conditions of the experiment: 8-vCPU EC2 instances with two disks (HDD m2.4xlarge or SSD i2.2xlarge, l.219); 20 workers (160 cores) for several runs (l.324); three workloads (sort, big data benchmark, ML).
- What must be true for it to apply: QUOTED (l.626) jobs "with a small number of multitasks relative to the available resources" lose pipelining; tasks must fit in memory (l.627, "jobs with large tasks"); work can be cut into single-resource pieces.
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.629) head-of-line blocking, "A monotask that reads a large amount of data from disk may block other tasks reading from that disk"; more memory use (l.630); the model "ignores many practicalities" such as a skewed disk, and ramp-up with one resource busy (l.390). Schedulers run at application level, so control is not perfect (l.215). Workloads were chosen "that do not have one resource with dramatically higher utilization than all other resources" (l.251), so the 9% figure is not shown for strongly one-sided jobs.
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. This is the closest published form of "detect, then switch": per-resource queues with a cap of one worker per core for CPU, one for a disk, and the bottleneck read off as max of ideal times. It needs the work to be split by resource, which a coding agent's session is not; it fits the test suite or merge step as its own single-resource queue.

### P4. Curtsinger and Berger, "COZ: Finding Code that Counts with Causal Profiling", SOSP 2015 (best paper). Copy read: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.03676 (arXiv version of the paper). Text: coz/p.txt (about 900 lines).
- Signal measured: change in the rate of visits to a user-placed progress point (throughput) or the pair of start and end points (latency), when one line is virtually sped up.
- How it is measured: QUOTED (l.105): "a causal profiler uses the novel technique of virtual speedups to mimic the effect of optimizing a specific line of code" by pausing all other threads for a fraction of the time that line runs; sampling of the instruction pointer implements it (l.195). Latency from Little's law (l.303-305).
- Switching or control rule: none; a profile per line, slope of the speedup curve ranks lines (l.191). Experiments choose line and speedup at random (l.120).
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.15): "Guided by COZ, we improve the performance of Memcached by 9%, SQLite by 25%, and ..." QUOTED (l.110): overhead "mean: 17%, min: 0.1%, max: 65%". Prediction check, QUOTED (l.799): "COZ predicted that this improvement would result in a 21.4% program speedup, which is nearly the same as the 21.2% we observe" (ferret).
- Conditions of the experiment: QUOTED (l.558): "a 64 core, four socket AMD Opteron machine with 60GB of memory, running Linux 3.14"; GCC 4.9.1 -O3; Memcached, SQLite and PARSEC.
- What must be true for it to apply: the program has a clear progress point; Linux x86-64; unmodified binaries with debug info (l.195-196); enough progress-point visits per experiment (default five, l.122). QUOTED (l.305): Little's law needs a stable system.
- Failure modes: lock waits need special handling of blocking calls (l.392-393, tables 1 and 2); QUOTED (l.734) contention shows as "a characteristic downward-sloping causal profile plot", meaning speeding that line would slow the program. Overhead is mostly the inserted delays (l.840). It identifies lines in one process; it does not pick a resource.
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. Coz is how one finds out that a serial step (a lock, a barrier, a single-threaded merge) is the limit when CPU shows idle. It is an offline diagnosis per program, too slow (it needs many runs) to use as a live signal.

### P5. Williams, Waterman, Patterson, "Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Multicore Architectures", CACM 52(4) 2009. Copy read: https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2008/Archive/EECS-2008-134.pdf (Berkeley tech report EECS-2008-134, earlier version of the same work; the CACM page text was not read). Text: roof/p.txt (about 300 lines).
- Signal measured: operational intensity (floating-point operations per byte of DRAM traffic after the cache), peak compute rate, peak memory bandwidth.
- How it is measured: peak numbers from the manual or microbenchmarks; traffic between caches and DRAM measured, not processor-to-cache (l.33-35).
- Switching or control rule: QUOTED (l.44): "Attainable GFlops/sec = Min(Peak Floating Point Performance, Peak Memory Bandwidth x Operational Intensity)". QUOTED (l.47): "either it hits the flat part of the roof, which means performance is compute bound, or it hits the slanted part of the roof, which means performance is ultimately memory bound." Ceilings below the roof rank which optimisation to try first (l.56, 73-74).
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.186): "All 16 cases validate this bound and bottleneck model since the upper and lower ceilings of Roofline bound performance". Ridge point moved from 1.0 (Opteron X2) to 4.4 (Opteron X4) (l.50).
- Conditions of the experiment: four multicore machines (Opteron X2 and X4, Xeon, Sun T2+, Cell) and four kernels (SpMV, LBMHD, stencil, 3-D FFT), 16 pairs.
- What must be true for it to apply: kernel-style floating-point code with a stable operational intensity; peak numbers known. Operational intensity moves with problem size and cache behaviour (l.81-84).
- Failure modes: a kernel's intensity is not a constant, so its side of the ridge point changes with input size (l.81, FFT l.177). Only two resources (compute, DRAM). Needs a hardware counter for DRAM traffic.
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. The memory side of the 24-thread machine can be tested with one ratio (work per DRAM byte per process) against the memory-bandwidth ceiling; when the sum of demand reaches the ceiling, more workers cannot help even with idle cores.

### P6. Anselmi and Cremonesi, "A Unified Framework for the Bottleneck Analysis of Multiclass Queueing Networks", Performance Evaluation 2010 (preprint DOI 10.1016/j.peva.2009.09.002). Copy read: https://polaris.imag.fr/jonatha.anselmi/anselmi09bottleneck.pdf (author's preprint). Text: anselmi/p.txt (about 1350 lines). This builds on Balbo and Serazzi 1997 and Casale and Serazzi 2004 (not opened, see below).
- Signal measured: per-class service demand D_ir = v_ir / mu_ir at every station, and the population mix (fraction of each class).
- How it is measured: the demands are model inputs, taken from counters as in P1 (visits and service time per class per station).
- Switching or control rule: QUOTED (l.127): "If different classes have distinct natural bottlenecks, it has been shown that the bottlenecks can migrate across different stations depending on the population mix". A station can saturate for some mix if and only if a set of linear constraints on the mix is feasible (l.212-218); a simplex program per station finds the set of stations that can ever bottleneck; Algorithm 3 tests whether given stations can saturate together (l.413-415); global switching points where bottleneck changes come from a linear system (l.696-706).
- Measured effect: SUMMARY (l.6 abstract): the analysis "reduces the computational requirements of existing techniques" and handles load-dependent stations. No gain is measured on a live system; it is analytic and numeric.
- Conditions of the experiment: closed multiclass BCMP networks (product form), population N growing to infinity at a fixed mix (l.18, l.96-108); numerical examples only.
- What must be true for it to apply: product-form assumptions, fixed per-class demands, large populations. QUOTED (l.120): a bottleneck is "a station which saturates its processing capacity as N -> infinity".
- Failure modes: asymptotic, not for small populations; a station can be non-dominated yet "masked-off" (l.139) and never saturate; simplex is "non-polynomial with respect to the input size but very efficient for practical purposes" (l.231); no memory or serial-step model unless these are modelled as stations.
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. If agents fall into a few classes (test-heavy, edit-heavy, build-heavy) with measured per-class demands on CPU, memory and the serial step, this tells which mixes saturate which resource, and where the bottleneck moves as the mix changes. It would be a table made in advance, not a live detector.

### Practice sources (not peer reviewed)
- Gregg, "The USE Method", brendangregg.com/usemethod.html (read in full, use/t.txt; the ACM Queue article is a related piece, not opened). QUOTED (l.132): "For every resource, check utilization, saturation, and errors." Definitions, QUOTED (l.140-142): utilisation is "the average time that the resource was busy servicing work"; saturation is "the degree to which the resource has extra work which it can't service, often queued". Caveat, QUOTED (l.161): "A burst of high utilization can cause saturation and performance issues, even though utilization is low when averaged over a long interval." Software resources and limits may be included (l.145). Rule, not a controller: it makes a checklist for a human.
- Linux kernel docs, "PSI - Pressure Stall Information" (Weiner, 2018), raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/master/Documentation/accounting/psi.rst (read in full, psi/raw.html). QUOTED (l.45-46): the "some" line is "the share of time in which at least some tasks are stalled on a given resource"; the "full" line (l.48-49) is the "share of time in which all non-idle tasks are stalled on a given resource simultaneously". Files /proc/pressure/cpu, memory, io, windows of 10, 60 and 300 seconds, plus a cumulative `total` in microseconds (l.38, 60-65); triggers via poll() (l.70-75). QUOTED (l.26-29): systems "can be managed dynamically using techniques such as load shedding, migrating jobs ... or strategically pausing or killing low priority or restartable batch jobs." This is a counter in the blocked-time family (P2): stall time per resource, not busy time. System-level CPU "full" is undefined (l.57). Linux-only; it says nothing about a serial step inside user space unless the step shows up as a stall on one of the three resources.

## Across the papers (agreements, disagreements, gaps)
- Agreement: the bottleneck is where the largest demand (or longest time) falls; improving anything else changes little at heavy load (P1, P3, P5 each state this in their own terms: max of demands, max of ideal times, min of two roofs).
- Agreement: utilisation is necessary but not sufficient. P2 shows low disk utilisation and small gain from removing disk wait; P7 (USE) warns that a long-window average hides saturation; PSI exports stall time because busy time misses it.
- Disagreement or tension: P1 and P6 treat demand as fixed per job; P2 and P3 show a task's bottleneck changes at fine time scale (P3 l.82, Figure 2: "the bottleneck changes between CPU and disk" within 30 seconds), so the signal has to be averaged over a window long enough to hold a stage but short enough to follow the mix. No paper gives that window.
- Shifting bottlenecks: handled offline by stage (P3) or by mix sectors (P6), not by a live detector.
- Two at once: P6 gives the condition for several stations to saturate together (a global saturation sector); P1 treats the second-highest demand as the next limit; P5 draws one roof per resource and takes the min. None gives a control rule for "two at once" beyond naming both.
- Gaps (REASONED): serial steps (a test suite one agent runs at a time) are modelled in none of the papers except as a station or as lock contention found by Coz; memory capacity as distinct from memory bandwidth is not handled by the laws above; no paper measures a switching rule on a live machine with a changing mix.
