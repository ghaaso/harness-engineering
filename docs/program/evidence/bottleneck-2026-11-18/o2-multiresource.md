# o2 multiresource: fairness and packing when several resources are scarce at once

Reading notes. All papers were downloaded as PDF (each starts with `%PDF-`) and turned into text with `pdftotext`
(`raw.txt` in `<scratchpad>/papers/multiresource/<name>/`). "raw line N" means line N of that text file. The PDFs have no
page numbers in the text, so locations are section names plus raw lines. I read the model, algorithm, evaluation and
limitation sections of each paper; I did not read every proof. Extraction problem: in Tetris (SIGCOMM 2014) and Graphene
(OSDI 2016) the digits and "%" signs of the body text are missing from the extracted text (I tried 4 copies of each; same
result). Their headline numbers are therefore NOT `quoted`; where given they are `summary` (from a search-result abstract
summary, not the paper's own text). DRF, DRFQ, DRFH, Parkes et al. and Carbyne have readable numbers.

## Gate summary
- P1 DRF (NSDI 2011): equalise each user's largest share of any one resource; needs a per-task demand vector, no durations. Mesos, 48 nodes: slot-based sharing gave worse throughput and response time than DRF for every slot count 3-6 (quoted, Sec 7.2, raw line ~839; the bar values in Figs 9-12 are not cleanly readable). Facebook trace sim: large jobs up to 66% faster, small jobs -3% (quoted, Sec 7.3).
- P2 DRF dynamics (same paper): when task shapes change, DRF re-targets the new dominant resource with no reconfiguration: job shares followed CPU, then memory (quoted, Sec 7.1). The allocation changes with the scarce resource by construction.
- P3 Tetris (SIGCOMM 2014): pick the task with the highest dot product of task demand and machine free resources, plus a remaining-work term; needs demand vectors (from history or first tasks) and rough remaining work. Gains over fair/DRF: numbers unreadable in the text and not verified (no mark). Fairness-vs-speed knob f.
- P4 Graphene (OSDI 2016): with DAG dependencies, packing without the DAG is Omega(d) times optimal (d = number of resources) (quoted, lemma, raw line 518); place long and hard-to-pack tasks first. `summary`: median job time -25%, throughput +30% on 200 servers.
- P5 Carbyne (OSDI 2016): each job gives away allocation that does not delay its own finish; leftover goes to short jobs / best packing. Quoted: 1.26x better efficiency and 1.59x lower average job time than DRF; gain grows with load until saturation (1.57x at 1x, 1.76-1.91x at 2x load, none extra at 6x).
- P6 DRFQ (SIGCOMM 2012): fair sharing in TIME (one packet at a time per resource). "Bottleneck fairness" (share whichever resource is currently the bottleneck) can be gamed and oscillates; DRFQ gave flow 1 160 Mbps vs 75 Mbps under bottleneck fairness (quoted, Table 7).
- P7 DRFH (IEEE/ACM ToN / TPDS-style journal, Wang, Liang, Li): DRF on heterogeneous servers; best-fit placement. Google trace, 2,000 servers: best slot scheduler reached 43.9% CPU / 28.0% memory utilisation (quoted, Table 2); DRFH higher (figure only).
- P8 Parkes, Procaccia, Shah (EC 2012): DRF is at best 1/m of optimal total welfare, unavoidable for any mechanism with sharing incentive or envy-freeness (quoted); with indivisible tasks no mechanism has Pareto efficiency + sharing incentive + strategy-proofness (quoted Thm 5.1).
- Cross-cutting (reasoned): every method assumes demands are stated or estimable and fixed in proportion per task; none addresses a refilling token quota or a serial one-at-a-time check as a first-class resource except DRFQ (time-shared, one packet at a time).

## Papers

### P1. Ghodsi, Zaharia, Hindman, Konwinski, Shenker, Stoica, "Dominant Resource Fairness: Fair Allocation of Multiple Resource Types", NSDI 2011. Read from: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/nsdi11/tech/full_papers/Ghodsi.pdf (copy: 11 pages). Pages read: abstract, Secs 1-7, 9 (proof appendix skimmed).
- Result: "In a nutshell, DRF seeks to maximize the minimum dominant share across all users." (Sec 1, raw ~line 15). Four properties: sharing incentive, strategy-proofness, Pareto efficiency, envy-freeness. Example: 9 CPU, 18 GB; A tasks <1 CPU, 4 GB>, B tasks <3 CPU, 1 GB>; DRF gives A 3 tasks (3 CPU, 12 GB), B 2 tasks (6 CPU, 2 GB) (Sec 4.1, raw line 217). Algorithm is progressive: "DRF picks the user with the smallest dominant share to run her task" (raw line 351); cost O(log n) per decision with a heap.
- Rivals shown to fail: Asset Fairness "can result in one user getting less than 1/n of all resources" (Sec 5.1/6.1) ; CEEI "is not strategy-proof" (raw line 491).
- Conditions: n users, m resources, each user's task has a demand vector ("each task is characterized by a demand vector", raw line 214); divisible resources in theory; Mesos with discrete tasks in practice; tasks may finish and release, DRF then re-picks.
- Measured effect: Mesos, 48 EC2 nodes of 8 cores / 7 GB (6 GB allocatable), 4 small-job users <1 CPU, 0.5 GB> and 4 large-job users <2 CPU, 2 GB>, 10 minutes: "with slot-based scheduling, both the throughput and job response times are worse than with DRF, regardless of the number of slots" (Sec 7.2, raw ~line 839). Bar values of Figs 9-12 are not cleanly readable, so only the sentence above is relied on. CPU-only fair sharing: "much fewer large jobs executed, because memory is overcommitted on some machines" (raw ~line 839). Facebook trace, 400 simulated nodes: large jobs' completion "reduce by as much as 66%"; small jobs -3% "because small jobs typically consist of a single execution phase" (Sec 7.3, raw line 913).
- Needs to know or measure: each task's demand vector per resource (stated by the user/framework); no durations needed. Total capacity per resource.
- Failure modes: fragmentation with discrete tasks and many machines (Sec 6.2; raw line 820 "not exactly equal ... due to resource fragmentation"); the paper itself lists as open "to minimize resource fragmentation without compromising fairness. This problem is similar to bin-packing" and placement constraints (Sec 9.1, raw ~line 990). Fairness only; no objective for makespan or completion time.
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): applies when several agents run at once and each agent's resource use is a stable vector (cores, GB, tokens per minute) and the question is who gets what share. When ONE resource is the same bottleneck for all agents, DRF reduces to ordinary max-min fairness on that resource ("Bottleneck fairness" property, raw line 186). It needs per-agent demand vectors, which for an agent are not given and change by phase (reasoned).

### P2. Grandl, Ananthanarayanan, Kandula, Rao, Akella, "Multi-Resource Packing for Cluster Schedulers", SIGCOMM 2014. Read from: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tetris_sigcomm14.pdf (12 pages). Pages read: all text; digits missing from extraction (see top).
- Result: "the packing heuristic projects tr and mr into a euclidean space and picks the task, machine pair with the highest dot product value" (Sec 1, raw line 20). Only satisfiable tasks are considered, "so overallocation is impossible" (Sec 3.2, raw line 533). To cut average job time, combine alignment score a with a remaining-work score p: pick the task with the highest a + e*p (raw line 548), with "SRTF" ordering (raw line 540). "Tetris uses knowledge of the job's DAG to preferentially schedule the last few tasks before a barrier" (Sec 3.5, raw line 557). A fairness knob f gives "a smooth trade-off between fairness and performance" (raw line 32).
- Why DRF loses (worked example, Sec 2.1, raw lines 43-47): three jobs with map then reduce phases; DRF equalises dominant shares, "leaves GB of cluster memory idle" in the map phase; a packing schedule that overlaps A's reducers (network) with B's mappers (memory) finishes every job earlier than DRF. Also: "if instead the cluster consists of three machines ... DRF all of the jobs will finish at t" later (raw line 127): treating the cluster as one bag of resources "hides the impact of fragmentation".
- Conditions: data-parallel jobs (map/reduce DAGs with barriers), four resources (CPU, memory, disk, network last hop), YARN prototype, trace-driven simulation of production traces. Baselines: Hadoop Fair/Capacity scheduler (slots), DRF (raw line 589).
- Measured effect: abstract says "gains of over [digits missing] in makespan and job completion time" (raw line 22); no readable figure. A loose upper bound "(not a true upper-bound ...)": packing could cut makespan and job time by large factors versus slots and DRF, but "the gains are lopsided -- [digits missing] of the jobs slow down" (raw line 321). Do not use any number from this paper without opening the figure.
- Needs to know or measure: per-task demand vector for CPU, memory, disk, network; estimated from "task statistics measured in prior runs of the job" and "the first few tasks" of the phase; "when none of the above methods are available ... Tetris over-estimates their task demands. Over-estimation is better than under-estimation which needlessly slows down tasks" (raw line 570). Also a resource tracker for actual machine utilisation; enforces allocations with token buckets for disk and network (raw line 572). Remaining work needs task counts and durations.
- Failure modes: finding the optimal packing is "(APX-hard)" (raw line 225); a strict shortest-remaining order "can slow down everyone" because those tasks may pack badly (raw line 24); starvation of large tasks was a worry (raw line 561); fairness is traded away by the knob; ignores dependencies beyond the barrier heuristic (Graphene, P3, proves this costs a factor of d).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): the dot-product rule needs a free-resource vector and a demand vector per candidate task; with one machine there is no fragmentation across machines, so the main gain left is complementarity (a CPU-heavy job with a memory-light one) and over-allocation avoidance. Needs measured demand per job; over-estimate when unknown.

### P3. Grandl, Kandula, Rao, Akella, Kulkarni, "Graphene: Packing and Dependency-aware Scheduling for Data-Parallel Clusters", OSDI 2016. Read from: https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/graphene_osdi16_cr.pdf. Pages read: abstract, Secs 1-6 text; digits missing from extraction.
- Result: "Relaxing either of these challenges, i.e. scheduling a DAG of homogeneous tasks or an independent set of heterogeneous tasks, leads to NP-hard problems" (abstract, raw line ~20). Insight: schedule "troublesome" tasks (long-running or hard to pack) first, then the rest "without violating dependencies" (abstract, raw lines 28-34). Lemma (Dependencies): "Any scheduling algorithm ... that does not account for the DAG structure (e.g., only schedules currently runnable tasks) is [Omega](d) times OPT where d is the number of resources" (raw line 518). Lemma (Resource Variation): "critical path scheduling can be [Omega](n) times OPT where n is the number of tasks" (raw line 523). So both packing and dependencies must be considered; the paper's own conjecture is that this holds for any scheduler that ignores one of them (raw line 530).
- Conditions: DAG jobs, median DAG "depth of five and thousands of tasks", task durations "sub-second to hundreds of seconds" (raw line 55); 200-server cluster plus production traces; baselines Tez on YARN capacity scheduler, critical-path order, Tetris (raw lines 1447-1451).
- Measured effect: `summary` (search-result summary of the abstract page): median job completion time improved by 25% and throughput by 30% on 200 servers; half of DAGs 19%-31% faster across workloads. Not quoted (digits unreadable). Quoted: "Both [Graphene] and the alternatives offer more gains at higher loads. This is because the need for careful scheduling and packing increases when resources are scarce." (raw line ~2223). "makespan improves relative to Tez and over relative to alternatives" (raw line ~1880, digits lost).
- Needs to know or measure: the whole DAG with per-stage task durations and resource usage, computed offline per job (runs "in tens of seconds on the DAGs used", raw line 1205); estimates from recurring jobs ("prior work shows that the task profiles of these jobs can be estimated from history", raw line 625); "[Graphene] is rather robust to estimation error because relatively small differences in tasks' duration and usages do not change the schedule" (raw line 1293); under-estimates over-allocate, over-estimates waste resources (raw lines 2355-2375).
- Failure modes: needs the DAG known ahead; offline search is costly per job; online part still trades fairness for speed, bounded by an operator-set unfairness threshold (raw lines 1107-1113); a one-stage or tiny job gains little (reasoned from DRF Sec 7.3 and Carbyne below).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): relevant only if the work of an agent can be written as a graph of steps with known rough durations and resource use (e.g., a plan with test, build, review steps) and there is a serial resource: then put the long/hard-to-pack steps first. Needs the dependency graph; without it Graphene's advantage is gone.

### P4. Grandl, Chowdhury, Akella, Ananthanarayanan, "Altruistic Scheduling in Multi-Resource Clusters" (Carbyne), OSDI 2016. Read from: https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~rgrandl/papers/carbyne.pdf. Pages read: abstract, Secs 1-5 (cluster and simulation results), related work.
- Result: "jobs yield fractions of their allocated resources without impacting their own completion times" (abstract, raw ~line 58). Leftover is reallocated for efficiency and short jobs. "CARBYNE closely approximates the state-of-the-art solutions (e.g., DRF) in terms of performance isolation, while providing 1.26x better efficiency and 1.59x lower average job completion time" (abstract, raw ~line 82). How much is free: "50% of the time, at least 20% of the allocated resources - 35%, 43%, 24%, 21%, 28%, 24% for [compute, memory, disk in/out, network in/out] - could be used as leftover" in the production Microsoft traces (Sec 3, raw line 626).
- Conditions: 100-machine cluster (TPC-DS, TPC-H, BigBench) plus simulation of 3,000-machine Facebook and 10,000-machine Microsoft traces; baselines DRF (fairness), SJF with critical-path (job time), Tetris (efficiency); DRF is the inter-job fair share (raw lines 1063, 1146-1157).
- Measured effect: vs DRF on TPC-DS "1.36x on average and 1.88x at the 95th percentile" (raw line 1349); simulation "up to 1.59x on average and 7.67x at the 95th percentile" (raw line 1454); Facebook trace "up to 2.85x ... and 2.23x on average" (raw line 1456). Online vs the best in each category: 0.06 units worse than DRF on fairness, 1.2x worse than SJF, 1.07x worse than Tetris (raw line 1225). Load: "at 1x cluster load, CARBYNE improves over alternatives by up to 1.57x on average ... at 2x the load, its gains are between 1.76x and 1.91x ... at 6x ... almost the same as at 4x load; this is because the cluster became saturated, without much room for leftover allocations" (raw line 1546). Slowdowns: "Only 16% of the jobs slow down - by at most 0.62x" (raw line 1419).
- Needs to know or measure: "estimates of tasks' resource demands - across CPU, memory, disk, and the network - and their durations" from "history of prior runs for recurring jobs" and similar tasks in a stage (raw lines 1065-1066); the DAG (to know which stages are barriers); the fair share from DRF.
- Failure modes: "CARBYNE assumes that we can accurately estimate tasks' resource demands and durations" (raw line 1548); tested with errors X in [-50, 50]%: "performs consistently better and becomes comparable when we underestimate" (raw line 1551). Saturated cluster: no gain (above). Failures need recomputation of the finish estimate (raw line 1013). Locality and stragglers are said to be minimally affected (raw line 1010).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): the idea (give back what does not delay you) only works when an agent has slack, i.e. waits on something else (a barrier, the serial check, a refill) and its finish time is predictable. Needs per-agent remaining-time estimates and the DAG.

### P5. Ghodsi, Sekar, Zaharia, Stoica, "Multi-Resource Fair Queueing for Packet Processing" (DRFQ), SIGCOMM 2012. Read from: https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2012/paper/sigcomm/p1.pdf. Pages read: Secs 1-8 text; proofs skimmed.
- Result: DRF in the time domain: "existing algorithms for DRF provide fair sharing in space ... In contrast, middleboxes require sharing in time; given a small number of resources ... that can each process only one packet at a time, the scheduler must interleave packets" (raw lines 19-24). DRFQ "generalizes the concept of virtual time from classical fair queuing to multiple resources that are consumed at different rates over time" (raw line 25). A packet's finish time is "the virtual start time plus the processing time on the dominant resource" (raw line 1001).
- Two natural rules rejected. Bottleneck fairness (find the currently congested resource, share it fairly): "it is not strategyproof; a flow can manipulate the scheduler ... by artificially increasing the amount of resources its packets use ... this can double the manipulative flow's throughput"; "when multiple resources can simultaneously be bottlenecked, this solution can lead to oscillations that substantially lower the total throughput" (raw line 17). Per-resource fairness (fair queueing independently per resource) also fails the share guarantee (raw line 421, Fig 11).
- Conditions: Click router on a 2.8 GHz Xeon with a 1 Gbps link, flows with different CPU/bandwidth profiles; packet simulations. Processing time per packet is modelled: "a simple linear model based on packet size" (raw line 2011, Table 6 R^2).
- Measured effect (quoted, Table 7, raw lines 2085-2090): flow 1 (bandwidth-bound) 191 Mbps alone, 75 Mbps under bottleneck fairness, 160 Mbps under DRFQ; flow 2 (CPU-bound) 33 / 32 / 28 Mbps. "With bottleneck fairness, the oscillations ... cause it to lose packets, back off, and get less than half the share it had running alone".
- Needs to know or measure: per-request consumption of each resource, "To implement any multi-resource scheduler, one needs to know the consumption of each packet for each resource" (raw line 2010); estimated before the request runs. "if the estimation is wrong, DRFQ will still run, but flows' shares may be off by the ratio to which processing times have been misestimated" (raw line 2064).
- Failure modes: assumes mostly "dominant-resource monotonic" flows (dominant resource does not change while backlogged) for the tightest guarantee (raw line 1898); a "dove-tailing" variant is needed otherwise (raw line 1343). Memoryless design is needed to avoid starvation after idleness (raw lines 413-417).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): this is the closest to a serial resource used one request at a time (a test suite that one job may run at a time): fair share of that resource's TIME, weighted by each job's dominant-resource time. Needs the expected time of each request on each resource before it starts. Also a warning against "share whichever resource is the bottleneck right now": manipulable and oscillating when two resources are near saturation.

### P6. Wang, Liang, Li, "Multi-Resource Fair Allocation in Heterogeneous Cloud Computing Systems" (DRFH), IEEE journal (Trans. Parallel and Distributed Systems / Networking line; the PDF is the author preprint). Read from: https://home.cse.ust.hk/~weiwa/papers/weiwang-drfh.pdf. Pages read: abstract, Secs 1-6 (analysis proofs skimmed).
- Result: DRF generalised from one server to many servers of different shapes: no envy, Pareto efficiency, "no coalition behavior of misreporting resource demands can benefit all its members" (abstract). A "Best-Fit DRFH" heuristic: serve the user with the lowest global dominant share, "the 'best' one whose remaining resources most suitably matches the demand" of that user's tasks, by smallest L1 distance H(i,l) between the demand vector and the server's remaining vector (raw lines 989-995; "O(k) time", raw line 1001).
- Conditions: Google cluster traces, 900+ users, 12K-server cluster configuration table; simulated 2,000 servers "so that fairness becomes relevant"; baseline "traditional Slots schedulers" with the best slot size tried.
- Measured effect: Table 2, Slots best case "dividing the maximum server ... into 14 slots leads to the highest overall utilization": CPU 43.9%, memory 28.0% (raw lines 1035-1040; 10 slots 35.1% / 23.4%; 20 slots 40.6% / 20.0%). "the two DRFH implementations significantly outperform the traditional Slots scheduler with much higher resource utilization" (raw line 1041) and "Best-Fit DRFH leads to uniformly higher resource utilization than the First-Fit alternative at all times" (raw line 1111). Job time: "no improvement over Slots scheduler for small jobs, a significant completion time reduction ... for those containing more tasks" (raw line 1175); percentages in Fig 7b are not readable in the text.
- Needs to know or measure: demand vector per user's tasks and the capacity vector of every server; the remaining-capacity vector at placement time.
- Failure modes: "While DRFH may violate the strong sharing incentive property ... this only happens in rare cases" (raw line 833); "the root cause ... is the heterogeneity among servers. When all servers are of the same specification ... DRFH reduces to DRF" (raw line 946). Assumes users report demands truthfully, strategy-proofness proved only in the paper's coalition form (raw line 562).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): with one machine this reduces to DRF (the paper's own statement for identical servers), so the heterogeneity result does not add anything for a single 24-thread box; useful only if agents run on more than one machine.

### P7. Parkes, Procaccia, Shah, "Beyond Dominant Resource Fairness: Extensions, Limitations, and Indivisibilities", EC 2012 (journal version ACM TEAC 2015). Read from: https://core.ac.uk/download/28944328.pdf (the Harvard DASH copy of the EC 2012 version, 25 pages). Pages read: abstract, Secs 1-6; proofs skimmed.
- Result: DRF keeps its properties with weights, zero demands, and group strategy-proofness (Sec 3, raw line 121-122). Limit: "DRF may provide very low social welfare compared to a welfare-maximizing allocation": with m resources, DRF "cannot have an approximation ratio better than m - epsilon for the social welfare" (Sec 4, raw lines 702-710), and the same holds for any mechanism with sharing incentive (raw line 712) or envy-freeness. With indivisible tasks: "Under indivisibilities there is no mechanism that satisfies PO, SI, and SP" (Theorem 5.1, raw line 735); "EF and PO are trivially incompatible" (raw line 46).
- Conditions: Leontief preferences (agents want resources "in fixed proportions", raw line 18); divisible resources; utility is the fraction of dominant resource usable (raw line 99).
- Measured effect: none (theory paper; no experiments).
- Needs to know or measure: each agent's fixed-proportion demand vector and weight.
- Failure modes: as above: low total welfare; indivisible tasks break the guarantees; "resource fragmentation ... requires careful attention" (Sec 6, raw line 911).
- On one machine with agents (reasoned): fairness guarantees do not buy throughput. If the goal is total work done (not isolation), DRF is the wrong objective by up to a factor m in the worst case; a packing or welfare objective is needed.
