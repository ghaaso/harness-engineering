# o2 refute (2026-11-18): verdicts on o2-claims.md C1-C8, from the paper texts alone
Texts read: the extractions under the brief's `papers/` scratchpad (memory/thrash, cluster/borg5, memory/merkel, classical/{chekuri,multires,panigrahy,res2025,res2025h}, quota/{cfs,hybrid,routellm}, multiresource/drfq). No web search was needed; the counter-evidence below is in the papers' own text. `memory/denning-thrash/p.pdf` is a saved 404 page; the Denning text is `memory/thrash/p.txt`.

## C1 PARTIALLY
Holds: n identical programs "fully occupy the memory" (M = ns), T >> n >> 1, and adding one more gives "a complete collapse of service" instead of "a gradual degradation" (p. 919). The analysis "breaks down when it is not true T>>n" (p. 919). Eq. 19 and "there must be space in memory for every active process's working set" (p. 921).
Does not hold as stated: (a) the paper gives two cures, not one: "we must do one or both of the following: first, we must prevent the missing-page probability m from fluctuating; and second, we must reduce the traverse time T" (p. 921). (b) "No measurement" is wrong. It cites one: Fikes et al. cut T from 10 ms to 1 ms and "the throughput of their system was increased by a factor of approximately 10" (p. 920). (c) It recommends a reserve, not filling memory to the limit: Q(p) = fM with 0<f<1, "(1-f)M pages of memory are held in reserve", because "if Q(p) = M, an unanticipated working set expansion can trigger thrashing" (pp. 920-921).
Corrected: "...collapses it (analytic; the paper cites one measurement from Fikes et al., a 10x throughput gain from a 10x shorter T). The stated cures are to keep the active working sets within memory, with a reserve (1-f)M for unexpected growth, and/or to cut the page transfer time T."

## C2 CONFIRMED
§6.2 says, word for word: "If a machine runs out of non-compressible resources, the Borglet immediately terminates tasks, from lowest to highest priority, until the remaining reservations can be met. If the machine runs out of compressible resources, the Borglet throttles usage (favoring LS tasks) so that short load spikes can be handled without killing any tasks." Memory and disk space are its examples of non-compressible resources, and CPU cycles and disk I/O bandwidth of compressible ones. "Kills nothing at first" is the right reading: the next sentence is "If things do not improve, Borgmaster will remove one or more tasks from the machine." Cells have "up to tens of thousands of machines" (abstract). Caveat for the setting: §6.2 also says "Using slack memory is disabled by default, because it increases the chance of a task being killed." This is a policy description from production, not a measured comparison.

## C3 CONFIRMED (as reported in the dissertation; the EuroSys paper was not read)
Merkel, ch. 5: the machine is a "2.4GHz Intel Core2 Quad Q6600" (§5.3), which reaches memory over a front-side bus. On it: "slowed down by a factor of 3.9, the SPEC benchmark ... by a factor of 3.2 when four instances ... are running in parallel" (§5.3.6). On the "AMD Opteron 2354 quad-core": "slowed down by a factor of 2.7 ... (Core2: factor 3.9) ... the most memory intensive SPEC benchmark ... factor 2.5 (Core2: factor 3.2)" (§5.3.7). The authors attribute the smaller slowdown to "the integrated memory controller". Pairing: "When one benchmark from the first and one from the second group are combined, neither of the benchmarks is slowed down significantly" (§5.3, Fig. 5.5). That pairing test ran "on two cores with separate L2 caches". Carry-over is the claim's own "fails if": the paper measured 2010 four-core chips, not a 12-core Zen 4.

## C4 PARTIALLY
(a) Chekuri-Khanna Lemma 2.6 is quoted correctly, but the problem is not "the shortest schedule" of jobs that share capacity over time. It is vector scheduling: "schedule n d-dimensional tasks on m machines such that the maximum load over all dimensions and all machines is minimized" (abstract, Def. 1.1). That is a partition across m machines. On one machine (m = 1) it is trivial, so the (d+1) bound does not carry over to one machine as stated.
(b) Perotin-Sun-Raghavan Theorem 6 is a lower bound for moldable jobs under precedence constraints. "Local priority" means "without taking into account the precedence graphs", and the bad instance uses "precedence constraints [that] form a tree" (§6). It says nothing about independent jobs, so the clause "any list scheduler that ranks jobs by their own properties only can be a factor d off" needs that condition.
(c) Panigrahy et al. hedge, for vector bin packing (fewest bins): "FFD should probably be the algorithm of choice". That is a heuristic recommendation for a different objective, not a result.
Corrected: "For vector scheduling (partitioning jobs across m machines to minimise the maximum load in any dimension), list scheduling on summed demand is within d+1. For precedence-constrained moldable jobs, list schedulers that ignore the precedence graph can be a factor d off. For vector bin packing with one binding resource, the authors suggest FFD 'should probably' be used."

## C5 CONFIRMED
Das and Sun, arXiv 2509.01086 v1. The model is "a job is revealed after all of its predecessor jobs have finished" (§1). "Resources are reusable" (§1). "we consider non-preemptive scheduling" (§1). The greedy bound reads "every greedy algorithm could be as bad as Ω(n)-competitive ... long and skinny jobs ... short and fat jobs" (§1; Lemma 7.1). Theorem 5.6 is "no algorithm (even randomized ones) for online multi-resource scheduling can achieve a competitive ratio better than (d-1)/2", with d ≥ 3 (checked in the HTML text; the PDF extraction drops the symbols). Two notes: the Ω(n) instance in Lemma 7.1 uses a single resource with budget 1 and relies on precedence; and Theorem 5.1 adds that even with one resource no online algorithm beats Ω(log n). Both are worst-case results with no measurement, as the claim says.

## C6 PARTIALLY
Holds: "the group as a whole will be limited to cpu.cfs_quota_us units of CPU time within the period of cpu.cfs_period_us" (§4). The experiments used periods of "100ms, 250ms, and 500ms" (§7). In the adopted design a cfs_rq is throttled once it has spent its local quota and "there was no global bandwidth available to 'top-up'"; it then stays unschedulable "in the current quota period" (§6.2).
Wrong source: the quoted lines 149-153 ("consumes all its runtime and when there is nothing left to borrow ... throttled") describe §5.1 "CFS hard limits". That is the earlier approach, which the paper sets aside: "for the reasons described in the subsequent sections, we have now opted for global specification" (§4).
Refuted part: "unused quota expires at the end of each period" holds only for the global pool, which is refreshed each period (§6.2, "The refresh operation first refreshes the global quota pool"). Line 341 is the timer that is programmed "to expire every cfs_period_us", not the quota expiring. Slices already handed to a CPU carry over: "the time locally assigned may have been allocated from the global pool in a previous quota interval ... The maximum outstanding over-subscription ... is constant at num_cpus × batch_slice" (§6.3). Quota can also be "'stranded'", and the authors want "stronger guarantees that quota will expire relatively concurrently" (§6.3).
Corrected: "...is throttled once its local slice is spent and the global pool is empty. The global pool is refilled each period, but slices already handed to CPUs can carry into the next period, up to num_cpus × slice (10 ms default)."

## C7 PARTIALLY
Holds: Table 7 gives flow 1 (bandwidth-bound) 191, 75 and 160 Mbps alone, under bottleneck fairness and under DRFQ. The run was two TCP flows, 20 ms added latency and a bottleneck check "every 300 ms" (§8.1.3). The introduction says bottleneck fairness "can lead to oscillations that substantially lower the total throughput".
Corrections: (a) The NIC was 1 Gbps, but "we throttled its outgoing bandwidth to 200 Mbps ... and ... CPU time ... to 20%" (§8.1). (b) The paper's text says "the oscillations in available bandwidth for flow 2 cause it to lose packets", while its table shows flow 1 losing. The claim's ellipsis hides that the text and table disagree. (c) The doubling under bottleneck fairness is not measured. It is a worked example (Fig. 3: share from 1/3 to almost 1/2; "In similar examples with more flows, flow 1 can almost double its share", §3.1). The measured doubling (Fig. 12) is under per-resource fair queueing, a different rule.
Corrected: "...on a Click middlebox throttled to 200 Mbps and 20 % CPU, ... 75 Mbps against 191 alone and 160 under DRFQ (one run, two TCP flows). A worked example in the same paper shows a flow can nearly double its share under that rule by inflating its CPU use."

## C8 CONFIRMED (claim text); one error in its evidence line
Hybrid LLM Table 1, medium gap (Llama-2 13b against GPT-3.5-turbo): drops of 1.0, 0.8 and 0.8 % at 20 %, and 3.5, 3.4 and 2.9 % at 40 %. The text says "All routers achieve 20% (40%) cost advantage with ≤ 1% (≤ 4%) quality drop" (§4). Large gap (FLAN-t5 800m against Llama-2 13b), 40 %: 13.8, 13.1 and 10.3 %, so "10.3 % or more" is right. The drop is relative BART score "w.r.t all-at-large". RouteLLM (ICLR 2025, §5): "On MMLU ... all routers perform poorly at the level of the random router when trained only on Arena dataset", which it attributes to "out-of-distribution" questions. Adding about 1,500 golden-label samples ("less than 2% of the overall training data") gave "approximately 20% less GPT-4 calls than random".
Evidence-line error: "for the large gap only the transformed router beat random" is wrong. The paper says "Both rdet and rprob perform marginally better than the random routing baseline". rtrans is the only one that is clearly better.
