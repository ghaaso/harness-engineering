## Summary
- Every system here switches on one cheap local signal: failed test-and-set attempts and empty-queue count (Lim), runnable threads minus hardware contexts (Johnson), time inside and outside a critical section plus bus-busy cycles measured in a short training run (Suleman), a rolling share of writes and early releases (Marathe).
- The rules are thresholds with hysteresis or a closed formula, not a learned model. Lim's own switching rule is a threshold; he names competitive and aging policies only as alternatives, untested.
- Suleman gives the one result that fits the question directly: two different limits (a serial critical section, and a shared bus) each give a closed-form thread count, P_CS = sqrt(T_NoCS/T_CS) and P_BW = 100 / BU1; the run-time estimate landed within 1 to 3 percent of the best fixed thread count on 8 applications (simulator, 32 cores).
- Gains over the best fixed method are small to modest when one method fits (Lim: "modest" for locks); large only against the worst fixed method or under overload (Johnson: 85 percent of peak at 200 percent load; Marathe: more than five times over eager acquire on one benchmark).
- Switching has a stated cost and a stated failure: Lim's lock degrades when contention flips about every 1000 critical sections; Johnson's controller oscillates when a load spike is short against its update interval.
- Not tested in any of them: several unrelated processes sharing a machine and a serial step shared across processes; all numbers are one machine, one benchmark family.

## Papers

### P1. Lim and Agarwal, "Reactive synchronization algorithms for multiprocessors", ASPLOS 1994. Copy read: https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~kubitron/courses/cs194-24-S14/hand-outs/p25-lim.pdf (course copy of the ACM paper). Text: p1/raw.txt, 1-770.
- Signal measured: lock contention. For the spin lock, failed test-and-set attempts per acquisition; in the other direction, whether the queue was empty at acquisition.
- How it is measured: by the acquiring process itself, as a count inside the lock code. QUOTED (l.244-258): "The reactive spin lock changes from the test-and-test-and-set protocol to the queuing protocol when the number of failed test-and-set attempts experienced by a process while acquiring the lock exceeds a threshold."
- Switching or control rule: threshold up, N consecutive empty-queue acquisitions down. QUOTED (l.256-258): "if a process finds the queue to be empty for some number of consecutive lock acquisitions. This provides some hysteresis to lessen the likelihood of oscillating and thrashing between protocols." Correctness during a switch comes from "consensus objects" (l.278): only the holder of the valid protocol's consensus object may change the mode. Competitive, aging and give-up-and-use-the-average policies are only proposed (l.352-355), not measured.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.8): "reactive algorithms perform close to the best static choice of protocols at all levels of contention. Furthermore, with mixed levels of contention, reactive algorithms outperform passive algorithms with fixed protocols, provided that contention levels do not change too frequently." And on applications: "modest performance gains for spin locks and significant gains for fetch-and-op." Per-protocol costs QUOTED (l.28-30 area): no contention 10 vs 21 cycles (test-and-set vs queue), under contention 220 vs 100 cycles. The reactive fetch-and-op picked test-and-test-and-set for 1-2 processors, queue for 4-16, combining tree for 32-64 (l.492). Bar charts of the full curves: in a figure, not read.
- Conditions of the experiment: cycle-accurate simulator of the Alewife machine, 33 MHz, up to 128 processors; the switching-overhead test on a real 16-node Alewife (l.361-362, 618).
- What must be true for it to apply: contention changes slowly against the cost of a switch; the right threshold is machine-specific. QUOTED (l.222): "the switching policy will have to be tuned for each machine architecture. However, we emphasize that this tuning is application independent."
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.761): "If contention levels vary too frequently ..., the overhead of switching protocols dominates and the performance of the reactive lock suffers. ... begins to deteriorate when forced to change protocols as frequently as every 1000 critical sections. However, the reactive lock is still always better than the worst static choice." The waiting method is held fixed (spin); choosing waiting method too is left as future work (l.40 area).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. A two-state method choice with a count threshold and a run-length hysteresis is cheap, but the switch must be rarer than the regime changes it follows, or a fixed method wins.

### P2. Johnson, Stoica, Ailamaki and Mowry, "Decoupling contention management from scheduling", ASPLOS 2010. Copy read: https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/142307/files/p117-johnson.pdf. Text: p3/raw.txt, 1-490.
- Signal measured: one number, "overload", the count of excess runnable threads over hardware contexts. QUOTED (l.231): "The controller uses OS process accounting facilities to monitor a single 'sensor' value: overload, defined as the number of excess runnable threads in the system (negative in underload situations)."
- How it is measured: a daemon thread wakes every 1 to 10 ms (l.243), reads OS process accounting at about 100 microsecond resolution (l.278), and writes a sleep target.
- Switching or control rule: it does not switch lock type. Locks always spin (queue lock, TP-MCS); when overload is positive, a random subset of spinning threads is told to block, and they wake on lower load or after about one time slice (l.72). QUOTED (l.72): "Effective contention management uses spinning for fast lock hand-offs and does not block in response to contention. Effective load control then prevents spinning threads from causing overload while keeping load low enough that lock holders are not preempted." Control is on all locks as a group, not per lock (l.226).
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.103): "we achieve peak performance for lightly loaded machines, while retaining 85% of that peak even with 200% load (two runnable threads per hardware context)." On why plain spinning fails: QUOTED (l.128): "as soon as utilization passes 100% priority inversions quickly dominate, wasting up to 85% of CPU time ... at peak performance, less than 10% of CPU time is wasted spinning on contended locks". Comparison curves against pthread mutex and TP-MCS (Figure 11): in a figure, not read.
- Conditions of the experiment: Sun T5220 Niagara II, 64 hardware contexts, 64 GB, Solaris 10 (l.294); microbenchmark with one global lock, Raytrace (SPLASH-2), TM-1 and TPC-C on a database engine.
- What must be true for it to apply: an accurate, frequent load reading from the OS. QUOTED (l.481): "Mainstream Linux kernels do not even track these statistics, and Solaris mechanism is not designed for frequent use: cost increases linearly with the number of threads".
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.471): "whenever the system encounters a large spike (or dip) in load which is small relative to the load controller's update interval, load control will respond too late or not at all, leading to rapid oscillation between priority inversions and low utilization." Nested lock acquisition breaks the assumption that a spinner is expendable (l.473). Its predecessor, load-triggered backoff, failed as "a one-sided control mechanism" (l.193): sleeping threads could not be woken early. The authors suggest a low-pass filter, PID and Kalman filtering as untested remedies (l.479).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. Runnable count against cores is the one signal that separates "processor is the limit" (overload) from "a lock is the limit" (spinning inside an underloaded machine); the method sheds waiters only when the first holds.

### P3. Suleman, Qureshi and Patt, "Feedback-driven threading: power-efficient and high-performance execution of multi-threaded workloads on CMPs", ASPLOS 2008. Copy read: https://utw10235.utweb.utexas.edu/people/suleman/papers/fdt.pdf. Text: p7/raw.txt, 1-560.
- Signal measured: two, one per limit. For a serial step, time inside the critical section (T_CS) and outside it (T_NoCS); for memory, off-chip bus utilization with one thread (BU1).
- How it is measured: a training loop of the first few iterations, single-threaded; cycle counter read at critical-section entry and exit; a hardware performance counter for bus-busy cycles. QUOTED (l.251-254): "Training loop is terminated if the ratio of TCS to TNoCS is stable (within 5%) for three consecutive iterations. Otherwise the training continues for a maximum of 1% of the total iterations." BAT stops early (l.462): "after 10000 cycles, if the product of the average bus utilization times the number of cores available on chip is less than 100%, BAT predicts that the system can not become bandwidth limited".
- Switching or control rule: closed form, thread count set once per kernel. QUOTED (l.210): "(PCS) increases only as the square-root of the ratio of time outside the critical section to the time inside the critical section." QUOTED (l.256): "The number of threads is chosen as the minimum of PCS and the number of cores available on the chip." Bus: P_BW = 100 / BU1 (l.438-441), assuming bus use grows linearly with threads (l.432).
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.260): "the execution time with SAT is within 1% of the minimum execution time" (4 sync-limited applications); QUOTED (l.465): "the execution time with BAT is within 3% of the minimum execution time" (4 bandwidth-limited). QUOTED (l.18): "SAT can reduce both execution time and power by up to 66% and 78%"; QUOTED (l.32): combined, "reduces the average execution time by 17% and the average power by 59%" over 12 applications. Illustrative number, QUOTED (l.210 area): with a critical section of 1 percent the system can become critical-section limited at a handful of threads (the sentence is cut in the text; the PageMine case, l.261: critical section 2.34 percent, estimate 6.53 rounded to 7).
- Conditions of the experiment: cycle-accurate x86 simulator, 32 in-order cores, 8 MB shared L3, bus serving one line per 32 cycles (l.96); 12 applications, one running at a time.
- What must be true for it to apply: one parallel loop whose iterations are alike, so a short sample predicts the rest; one limit dominating; bus use linear in threads. QUOTED (l.537-541): BAT predicted 7 threads where 8 was best "because BAT assumes that bandwidth utilization increases linearly with the number of threads."
- Failure modes: phase changes inside a kernel are not tracked (the estimate is made once per kernel; REASONED from l.256-258, the paper's phase handling was not found by search). The sub-linear bandwidth case above. Only the minimum of the two estimates is described for the combined case; interplay of the two limits in one program is tested only as the average over 12 applications.
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. This is the closest published form of "measure which limit binds, then set the width by formula". The same two measurements (share of time in the serial step; busy share of the shared resource for one worker) give a width for N workers, and the answer is the smaller of the two widths and the core count.

### P4. Scherer and Scott, "Advanced contention management for dynamic software transactional memory", PODC 2005. Copy read: https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/2005_PODC_CM.pdf. Text: p5/raw.txt, 1-500.
- Signal measured: per transaction, the number of objects opened so far (a priority), and the enemy's priority.
- How it is measured: local counters in the contention manager.
- Switching or control rule: not a switch; a fixed policy chosen per environment. QUOTED (l.83): "Polka ... backs off for a number of intervals equal to the difference in priorities between the transaction and its enemy. Unlike Karma, however, the length of these backoff intervals increases exponentially." Read-style rule (l.477): visible reads win when write contention is low; invisible reads when high.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.190): "No single manager outperforms all others in every benchmark, but Polka achieves good throughput even in the cases where it is outperformed." QUOTED (l.477-478): "visible reads yield a large performance benefit due to the lower cost of read-object validation when write contention is low. With sufficiently high write contention, however, the writes to shared memory cache lines needed to add a transaction to a object's readers list degrade performance ... and invisible reads become desirable." Throughput curves: in a figure, not read.
- Conditions of the experiment: 16-processor SunFire 6800, UltraSPARC III 1.2 GHz, Java 1.5 beta HotSpot; micro-benchmarks (IntSet, Stack, ArrayCounter, LFUCache and others), 1 to 48 threads (l.103).
- What must be true for it to apply: obstruction-free STM with a modular manager interface.
- Failure modes: ArrayCounter, a torture test, makes several managers livelock; Karmexp, differing from Polka only in how the backoff is fixed, livelocks (l.137-139). Open question stated by the authors (l.481): "to what extent and with what level of overhead contention managers can be made to adapt between multiple policies."
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. Evidence that a fixed policy chosen robustly can lose little against the best one; and that the backoff shape (growing) mattered as much as the rule.

### P5. Marathe, Scherer and Scott, "Adaptive software transactional memory", DISC 2005 (LNCS 3724). Copy read: https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/scott/papers/2005_DISC_ASTM.pdf. Text: p6/raw.txt, 1-260.
- Signal measured: per thread, rolling averages of the percentage of writes and of early releases (releases that follow at least one write) across recent transactions.
- How it is measured: counters in the transaction descriptor, no sampling phase.
- Switching or control rule: QUOTED (l.107): "So long as the percentage of writes is below a threshold w and the percentage of early releases exceeds another threshold r, subsequent transactions use lazy acquire; otherwise they use eager acquire." Thresholds (l.108): "we have set the early release threshold r at 50% and the write access threshold w at 25%. Limited experimentation with other values suggests that results are largely insensitive to the exact percentage used." The object reference style (direct or indirect) adapts per object by whether it is currently acquired.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.232): "no single STM performs the best (or comparably) in all possible scenarios. ... a new STM system that adapts to the offered workload, yielding throughput comparable to the best existing system in all scenarios we have tested." Largest single gap, QUOTED (l.227): "Lazy acquire is a clear winner, with throughput more than a factor of five higher than that of eager acquire" (RandomGraphList).
- Conditions of the experiment: 16-processor SunFire 6800, Java 1.5 beta, 10 s per run, 1 to 48 threads, concurrent list, tree and cache benchmarks (l.112).
- What must be true for it to apply: recent transactions predict the next ones ("transactions will tend to behave similarly to those recently completed", l.108).
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.102): "there is no way in general to distinguish between these scenarios. ... the tradeoffs largely 'cancel out': Though eager acquire is usually a little faster, neither dramatically outperforms the other in most cases." So the adaptive rule is kept only for the one case with a large gap. Mixed read/write loads can make objects flip between states repeatedly (l.93). Lazy ASTM livelocks for a while at low thread counts on one benchmark (l.227).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. The adaptive form earned its keep only where one method was far worse (five times); where methods are within a small margin, the default won. A switch is worth building when the regimes differ by a large factor.

### P6. Dice, "Malthusian locks", EuroSys 2017 (arXiv extended version 1511.06035v7). Copy read: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06035v7. Text: p8/raw.txt. NOTE: the arXiv text carries slide-style annotations and runs words together in places; only clean sentences are quoted. Read partly (abstract, design, policy sections), not every experiment.
- Signal measured: whether the lock is saturated: whether at least one thread is always waiting; no separate sensor. Active set (ACS) is kept just large enough to saturate the lock.
- How it is measured: inside the lock; culling at unlock time moves excess waiters to a passive set.
- Switching or control rule: concurrency restriction. QUOTED (l.57): "We borrow the concept of swapping from the field of memory management and impose concurrency restriction (CR) if a lock suffers from contention. The resultant admission order is unfair over the short term but we explicitly provide long-term fairness by periodically shifting..." It uses no tuned parameter or learned state. QUOTED (l.2298): "No stateful adaptive mechanisms are employed".
- Measured effect: stated as avoiding collapse, not a percentage found in clean text: QUOTED (l.515): "CR does not improve performance in this region, but it avoids worsening performance arising from scalability collapse." Region below saturation, QUOTED (l.499-503): "when the thread count is less than saturation, CR would be ineffective and does not operate ... providing neither harm nor benefit." Numeric speedups: in a figure or in noisy text, not read.
- Conditions of the experiment: SPARC T5 and other systems with many logical CPUs (l.638; details not cleanly extracted).
- What must be true for it to apply: threads are interchangeable; a lock is the serial step; unfairness over the short term is tolerable; a hot shared cache is the mechanism of the gain (fewer distinct threads touching the critical-section data, l.704-712).
- Failure modes: counter-example named by the author (l.13): a ragged barrier where all threads must progress. QUOTED (l.2680): "While CR typically does no harm, it is also difficult to determine" when it helps, since several resources are usually involved.
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. With a serial step, running fewer workers at that step does the same work with less interference; the rule needs no mode detection because it is inactive below saturation.

## Across the papers
- Agreement: the best method depends on one quantity (contention, load, share of serial time, share of writes); a short local measurement is enough; thresholds are insensitive (Marathe l.108) or machine-specific (Lim l.222).
- Agreement: the cost of switching sets a floor on how fast regimes may change (Lim: about 1000 critical sections; Johnson: update interval against spike length).
- Disagreement: Lim and Marathe switch the method; Johnson and Dice keep one method and change the width (how many wait or run). Suleman does the same for process count. REASONED: for a machine of workers, width control has the simpler rule, and method switching needs a regime that lasts.
- Gap: all five (and Karlin 1991 by its abstract) treat one application or one lock at a time; none tests several unrelated programs sharing the machine, or the serial step being a shared external resource (a test suite one process can run at a time).
- Gap: gains are reported against the best fixed method in the same environment; none reports cost of measurement beyond Johnson's syscall cost.
- Ranking claim from the papers: SUMMARY only, from the abstract of Karlin et al. (search result): adaptive spin-then-block beat non-adaptive among seven strategies. Not verified in full text.
