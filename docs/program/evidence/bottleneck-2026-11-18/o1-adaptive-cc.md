## Summary
- Only three of the seven switch at run time from a measured signal: MOCC (abort counts per page), CormCC (per-partition features, a classifier, once a second) and Cicada (throughput gradient, every 5 ms). Polyjuice is trained offline; Tebaldi and IC3 are fixed by offline analysis; Bamboo has an optional per-lock switch with no rule given.
- The signals are all cheap counters the workers already have: aborts, committed transactions, read/write mix, how partitionable the work is. None uses lock-wait time or CPU or memory state.
- The simplest rule that works (MOCC) is a counter with a threshold (10) and a reset. It copes with a hot spot that moves every 0.1 s; a threshold that is too high learns slowly, one too low is fooled by stray aborts.
- The switch itself can be made cheap: CormCC's mediated switch keeps at least 93% of the better protocol during the change; its full-stop switch stalls on long transactions.
- The gain from mixing is bounded: CormCC 0.91x to 2.6x of the best single protocol in random workloads; Polyjuice 15 to 56 percent over the best known one, and 8 percent slower at no contention.
- Gaps: no paper switches on CPU, memory or serial-step saturation. All run on one fixed machine with the workload being transactions on records. Oscillation is not reported as a measured problem in any of them; Cicada says its search had no local maxima (one workload set).

## Papers

### P1. Wang and Kimura, "Mostly-optimistic concurrency control for highly contended dynamic workloads on a thousand cores", PVLDB 2016 (vol 10). Copy read: https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol10/p49-wang.pdf. Text: mocc/paper.txt, 787 lines.
- Signal measured: aborts caused by failed read verification, counted per page ("temperature"). QUOTED (l.161): "The temperature statistics track the number of aborts due to verification failures."
- How it is measured: QUOTED (l.165): "Each page p maintains a temperature tempp, which probabilistically implies that there were about 2tempp aborts in the page." The counter is incremented with probability 2^-temp so a hot page is not written often (avoids the counter itself becoming the contended cache line).
- Switching or control rule: a read on a page whose temperature is at or above a threshold H takes a read lock (pessimistic); otherwise optimistic. QUOTED (l.375-377): "In most cases, we recommend setting the threshold to 10." Counter forgetting: "The counter must be either reset or decremented because the nature of the page might change over time. Our experiments in Section 5 occasionally reset the value of the counter to zero." Also a retry list of records from the aborted run, locked on the next try.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.12): "MOCC performs 8x and 23x faster than OCC and pessimistic locking, respectively" (high-conflict YCSB, 288 cores). QUOTED (l.543): on TPC-C "MOCC behaves exactly the same as OCC because the temperature statistics of almost all data pages are below the threshold". QUOTED (l.716): on a long read-write transaction, OCC aborts more than 99.5% and "MOCC performs an order of magnitude better than all others".
- Conditions of the experiment: FOEDUS database, 288 cores (16 sockets), YCSB with 50 hot records and TPC-C; a shifting-workload test where the hot table changes every 0.1 s (l.673).
- What must be true for it to apply: aborts are a good proxy for future conflict on the same page; a thread can take a lock on a record cheaply (queue lock designed for many cores).
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.712): "H = 20 requires 220 x 1,000,000 aborts to trigger read locks, which is virtually a monolithic OCC." QUOTED (l.713): "When the threshold is too small, however, MOCC can be confused by just a small number of sporadic aborts, leading to lower performance." The reset makes the statistic wrong just after it (l.377).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. A per-resource counter with a probabilistic increment, a threshold and a periodic reset is a rule a monitor could copy: count "waits caused by X", switch that resource to the safe method above a fixed count, forget on a timer.

### P2. Tang, Elmore et al., "Toward Coordination-free and Reconfigurable Mixed Concurrency Control" (CormCC), USENIX ATC 2018. Copy read: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/atc18/atc18-tang.pdf. Text: corm/paper.txt, 629 lines. The classifier details are in a technical report not read (cited l.226).
- Signal measured: features extracted per partition from worker statistics; the figures show partitionability (share of cross-partition transactions), read/write ratio, skew. QUOTED (l.298): "A coordinator thread is used for extracting features for the prediction classifiers from statistics collected by workers and predicting the ideal protocol to be used." The exact feature list is not in this paper (in a figure, not read; TR not opened).
- How it is measured: QUOTED (l.433): "CormCC collects features every second and concurrently selects the ideal protocol for each partition."
- Switching or control rule: QUOTED (l.226): "we build two binary classifiers to predict the ideal protocol for each partition." Candidates: partition locks (PartCC), Silo OCC, no-wait 2PL. Trained on benchmarks with varied parameters (l.303). The switch is "mediated" (l.158-212): all workers first run a protocol compatible with both old and new, then drop the old one.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.514): "CormCC can achieve at most 2.5x, 1.9x, 1.8x, and 1.7x throughput of PartCC, OCC, 2PL, and Hybrid respectively." QUOTED (l.518): "the lowest ratio in YCSB and TPC-C test is 0.91x and 0.94x ... CormCC can achieve at least 91% and 94% throughput of the best protocol due to wrong protocol selection for some partitions." Switch cost, QUOTED (l.563): "Mediated protocol can achieve at least 93% throughput of OCC or 2PL"; (l.564) "the maximum throughput drop is about 20% during protocol switching from PartCC to OCC" with long transactions.
- Conditions of the experiment: in-memory prototype, YCSB (10 million records) and TPC-C with 32 warehouses/partitions; parameters re-randomised every 5 s (l.433), a 100 s test with points every 2 s (l.514).
- What must be true for it to apply: the data can be split into partitions with different access patterns; workload parameters are in the range the classifiers were trained on; the three protocols' fast paths can be run side by side.
- Failure modes: wrong picks cost up to 9% (YCSB) and 6% (TPC-C) against the best single one in these tests (l.518); a full stop-and-switch "suffers due to waiting for the completion of long-running transactions" (l.563); mixed execution has a measured overhead (Figure 15, in a figure, not read). Oscillation and workloads outside training are not tested in the text read.
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. A switch that first runs both methods for a short overlap avoids stopping everybody; a classifier trained on varied workloads picks per part, not for the whole.

### P3. Wang, Zhang et al., "Polyjuice: High-Performance Transactions via Learned Concurrency Control", OSDI 2021. Copy read: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi21-wang-jiachen.pdf. Text: poly/paper.txt, 881 lines.
- Signal measured: throughput of each candidate policy on a replay of logged transactions (a training signal, not a run-time one). At run time the policy table is looked up by transaction type, access number and number of earlier aborts (l.293).
- How it is measured: QUOTED (l.296): "During regular execution, Polyjuice logs executed transactions together with their inputs. Using a separate training machine, Polyjuice emulates the target workload by reissuing transactions with their logged inputs. We measure a policy's commit throughput under the emulated workload."
- Switching or control rule: an evolutionary search over a table of actions, seeded with OCC, 2PL and IC3-like policies (l.309). Retrain rule used in the analysis, QUOTED (l.618): "retraining is deferred until the predicted conflict rate differs from the one used for training the current policy by 15%." Conflict rate, QUOTED (l.616): "conflict_rate = conflict_requests/total_requests within n minutes", n = 5.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.55): "Polyjuice can find a CC policy whose throughput is better than the best of existing algorithms by 15% to 56%." QUOTED (l.55): "For workloads with almost no contention, Polyjuice learns the same policy as OCC and incurs 8% slowdown due to its implementation overhead." Policy switch, QUOTED (l.698): "it takes about 3 seconds to fully switch to a new policy, and switching does not negatively impact performance."
- Conditions of the experiment: 56-core, 2-socket machine; TPC-C, TPC-E, a microbenchmark; trained for the given thread count and warehouse count (l.330).
- What must be true for it to apply: QUOTED (l.716): "training takes on the order of several hundred seconds. Thus, Polyjuice is not suitable for scenarios in which workload changes quicker than every few minutes." Replay must reproduce the access pattern (l.718).
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.703): "the performance of Polyjuice (1-warehouse) is 71% of Silo under 48 warehouses"; "the performance differences between fixed and optimal policies are small when the evaluation workload is not too far off from the training workload." The paper itself says (l.56) it "is not suitable for dynamic workloads that can change rapidly and unpredictably." Its retrain count (15 retrains in 196 days) comes from an e-commerce trace, request logs only (l.616-618).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. A learned table fits a workload that repeats (a daily peak); it does not answer a change within minutes. A cheap fixed rule must stand in the gap.

### P4. Su, Crooks, Ding, Alvisi, Xie, "Bringing Modular Concurrency Control to the Next Level" (Tebaldi), SIGMOD 2017. Copy read: https://cs.utexas.edu/~lorenzo/papers/Su17Bringing.pdf. Text: teb/paper.txt, 556 lines. (The brief named the authors differently; the PDF lists these.)
- Signal measured: none at run time. Groups of transactions are chosen by hand or offline analysis. QUOTED (l.295): "Optimal grouping for both benchmarks is obtained by recursively identifying highly contended transactions and pairing them with concurrency controls well-suited to the transactions' inherent structure."
- How it is measured: not applicable. (CormCC's criticism, QUOTED corm l.431: "Tebaldi needs to know the conflicts of a workload a priori such that it can utilize the static analysis [23] to make an efficient configuration offline.")
- Switching or control rule: a tree of protocols; a parent protocol handles conflicts between child groups (2PL, serializable snapshot isolation, runtime pipelining). No run-time switching.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.35): "more than 20x throughput improvement over the basic two-phase locking protocol, and a 3.7x throughput increase over Callas". Overhead, QUOTED (l.472): adding a runtime-pipelining layer, "throughput drops by 40%".
- Conditions of the experiment: 20-core machines on a 10 Gb network, TPC-C, SEATS, microbenchmarks; distributed key-value store (l.224).
- What must be true for it to apply: the transaction types and their conflicts are known beforehand; the right grouping is found by a person.
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.450): at low conflict "the overhead of maintaining the pipeline outweighs its benefit: the more conservative but simple 2PL performs best". Every transaction passes every protocol on its path (l.468).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. It is a design time split by kind of work; it shows that the fit method depends on the kind of conflict, not that it can be detected live.

### P5. Wang, Mu, Cui, Yi, Chen, Li, "Scaling Multicore Databases via Constrained Parallel Execution" (IC3), SIGMOD 2016. Copy read: https://cs.nyu.edu/media/publications/TR2016-981.pdf (the NYU tech report of the same paper). Text: ic3/paper.txt, 1162 lines.
- Signal measured: none at run time; static analysis of the transaction types before execution (l.54-56, 292). It tracks direct dependencies between transactions at run time but does not choose a protocol from them.
- How it is measured: QUOTED (l.56): "we assume the transaction workload is static and known before the execution of any transaction." A later section adds online analysis for new transaction types with a transitional graph (l.274-283).
- Switching or control rule: fixed. QUOTED (l.229): "IC3 is not beneficial for all application workloads; it is only effective for contentious workloads that access multiple tables within a single transaction."
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.20): on TPC-C with 1 warehouse, "IC3 achieves 434K transactions/sec on 64 threads while the throughput of 2PL and OCC is under 50K transactions/sec." QUOTED (l.425): "Under low contention, IC3's throughput is 6% lower than 2PL due to the overhead of dependency tracking."
- Conditions of the experiment: 64-core machine, 30 s runs, threads pinned one per core (l.336); micro, TPC-C, TPC-E.
- What must be true for it to apply: known transaction types; contended multi-table transactions.
- Failure modes: no gain for single-table transactions (l.229); QUOTED (l.426): OCC "has only a few hundreds of TPS (e.g. 341 TPS for 4 warehouses) as the abort rate is more than 99%" (the baseline's collapse, which is the case a switch would avoid). Bamboo reports IC3 underperforms when hot columns are shared (bam l.938).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. It is the "pay a fixed overhead to run parallel under contention" end of the range; at low contention it loses a few percent.

### P6. Guo, Wu, Yan, Yu, "Releasing Locks As Early As You Can: Reducing Contention of Hotspots by Violating Two-Phase Locking" (Bamboo), SIGMOD 2021. Copy read: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.09906 (extended version of the SIGMOD paper). Text: bam/paper.txt, 995 lines.
- Signal measured: per-transaction time spent waiting on its commit dependencies; the position of a write in the transaction. No global contention signal.
- How it is measured: QUOTED (l.293): "writes in the last delta (0 <= delta <= 1) fraction of accesses are not retired ... However, if a transaction turns out to spend significant time (i.e., longer than delta of the total execution time) waiting on the commit_semaphore, we will retire those write operations at the end of a transaction." (Greek letter not extracted; delta supplied by me from context.)
- Switching or control rule: QUOTED (l.232): "If the function is never called for all transactions, then Bamboo degenerates to Wound-Wait." QUOTED (l.288): "This allows a system to dynamically turn on/off the dirty read optimization of Bamboo based on the performance and frequency of cascading aborts." No threshold or policy for that switch is given in the text read.
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.76): TPC-C "up to 2x for stored procedures and 4x for interactive transactions compared to the best baseline (i.e., Wait-Die and Silo respectively)". QUOTED (l.774): "For cases of lower contention, Bamboo has 10% degradation in throughput compared with Wound-Wait due to overhead."
- Conditions of the experiment: in-memory DBx1000-style framework, up to 100+ threads (l.771), YCSB and TPC-C, stored-procedure and interactive modes.
- What must be true for it to apply: a few hot tuples, writes that finish early in the transaction (so retiring pays), cheap aborts.
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.771): "Bamboo underperforms SILO when the thread count more than 96" (lock thrashing); QUOTED (l.690): at one setting Bamboo beats Wound-Wait by 37% "although the abort rate of Bamboo is 72% higher"; cascading aborts waste work (l.73).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. The ability to fall back to the plain protocol per lock is the useful part; the paper leaves the trigger as a design hint, not a tested rule.

### P7. Lim, Kaminsky, Andersen, "Cicada: Dependably Fast Multi-Core In-Memory Transactions", SIGMOD 2017. Copy read: https://static.aminer.org/pdf/20170130/pdfs/sigmod/mrjyqdv2cligoojmnril3st7kcp5e0st.pdf (a conference copy hosted by AMiner). Text: cic/paper.txt, 1059 lines. Not a protocol switch but the one measured, adaptive control loop in this set.
- Signal measured: committed transactions per period, summed over all threads.
- How it is measured: QUOTED (l.267): "Each worker thread tracks the number of locally committed transactions. Periodically (5 ms in our implementation), the leader aggregates the number of committed transactions across all threads to obtain the throughput."
- Switching or control rule: QUOTED (l.267): "The leader uses hill climbing ... If the gradient (the throughput change divided by the maximum backoff time change) is positive, it increases the maximum backoff time by a fixed amount (0.5 us in our implementation); if the gradient is negative, it decreases the maximum backoff time by the same amount. If the gradient is zero or undefined ... it chooses a direction at random." Also a second switch (l.230): "Each thread adaptively omits both steps if the recent transactions have been committed (5 in a row in our implementation)."
- Measured effect: QUOTED (l.35): "Cicada achieves 3X and 1.37X higher throughput on contended TPC-C and YCSB workloads, respectively, than the next fastest scheme" (the whole system, not the backoff alone). For the backoff, QUOTED (l.850): "Cicada achieves nearly optimal performance under both workloads" against fixed settings (the curves are in a figure, not read).
- Conditions of the experiment: one 28-core machine, 28 threads, TPC-C, TPC-C-NP, YCSB (l.850).
- What must be true for it to apply: the throughput as a function of the setting has one peak. QUOTED (l.850): "Our experiments also showed the unique maximum throughput without local maxima, making contention regulation reliable."
- Failure modes: QUOTED (l.266): local backoff is "overly conservative under high abort rates"; QUOTED (l.850): "the optimal maximum backoff time is workload dependent, and it can be desirable to use small (often zero) maximum backoff time for the best throughput even though the abort rate may stay high." So a high abort rate alone is not a reason to back off (REASONED: do not use the rate of failures as the signal; use throughput).
- What it would mean for several processes on one machine: REASONED. Throughput of useful work, measured every few milliseconds and climbed by small fixed steps, needs no model of the bottleneck and follows a moving optimum, as long as the throughput curve is single-peaked.

## Across the papers (agreements, disagreements, gaps)
- Agree (QUOTED in P1, P2, P5, P6): no single protocol wins; OCC is best at low contention and collapses at high (IC3 l.426: 99% aborts at 4 warehouses); lock based wins at high contention and costs 6 to 15 percent at low (IC3 l.425; Bamboo l.774).
- Agree that the run-time signal can be a plain counter of failures or of commits (P1, P7). They disagree about what to count: aborts per page (MOCC) against throughput, with a warning that high aborts can be fine (Cicada l.850). REASONED: the two are compatible if the abort count only triggers a trial and the throughput decides.
- Disagree on who decides: offline analysis and a person (Tebaldi, IC3), offline learning (Polyjuice), or a run-time classifier (CormCC) or counter (MOCC).
- Switch cost is measured only in CormCC (P2) and Polyjuice (P3): 3 s to switch fully, no loss; mediated switch at least 93 percent. MOCC and Cicada have no switch to measure (per-access decision, a continuous parameter).
- Gaps: (1) none of the seven switches on a resource other than record conflicts (no CPU, memory or serial-step signal); (2) no direct measurement of oscillation near a threshold; (3) CormCC's feature list and classifier are in a technical report not read; (4) all experiments are single-machine or small clusters with one benchmark family, so what is untested is any workload that is not short transactions on records. A result here is a result about that form only.
