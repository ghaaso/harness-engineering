## A. Commutativity as the condition

### 1. Scalable commutativity rule
- Problem: which operation pairs can run on many cores without contending on shared memory.
- Rule, QUOTED, PDF p6 (L139): "Assume a specification S with a correct reference implementation M. Consider a history H = X ||Y where Y SIM-commutes in H, and where M can generate H. Then there exists a correct implementation m of S whose steps in the Y region of H are conflict-free." Plain form, p1 (L16): "whenever interface operations commute, they can be implemented in a way that scales."
- Commutes only sometimes, QUOTED, p2 (L22): SIM commutativity "is state-dependent and interface-based, as well as monotonic". p4 (L76): "two calls to open("a", O_CREAT|O_EXCL) often don't commute"; they do commute "if called from processes with different working directories".
- Limit, QUOTED, p7 (L209): "It does not show the converse, however: commutativity suffices for conflict-free accesses, but it may not be necessary." And p4 (L55): "the actual construction is not very practical".
- Not everywhere at once, QUOTED, p7 (L208): "no correct implementation can be conflict-free across all of H. In the end, a system designer must decide which situations involving commutative operations are most important".
- Result, QUOTED, p1 (L6): "Linux scales for 68% of the 13,664 tests generated by COMMUTER for these calls"; "sv6 scales for 99% of the tests." p2 (L28) and p12: sv6 conflict-free for 13,528 of 13,664, Linux 9,389. p11 (L327): "4,275 cases ... were not conflict-free" on Linux.
- Interface changes that make calls commute, QUOTED, p14 (L486): open "must allocate the lowest unused file descriptor"; so they do not commute; sv6 adds "an O_ANYFD flag". p13 (L454): ScaleFS "never reuses inode numbers" and generates them from a per-core counter.
- Measured on 80 cores, QUOTED, p14 (L483): "With the commutative fstatx, statbench scales perfectly and experiences zero L2 cache misses"; p14 (L485): with 80 cores, fstat "is 3.9x more expensive than on Linux" (cost of the scalable link count). p15 (L572): mail server, non-commutative operations "cause the benchmark's throughput to collapse at a small number of cores", commutative APIs reach "7.5x scalability from 1 socket (10 cores) to 8 sockets".
- Wins: interface design, where the interface can be changed to make operations commute. Loses: where the interface is fixed and its operations do not commute (the rule says nothing then).
- Needs: operations defined by an interface; commutativity judged per state and arguments, not per operation name; an implementation free to choose its data layout.
- Ref: Clements, Kaashoek, Zeldovich, Morris, Kohler, SOSP 2013; ACM TOCS 2015.
- Known: QUOTED (paper text).

### 2. Commutativity-based concurrency control
- Problem: let transactions on abstract data types run concurrently while staying serializable.
- How: the conflict relation between operations comes from whether they commute, using the results the operations return, not just reads and writes. Two algorithms (a locking one and a timestamp-style one) with recovery by intentions lists or undo logs.
- Guarantee: serializability of transactions (proved); a general correctness condition for pairing a recovery method with a conflict relation.
- Wins: types with rich operations (counters, sets, queues) where read/write locking blocks needlessly. Loses: operations that do not commute still block or abort.
- Needs: a typed object with declared operations and their commutativity, including result-dependence.
- Failure modes: the commutativity table must be right; a wrong entry breaks serializability. Measured numbers: figure not recalled.
- Ref: Weihl, IEEE Trans. Computers 37(12), 1988, pp. 1488-1505 (also MIT/LCS/TM-367).
- Known: SUMMARY (search of indexed abstracts).

## B. Several writers of one document

### 3. State-based and operation-based CRDTs
- Problem: replicas accept updates locally, with no coordination, and still agree.
- How: state-based: states form a join-semilattice, updates are monotone, merge is the least upper bound. Operation-based: concurrent operations commute, delivery is causal and exactly once.
- Sources read: INRIA RR-7506 (Jan 2011, long form) and RR-7687 (SSS 2011); page numbers are PDF pages.
- State-based condition, QUOTED, p13 (L327): "A state-based object whose payload takes its values in a semilattice, and where merge(x, y) = x ⊔ y, converges towards the LUB of the initial and updated values. If, furthermore, updates monotonically advance upwards ... it converges towards the LUB of the most recent values." Proposition 2.1 (p13, L331): "Any two object replicas of a CvRDT eventually converge, assuming the system transmits payload infinitely often between pairs of replicas over eventually-reliable point-to-point channels."
- Operation-based condition, QUOTED, p14 (L450): Proposition 2.2: "Any two replicas of a CmRDT eventually converge under reliable broadcast channels that deliver operations in delivery order <d."; concurrent operations must commute (Definition 2.6); "causal delivery ... does not require consensus".
- Cost, QUOTED, p41 (L1949): "CRDTs tend to become inefficient over time, as tombstones accumulate and internal data structures become unbalanced". p6 (L50): "Some of our designs suffer from unbounded growth; collecting the garbage requires a weak form of synchronisation". p42 (L2037): resetting payload, removing tombstones from a 2P-Set or rebalancing Treedoc "requires an atomic, unanimous agreement between all replicas, i.e., a commitment protocol such as 2-Phase Commit or Paxos Commit".
- What cannot be done locally, QUOTED, p20 (L660): a non-negative counter invariant "is a global invariant, which cannot be evaluated based on local information only ... two replicas at value 1 might still concurrently decrement, and the value converges to -1." p19 (L640): decrement of a max-merge counter "would violate monotonicity".
- SSS 2011 version (INRIA RR-7687, read too), QUOTED, p4 (L31): "Replicas of any CRDT are guaranteed to converge in a self-stabilising manner, despite any number of failures." p9 (L209) Theorem 2.1: "Assuming eventual delivery and termination, any state-based object that satisfies the monotonic semilattice property is SEC." p11 (L241) Theorem 2.2: "Assuming causal delivery of updates and method termination, any op-based object that satisfies the commutativity property for all concurrent updates ... is SEC." p6 (L55): "A strong equivalence between the two conditions." p12 (L257): "SEC is weaker than strong consistency".
- Wins: availability, offline work, no central server. Loses: data types where an invariant needs agreement (see 8); metadata can grow.
- Needs: a data type designed to be a CRDT; reliable (eventual) delivery; for op-based, causal delivery.
- Failure modes: the merge result is convergent, not necessarily what the user meant (see 6, 7); metadata growth (tombstones, version vectors); one commentary says "strong eventual consistency" adds convergence, not a stronger meaning of consistency.
- Ref: Shapiro, Preguica, Baquero, Zawirski, SSS 2011 (INRIA RR-7687).
- Known: QUOTED (paper text read in full).

### 4. Sequence CRDTs for text: Treedoc, Logoot, LSEQ, RGA
- Problem: concurrent inserts and deletes in an ordered list of characters or lines.
- How: Treedoc gives each atom a path in a tree and keeps tombstones for deleted atoms. Logoot gives each atom a dense position identifier made of tuples and needs no tombstones. LSEQ chooses identifier allocation to keep identifiers short. RGA links each insert to the atom it follows and orders concurrent inserts by timestamp.
- Result (SUMMARY): Treedoc's best case is a plain array; its authors report costs reasonable on real editing traces (figure not recalled). Logoot identifiers grow with editing. LSEQ slides: sub-linear identifier-size bound; a document with 1 million operations ending at 1 line can hold 499,999 tombstones (a tombstone example from the slides). A 2026 preprint says LSEQ still shows unbounded identifier growth under intense editing (qualitative).
- Wins: character-level concurrent editing without a server. Loses: memory and identifier growth; history needs compaction, which itself needs coordination.
- Needs: unique replica ids; causal delivery for the tombstone designs.
- Failure modes: see 5 (interleaving).
- Refs: Preguica and others (Treedoc) 2009; Weiss, Urso, Molli (Logoot) 2009; Nedelec and others (LSEQ) 2013; Roh and others (RGA) 2011. All four refs: TEXTBOOK (years from memory, not fetched).
- Known: SUMMARY for the results above; TEXTBOOK for refs.

### 5. Interleaving anomaly and Fugue
- Problem: two writers insert runs of text at the same place; the merged result mixes the characters.
- How: Kleppmann and others named the anomaly; Fugue and FugueMax are algorithms that minimise interleaving.
- Result (SUMMARY): Logoot and LSEQ show the anomaly; RGA avoids the main one (mechanised proof) but has a milder form; Fugue paper reports almost all surveyed CRDT and OT text algorithms suffer from interleaving. The 2019 paper's own errata page says its non-interleaving definition (section 2.1) cannot be met by any algorithm and its algorithm (section 3.1) does not guarantee convergence.
- Refs: Kleppmann, Gomes, Mulligan, Beresford, PaPoC 2019 (flawed, see errata); Weidner and Kleppmann, "The Art of the Fugue", arXiv 2305.00583 (2023).
- Known: SUMMARY (search).

### 6. Operational transformation (OT)
- Problem: concurrent edits to one document must converge and keep their intent when applied in different orders.
- How: each operation is transformed against the concurrent ones already applied, so it means the same thing in the new context. Origin: dOPT.
- dOPT, QUOTED, Cormack, Waterloo CS-95-08 (not CS-95-06), PDF p1: "dOPT is shown by counterexample to be incorrect. A corrected algorithm is given for a restricted environment based on point-to-point communication. There appears to be no simple correction to dOPT for a general environment based on broadcast communication."
- Conditions, QUOTED, Imine, Molli, Oster, Rusinowitch, ECSCW 2003, p4-5 (L55-58): transformation functions "must satisfy two conditions": C1 (same state either order) and C2 (transforming against a sequence does not depend on the order). Same as TP1 and TP2 of the first pass.
- Published functions fail, QUOTED, same paper, abstract: "we have verified the correctness of state-of-art transformation functions defined on Strings with surprising results." p6 (L77): "It is well known that these transformation functions are not correct"; the prover found a C1 counter-example "in a few seconds"; p7 (L91) and p9 (L131): further functions violate C2 with three concurrent operations. Suleiman's functions are called "over-specified" (p12, L201).
- Count, QUOTED, Boucheneb and Imine, INRIA RR-6510 (2008), abstract: "if the number of sites exceeds 2 then the convergence property is not satisfied for all OT algorithms considered here. A counterexample is provided for every algorithm." (Five IT algorithms; p22, L630.) This is a model-checking result on the algorithms they chose, not a count of all published ones.
- Ellis and Gibbs 1989 itself was not read (no open copy found).
- Wins: compact documents, no tombstones, intent-preserving for rich operations. Loses: transformation functions are hard to get right; many published algorithms were later found faulty (SUMMARY: dOPT; wider claim TEXTBOOK, see Oster and others 2006 for formal model checking, not fetched).
- Refs: Ellis and Gibbs, SIGMOD Record 18(2), 1989, pp. 399-407; Ressel, Nitsche-Ruhland, Gunzenhauser, CSCW 1996 (SUMMARY).
- Known: QUOTED for the counterexample results; Ellis and Gibbs unread.

### 7. How real-time editors keep many writers on one file
- Jupiter (Nichols and others, UIST 1995): one central server; each client talks only to the server, so transformation happens between two parties at a time. Google Wave followed Jupiter; one source says Google Docs also uses OT (SUMMARY, secondary). The server fixes a total order.
- Cost of that design (SUMMARY): needs a central gatekeeper; CRDT advocates cite this as its weakness.
- Sun and others, "Real Differences between OT and CRDT" (arXiv 1810.02137 and companions, 2018-2019): argue CRDT text solutions also follow a transformation approach, are not natively commutative for concurrent operations, and that OT is still the choice in most production co-editors. This is an authors' position paper, not a neutral benchmark (my note).
- Other editors (Etherpad, Figma, Yjs, Automerge): TEXTBOOK/uncertain, not verified here; details not recalled.
- Known: SUMMARY.

### 8. Replicated tree with move operation
- Problem: concurrent moves of subtrees must not create cycles or duplicates; common in file systems and JSON/XML models.
- How: a single move operation (timestamp, new parent, metadata, child); creation and deletion are moves; operations are applied in timestamp order, with undo and redo of later-ordered ones when a late one arrives; a move that would create a cycle is skipped.
- Guarantee, QUOTED, PDF p1 (L6): "ensuring that the tree structure remains valid (in particular, no cycles are introduced), and guaranteeing that all replicas converge towards the same consistent state. Our algorithm requires no synchronous coordination between replicas"; "we demonstrate bugs in Google Drive and Dropbox that arise with concurrent moves." Theorems proved in Isabelle (p8): unique parent, no cycles, convergence, strong eventual consistency.
- Conflict rule, QUOTED, p6 (L388): "If two operations would introduce a cycle when combined ... then the operation with the greater timestamp is ignored". The lower-timestamp operation wins; a move can be silently dropped.
- Why replicas cannot just check locally, QUOTED, p2 (L43): "different replicas may perform operations that are individually safe, but whose combination leads to a cycle."
- Cost, QUOTED, p1 (L25): "state machine replication has approximately four times higher throughput than our algorithm, but our algorithm has up to 100,000 times lower latency." p10 (L533): leader-based 14,000 move ops per second against the CRDT's 600 (Isabelle-generated code), 22,000 against 5,700 (hand-written). p10 (L527): at peak "200 undos and redos per remote operation". p10 (L535): local CRDT operations take "1-2 µs"; leader round trip 145-176 ms. Setup: three replicas, California, Ireland, Singapore.
- Memory, QUOTED, p7 (L402): "The algorithm ... retains operations in the log indefinitely, so the memory use grows without bound"; truncation needs causal stability (p7, L404: known replica set, FIFO links). Deletion has no fast path: p7 (L398) "Deletion operations must go through the undo-do-redo process".
- Ref: Kleppmann, Mulligan, Gomes, Beresford, IEEE TPDS 33(7), 2021/2022.
- Known: QUOTED (paper text read in full).

### 8b. JSON CRDT (Automerge's model)

## C. Theory of when no coordination is needed

### 9. CALM theorem
- Problem: when is coordination unavoidable.
- Statement, QUOTED, arXiv 1901.01930 PDF p3 (L119): "Theorem 1. Consistency As Logical Monotonicity (CALM). A program has a consistent, coordination-free distributed implementation if and only if it is monotonic." Definition, p3 (L111): "A program P is monotonic if for any input sets S,T where S ⊆ T, P(S) ⊆ P(T)."
- Why, QUOTED, p3 (L124): "Non-monotonic programs ... must be concerned that truth of a property could change in the face of new information. Therefore they cannot proceed until they know all information has arrived, requiring them to coordinate."
- Examples, QUOTED, p2 (L85, L91): deadlock detection needs no coordination: "decisions based on incomplete information are stable". Garbage collection does: "a decision based on incomplete information ... can be invalidated by the subsequent arrival of new information".
- Shopping cart, QUOTED, p4 (L157-158): additions and deletions kept as two growing sets commute, but "neither operation commutes with checkout"; so "monotonicity allows shopping to be coordination free, even though checkout still requires coordination."
- Measured, QUOTED, p1 (L19): "state-of-the-art multiprocessor key-value stores can spend 90% of their time waiting for coordination; a coordination-free implementation called Anna ran over two orders of magnitude faster".
- Limit, QUOTED, p5 (L189): "Bare assignment is a nonmonotonic programming construct ... This makes it extremely hard to take advantage of the CALM theorem to analyze systems written in traditional imperative languages!"
- Wins: a design test applied to a program, not a data type. Loses: monotonic is a strong test; says "needs coordination", not how cheap it can be.
- Needs: program expressed in logic where monotonicity can be judged; asynchronous messages, eventual delivery.
- Refs: Hellerstein and Alvaro, "Keeping CALM", arXiv 1901.01930 (2019; CACM venue not confirmed). Formal proof: Ameloot, Neven, Van den Bussche, 2013 (JACM; not fetched, TEXTBOOK). Conjecture: Hellerstein 2010 (TEXTBOOK).
- Known: QUOTED (paper text read in full).

### 10. Invariant confluence and coordination avoidance
- Problem: which application invariants can hold with no coordination between replicas.
- How: QUOTED, PDF p4 (L243 raw): "the I-confluence property informally ensures that divergent but I-valid database states can be merged into a valid database state". Analysis is per (invariant, operation) pair.
- Theorem, QUOTED, p5 (L280): "A globally I-valid system can execute a set of transactions T with coordination-freedom, transactional availability, convergence if and only if T is I-confluent with respect to I."
- Result, QUOTED, p1 (L20): "ten of twelve of TPC-C's invariants are I-confluent under the workload transactions"; "over 12.7M TPC-C New-Order transactions per second on 200 servers, a 25-fold improvement over prior results." p9 (L587): "over 12.74 million transactions per second on 200 servers".
- Not I-confluent, QUOTED, p8 (L439): "Two of TPC-C's invariants are not I-confluent with respect to the workload transactions and therefore do require coordination. On a per-district basis, order IDs should be assigned sequentially".
- Table 2, QUOTED, p6 (layout L384-399): Yes: attribute equality and inequality; uniqueness when the system "chooses some value"; foreign key insert; cascading delete; secondary index update; materialized view update; counter increment under ">" and decrement under "<"; "NOT CONTAINS" on sets, lists, maps. No: uniqueness when the user "chooses specific value"; AUTO_INCREMENT insert; foreign key delete; counter decrement under ">"; increment under "<"; SIZE= on sets, lists, maps.
- Uniqueness, QUOTED, p6 (L323) and p7 (L324): "uniqueness is not I-confluent for inserts of unique values"; "we can achieve uniqueness without coordination--as long as we have a notion of replica membership".
- Counter example, QUOTED, p6 (L334): a ">" threshold invariant "is I-confluent for counter increment and assign but not decrement"; so "concurrent salary increments but not concurrent salary decrements".
- Foreign key delete, QUOTED, p7 (L327): "Arbitrary deletion of records is unsafe: a user might be added to a department that was concurrently deleted".
- Benchmarks, QUOTED, p10 (L620): of the 14 non-TPC-C OLTP-Bench workloads, "nine of fourteen ... did not involve integrity constraints", one matched TPC-C, two needed unique ID assignment, two were research benchmarks.
- Microbenchmark, QUOTED, p3 (L134): coordination-free "over 12M transactions per second"; the lock-based version "approximately 1.1M transactions per second"; for distributed transactions locking throughput "drops by over three orders of magnitude".
- Limits, QUOTED, p6 (L291): "If invariants are incorrectly or incompletely specified, an I-confluent database system may violate application-level correctness."
- Wins: foreign-key inserts, replica-generated unique ids, counters moving the safe way, derived views. Loses: chosen-value uniqueness, sequential ids, deletes that others depend on, thresholds crossed by the unsafe direction.
- Needs: invariants stated explicitly; operations known in advance; a merge function (set union in their model).
- Ref: Bailis, Fekete, Franklin, Ghodsi, Hellerstein, Stoica, PVLDB 8(3), 2014/2015. Read in full text (page numbers are PDF pages, journal p185 = PDF p1).
- Known: QUOTED (paper text).

## D. Convergence and reconciliation

### 11. Eventual consistency and session guarantees
- Problem: replicas without coordination must at least converge; clients need sane views meanwhile.
- How: eventual consistency: if updates stop, replicas converge (Vogels 2009, CACM; TEXTBOOK). Session guarantees (read your writes, monotonic reads, writes follow reads, monotonic writes): Terry and others, PDIS 1994 (TEXTBOOK).
- Guarantee: convergence only after quiescence; says nothing about the converged value being right. Strong eventual consistency (3) adds determinism of the converged state.
- Failure modes: last-writer-wins silently drops a concurrent write (TEXTBOOK).
- Known: TEXTBOOK.

### 12. Bayou: application-specific conflict handling
- Problem: weakly connected replicas accept writes while apart; conflicts depend on the application.
- How (SUMMARY): every write carries a dependency check (a query plus the expected answer) and a merge procedure; if the check fails at a replica, the merge procedure runs, may rewrite the write (for example book another room) or record it for a person to fix. Writes are tentative until committed in a primary's order; replicas replay in that order (rollback and redo; TEXTBOOK).
- Guarantee: replicas applying the same writes in the same order reach the same state. No bound recalled.
- Wins: semantic conflicts that a generic rule cannot settle. Loses: the application author writes merge code per operation.
- Ref: Terry, Theimer, Petersen, Demers, Spreitzer, Hauser, SOSP 1995.
- Known: SUMMARY (search).

### 13. Dynamo: version vectors and application merge
- Problem: a store always writable even during failures.
- How (SUMMARY, partly third-party): sloppy quorum takes the first healthy nodes on the ring; vector clocks tag versions; if neither version dominates, the store returns both and the client merges (shopping cart: union).
- Result: paper reports at the 99.9th percentile (SLA basis); figures disagree between secondary sources (about 200 ms; sub-300 ms); not confirmed against the paper. Treat as figure not recalled.
- Failure modes: deleted items can reappear after a union merge (TEXTBOOK, from the paper's cart example); sloppy quorum loses the read-write overlap guarantee (SUMMARY).
- Ref: DeCandia and others, SOSP 2007.
- Known: SUMMARY (third-party), TEXTBOOK for the cart example.

## E. Merging versions of a file

### 14. Three-way merge (diff3)
- Problem: combine two edits of a common ancestor of a text file.
- How: diff each side against the ancestor, find regions where both changed, apply the non-overlapping changes, mark overlaps as conflicts.
- Abstract, QUOTED, PDF p1 (L5): "characterizing its good properties turns out to be rather delicate: a number of seemingly natural intuitions are incorrect in general."
- Holds, QUOTED, p7 (L118-119): a configuration is safe if "the O2 component contains an element x that occurs exactly once in each of O, A, and B"; "Theorem: Every safe tiling-respecting configuration (A O B) leads to a unique conflict-free synchronization." (The edits sit at the two ends; the middle region is untouched.)
- Fails, QUOTED, p7 (L115): the two obvious separation conditions (a long untouched middle; a middle that occurs nowhere else) are each not enough: "this guess is wrong on both counts." p9 (L131): generalised to several edited regions, "this generalization no longer ensures a conflict-free synchronization."
- Lacks, QUOTED, p9 (L136): "Diff3 is not idempotent." p10 (L214): "Diff3 does not guarantee near success on similar replicas." p11 (L413): "Diff3 is not stable, even for non-conflicting runs." In the paper's words (p2, L15) none of these properties holds in general.
- Wins: line-based text, edits in different places. Loses: moved or reordered text, adjacent edits, any edit whose meaning depends on code elsewhere (a clean merge can be wrong: TEXTBOOK, standard criticism).
- Needs: common ancestor; text as a list of lines.
- Ref: Khanna, Kunal, Pierce, FSTTCS 2007 (LNCS 4855), doi 10.1007/978-3-540-77050-3_40.
- Known: QUOTED (paper text read in full).

### 15. Patch theory (Darcs, categorical theory, Pijul)
- Problem: a formal account of changes so that merge is well defined and order-insensitive.
- How: Darcs: patches commute when order does not matter; conflicts handled by extra constructs (SUMMARY: "mergers, conflictors, graphictors" were proposals). Categorical theory: files are objects, patches are morphisms, merge of two patches with a common source is a pushout; a pushout need not exist when patches conflict, so the authors extend the category (free finite cocompletion) so conflicts become first-class objects. Pijul is commonly said to build on this (SUMMARY).
- Guarantee: merge is unique up to isomorphism when it exists; merge is independent of order (follows from the pushout definition; TEXTBOOK).
- Failure modes: Darcs had exponential-time merge cases (TEXTBOOK, well known; figure not recalled); a forum post argues the model of deletion makes the category a groupoid (one user's opinion, SUMMARY).
- Refs: Mimram and Di Giusto, "A Categorical Theory of Patches", arXiv 1311.3903 (2013); Roundy, Darcs, 2005 (TEXTBOOK).
- Known: SUMMARY (search).

### 16. Semistructured merge
- Problem: line merge conflicts where a structure-aware tool would not.
- How: parse classes into a tree down to method level; match nodes by name and kind; merge methods' bodies as text; so reordering of members is no conflict.
- Result, QUOTED, Apel and others FSE 2011, PDF p7 (L462): "Compared to unstructured merge, semistructured merge (including conflict handlers) can reduce the number of conflicts in 60 % of the sample merge scenarios by 34±21 % (the number of conflicting lines of code is reduced in 82 % of the sample merge scenarios by 61±22 %; the number of conflicting files is reduced in 72 % ... by 28±12 %)." Sample: 180 merge scenarios (p7, L463, "46 of 180").
- Where it does worse, QUOTED, p8 (L569) and p7 (L463): in 26 percent (46 of 180) of scenarios, "semistructured merge increases the number of conflicts by 109±170 %"; p9 (L581): "renaming poses a challenge for semistructured merge ... The point is that semistructured merge uses superimposition". p10 (L689): "The reason is that superimposition relies on nominal equality." p10 (L692): "the revisions must be syntactically correct". p9 (L582): a renamed directory gives "more conflicts but the overall number of conflicting lines is smaller".
- Combination, QUOTED, p10 (L696): "a combination of unstructured and semistructured merge can reduce the number of conflicts further (by 35 % compared to pure unstructured merge)."
- Later, QUOTED, Cavalcanti, Borba, Seibt, Apel (2019 study; 43,509 scenarios from 508 Java projects, p5 L64), abstract: "semistructured and structured merge differ in 24% of the scenarios with conflicts." Overall they differ in 0.58 percent (253) of all scenarios (p6, L93). Conflicts found: 4,732 semistructured, 4,793 structured (p6, L86); scenarios with conflicts 1,007 against 814 (p6, L90). False positives: semistructured in 36 scenarios, structured in 4; false negatives: structured in 39, semistructured in 5 (p2, L17). Why they differ (p7, L107): "when changes occur in overlapping text areas that correspond to different AST nodes". Caveat in the paper (p4, L52): "we do not have the ground truth"; false positives and negatives are judged by Travis CI builds, tests and manual reading.
- OOPSLA 2017, Cavalcanti, Borba, Accioly (read, QUOTED): 34,030 scenarios, 50 Java projects, p12 (L157): "19,238 conflicts when using unstructured merge, and 14,544 using semistructured merge. This represents a semistructured merge reduction of approximately 24%". p13 (L169): additional false positives 43.47 ± 19.01 % of unstructured conflicts against 30.21 ± 20.68 % semistructured; p16 (L211): additional false negatives 20.60 ± 21.30 % semistructured against 9.62 ± 16.29 % unstructured (an upper bound, "overestimated"). p3 (L34): "we find no evidence that semistructured merge leads to fewer false negatives". Improved tool s3m (p18, L353): "reduces the number of reported conflicts by half, has no additional false positives, has at least 8% fewer false negatives".
- Needs: a parser and a matching rule per language; syntax that parses.
- Failure modes: renames; code outside the parsed structure; false negatives mean a clean merge that is wrong.
- Ref: Apel, Liebig, Brandl, Lengauer, Kastner, "Semistructured Merge", ESEC/FSE 2011.
- Known: QUOTED (paper text read in full).

### 17. Structured merge (tree merge, JDime)
- Problem: same, with full abstract-syntax-tree matching.
- How: tree differencing then merge on the tree; JDime adds auto-tuning: cheap line merge first, switch to tree merge only where a conflict appears.
- Result, QUOTED, Apel, Leßenich, Lengauer, ASE 2012, PDF p1 (L7): "structured merge with auto-tuning is up to 12 times faster than purely structured merge, 5 times on average"; 8 Java projects, 72 merge scenarios, "over 17 million lines of code".
- Conflicts, QUOTED, p7 (L332): "On average, structured merge reports 39 % of the number of conflicts and 24 % of the number of conflicting lines of unstructured merge." Mostly "ordering conflicts". Auto-tuning misses little, p7 (L333): "Not many conflicts are being missed: up to 26 (in FreeCol) and 2 on average."
- Speed, QUOTED, p7 (L334): "Unstructured merge is up to 71 times faster than structured merge, 15 times on average."
- Mens' 90 percent claim, QUOTED, p7 (L336): "21 % of the changed files cannot be merged with unstructured merge--twice as many files as predicted by Mens. With structured merge, this fraction can be decreased to 15 %."
