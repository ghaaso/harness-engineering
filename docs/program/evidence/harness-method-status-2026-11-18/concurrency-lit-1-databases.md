## Part A. Schemes (field: name | problem | how | guarantee/measure | wins/loses | needs | failure modes | reference | how known, URL)

### 1. Multigranularity (hierarchical, intention) locking
- Problem: one lock size fits badly; a scan wants one lock on a table, a point update wants one on a row.
- How: locks on a tree (database, table, page, row). Before locking a node in S or X, take intention locks IS, IX (and SIX) on every ancestor. Compatibility matrix lets IS/IX coexist.
- Guarantee: with the protocol and 2PL, histories are serializable (TEXTBOOK). The paper has no lock-count measurement; it is an argument and a protocol.
- QUOTED (read in full: papers/gray2/paper.txt, an OCR scan with spacing errors, 2371 lines; https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/cs542-20Spr/readings/cc/granularity-locks.pdf). The argument (l.79-109): "The choice of lockable units presents a tradeoff between concurrency and overhead, which is related to the size or granularity of the units themselves. On the one hand, concurrency is increased if a fine lockable unit (for example a record or field) is chosen." A fine unit "would be costly for a 'complex' transaction which accesses a large number of records. Such a transaction would have to set and reset a large number of locks, incurring the computational overhead of many invocations of the lock subsystem, and the storage overhead of representing many locks." A coarse unit "discriminates against transactions which only want to lock one member of the file." "it would be desirable to have lockable units of different granularities coexisting in the same system."
- QUOTED, the intention lock (l.165-175): "intention mode (I), is introduced. Intention mode is used to 'tag' (lock) all ancestors of a node to be locked in share or exclusive mode. These tags signal the fact that locking is being done at a 'finer' level and thereby prevents implicit or explicit exclusive or share locks on the ancestors." "The protocol to lock a subtree rooted at node R in exclusive or share mode is to first lock all ancestors of R in intention mode and then to lock node R in exclusive or share mode."
- QUOTED, why intention locks coexist (l.218-226): "intention mode is compatible with itself since two transactions having intention access to a node will explicitly lock descendants of the node in X, S or I mode and thereby will either be compatible with one another or will be scheduled on the basis of their requests at the finer level." Later refined to IS, IX and SIX (l.232-260).
- QUOTED, the rules (l.395-402): "(a) Before requesting an S or IS lock on a node, all ancestor nodes of the requested node must be held in IX or IS mode by the requestor. (b) Before requesting an X, SIX or IX lock on a node, all ancestor nodes of the requested node must be held in SIX or IX mode by the requestor."
- Escalation: the word does not occur in this paper (searched); the failure mode in the next row is TEXTBOOK.
- Wins: mixed workloads of big scans and small updates. Loses: lock manager overhead on every path; escalation can block others.
- Needs: data that forms a containment hierarchy; transactions that declare access at the right level.
- Failure: lock escalation turns many row locks into one table lock and stalls concurrent writers; deadlocks between escalators (TEXTBOOK).
- Ref: Gray, Lorie, Putzolu, Traiger, "Granularity of locks and degrees of consistency in a shared data base", 1976 (IFIP); Gray and Reuter 1993.
- Known: TEXTBOOK. No URL fetched.

### 2. Two-phase locking (2PL), strict 2PL
- Problem: make interleaved transactions equal to some serial order.
- How: grow phase takes locks (S for read, X for write), shrink phase releases; no lock after first release. Strict 2PL holds X locks to commit.
- Guarantee: every 2PL history is conflict-serializable (Eswaran, Gray, Lorie, Traiger 1976). Strict 2PL also gives recoverability, no cascading aborts (TEXTBOOK).
- Wins: high contention on a few items, when a retry is costly: waiting beats redoing work (TEXTBOOK; Agrawal l.1195 QUOTED for the resource-limited case, B1). Loses: read-heavy workloads (lock traffic), long transactions (hold locks long), hotspots (queues).
- Needs: a lock manager all writers go through; writers that lock before they touch.
- Failure: deadlock; lock thrashing (more waiting than working as concurrency grows, Agrawal et al. below); convoys on hot items.
- Ref: Eswaran, Gray, Lorie, Traiger, "The notions of consistency and predicate locks in a database system", CACM 1976.
- Known: TEXTBOOK. No URL fetched.

### 3. Deadlock handling: detection, wait-die, wound-wait, no-wait
- Problem: 2PL transactions can wait in a cycle forever.
- How: detection builds a waits-for graph and aborts one victim per cycle. Wait-die: older waits for younger, younger requesting from older aborts ("dies") and restarts with its old timestamp. Wound-wait: older requesting from younger aborts ("wounds") the younger; younger waits for older. No-wait: abort on any conflict.
- Guarantee: wait-die and wound-wait are deadlock-free by construction and starvation-free, because a restarted transaction keeps its timestamp and so eventually is oldest (Rosenkrantz, Stearns, Lewis 1978; TEXTBOOK).
- Measured: see B2 (Yu l.385, l.330): at medium write contention NO_WAIT and WAIT_DIE scale past 512 cores and DL_DETECT thrashes at 256 cores; at the highest contention NO_WAIT "succumbs to lock thrashing" and OCC is best at 1024 cores. QUOTED there; the first-pass line was only partly right (see Corrections).
- Wins: prevention schemes when cycles are likely and detection cost is high. Loses: prevention aborts transactions that were not in a cycle (false aborts); detection costs a graph and a timeout or periodic scan.
- Needs: a total order of transaction age (timestamps); restartable transactions.
- Failure: "both NO_WAIT and WAIT_DIE have a high transaction abort rate" (QUOTED, Yu l.383); WAIT_DIE averaged 1.4 aborts per transaction at a 10% update rate (QUOTED, Harding l.254-255); detection latency leaves victims waiting (TEXTBOOK).
- Ref: Rosenkrantz, Stearns, Lewis, "System level concurrency control for distributed database systems", ACM TODS 1978.
- Known: TEXTBOOK for guarantees; measures QUOTED from Yu 2014 and Harding 2017 (Part B). Blog URL used by the first pass is no longer needed.

### 4. Optimistic concurrency control (OCC), backward and forward validation
- Problem: avoid lock cost and lock waits when conflicts are rare.
- How: three phases. Read: work on private copies, no locks. Validate: check against other transactions. Write: publish. Backward validation (Kung and Robinson): check the committing transaction's read set against write sets of transactions that committed during its life; abort self on overlap. Forward validation: check its write set against read sets of still-running transactions; abort either side.
- Guarantee: serializable in the order of transaction numbers (Kung and Robinson 1981, the three conditions are quoted below; QUOTED).
- Wins: low contention, read-mostly: QUOTED below (Kung l.56, l.215; Agrawal l.1195; Larson l.427). Loses: frequent conflicts: QUOTED in Part B (Yu l.383; Harding l.206 gives the opposite for OCC against 2PL and TO under update load). Long transactions hardest hit: TEXTBOOK, not found in the papers.
- Needs: private workspace per transaction; a read set and write set tracked per transaction; a cheap abort and rerun.
- Failure: starvation of long transactions (backward validation aborts the victim, never the winner); wasted work on abort; validation as a serial point (Kung and Robinson validate in a critical section).
- Ref: Kung and Robinson, "On optimistic methods for concurrency control", ACM TODS 6(2), 1981. Read in full: papers/kung/plain.txt (230 lines; line numbers below). https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~htk/publication/1981-tods-kung-robinson.pdf
- QUOTED, phases (l.58-59): "Writes are severely restricted. It is required that any transaction consist of two or three phases: a read phase, a validation phase, and a possible write phase". "During the read phase, all writes take place on local copies of the nodes to be modified. Then, if it can be established during the validation phase that the changes the transaction made will not cause a loss of integrity, the local copies are made global in the write phase."
- QUOTED, the validation conditions (l.126-130): "for each transaction Tj with transaction number t(j), and for all Ti with t(i) < t(j); one of the following three conditions must hold: (1) Ti completes its write phase before Tj starts its read phase. (2) The write set of Ti does not intersect the read set of Tj, and Ti completes its write phase before Tj starts its write phase. (3) The write set of Ti does not intersect the read set or the write set of Tj, and Ti completes its read phase before Tj completes its read phase."
- QUOTED, serial validation (l.151): "The simplest way to implement this is to place the assignment of a transaction number, validation, and the subsequent write phase all in a critical section."
- QUOTED, failure and starvation (l.149; l.214): "If the concurrency control detects a 'starving' transaction ... the transaction can be restarted, but without releasing the critical section semaphore. This is equivalent to write-locking the entire database". "The major difficulty in locking approaches is deadlock, which can be solved by using backup; in an optimistic approach, the major difficulty is starvation, which can be solved by using locking."
- QUOTED, when optimistic wins (l.56; l.215): "if the transaction pattern becomes query dominant (i.e., most transactions are read-only), then the concurrency control overhead becomes almost totally negligible". "These methods may well be superior to locking methods for systems where transaction conflict is highly unlikely. Examples include query-dominant systems and very large tree-structured indexes." And, stated as unsolved: "Some techniques are definitely needed for determining all instances where an optimistic approach is better than a locking approach".
- QUOTED, B-tree estimate (l.209): "if d = 3, m = 199, and n = 10^4, we have pc < 0.0007. Thus we see that it is very rare that one insertion would cause another concurrent insertion to restart for large B-trees." An analytic bound, not a measurement.

### 5. Timestamp ordering (TO) and the Thomas write rule
- Problem: serialize without locks by fixing the order at start.
- How: each transaction gets a timestamp; each item keeps the largest read and write timestamps. A read of an item written by a later timestamp aborts the reader; a write under a later read or write timestamp aborts the writer. Thomas write rule: a write older than the item's write timestamp, with no later reader, is skipped (obsolete), not aborted.
- Guarantee: histories are serializable in timestamp order (TEXTBOOK). Thomas rule gives view-serializable histories that plain TO would abort (Thomas 1979).
- Measured: QUOTED in B2: TIMESTAMP and OCC "copy tuples to perform a read" and are slow in a read-only run; timestamp allocation is the bottleneck at high core counts (Yu l.326-328).
- Wins: low contention, no lock manager. Loses: hot items cause abort storms; reads write timestamps (a read becomes a write to shared memory).
- Needs: a global timestamp source; restartable transactions; for recoverability, buffered writes or commit dependency.
- Failure: cascading aborts without extra care; livelock when restarts keep colliding (TEXTBOOK).
- Ref: Thomas, "A majority consensus approach to concurrency control for multiple copy databases", ACM TODS 1979; Bernstein and Goodman, "Concurrency control in distributed database systems", ACM Computing Surveys 1981.
- Known: TEXTBOOK; measures QUOTED in B2 and B3.

### 6. Multi-version concurrency control (MVCC)
- Problem: stop readers and writers blocking each other.
- How: each write makes a new version stamped with its writer; a reader sees the version valid at its timestamp. Old versions are garbage collected when no reader can need them.
- Guarantee: read-only transactions never block and never abort (TEXTBOOK). Multiversion TO is serializable (Bernstein and Goodman 1983, TEXTBOOK). Plain MVCC with snapshot reads is weaker than serializable (see 7).
- Measured: QUOTED in B5 (Larson): "The multiversion schemes have higher overhead but are much less sensitive to hotspots and the presence of long-running transactions"; figures in B5 (80X update throughput with long readers; 2M per second for 1V at low contention).
- Wins: long readers, read-heavy mixes, hotspots. Loses: update-heavy, storage and version-chain cost, GC pressure.
- Needs: version storage; a way to know the oldest active reader.
- Failure: long-running reader stops GC and bloats storage (TEXTBOOK). In Yu et al. MVCC bottlenecked on timestamps (QUOTED B2); in Harding "MVCC and TIMESTAMP have a steep performance degradation when theta reaches 0.5 because they block newer transactions" (QUOTED B3).
- Ref: Bernstein and Goodman, "Multiversion concurrency control: theory and algorithms", ACM TODS 1983; Reed's MIT thesis 1978.
- Known: TEXTBOOK; measures QUOTED in B2, B3, B5. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.0228

### 7. Snapshot isolation (SI) and write skew
- Problem: give each transaction a stable view without read locks.
- How: read from the snapshot at start; at commit, abort if another transaction that committed since start wrote the same item (first-committer-wins), or block via a write lock (first-writer-wins).
- Guarantee: avoids the ANSI anomalies (dirty and non-repeatable read, phantoms in the ANSI sense) but is NOT serializable. It admits write skew: two transactions read overlapping data, write disjoint items, and jointly break an invariant neither breaks alone (SUMMARY of Berenson et al. 1995).
- Wins: read-heavy, mostly disjoint writers; no read-write blocking. Loses: when an invariant spans items that different writers update (write skew), and under hot-item write conflicts (aborts).
- Needs: writers whose conflicts show up as overlapping write sets, or invariants confined to the items written. Otherwise add locks on reads or a stronger scheme.
- Failure: write skew; read-only transaction anomaly (Fekete, O'Neil, O'Neil 2004, TEXTBOOK-level recall).
- Ref: Berenson, Bernstein, Gray, Melton, O'Neil, O'Neil, "A critique of ANSI SQL isolation levels", SIGMOD 1995.
- QUOTED (Ports and Grittner l.36-55, read in full): "two concurrent transactions that read the same data, but modify disjoint sets of data" give write skew under snapshot isolation: "The write locks taken on update don't solve this problem, because the two transactions modify different rows and thus do not conflict." Berenson 1995 itself not read; summary row stays SUMMARY.

### 8. Serializable snapshot isolation (SSI)
- Problem: keep SI's non-blocking reads and also make it serializable.
- How: track read-write antidependencies between concurrent transactions. A non-serializable SI history contains a cycle with two adjacent rw edges (a "dangerous structure": T1 -rw-> T2 -rw-> T3). Abort a transaction in such a pattern. Detection is conservative: some aborts are false positives.
- Guarantee: serializable (theorem from Fekete et al. 2005, used by Cahill et al. 2008; TEXTBOOK). Cahill 2008 itself could not be opened: the University of Washington copy answered with a login page, the thesis copy (core.ac.uk) with an error page, the usyd repository exposed no file. Its figures (SmallBank in Berkeley DB, InnoDB) stay SUMMARY: "close to plain SI and significantly better than 2PL under contention" (a small model's text, not the page).
- QUOTED, from Ports and Grittner 2012 (read in full: papers/ports/plain.txt, https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol5/p1850_danrkports_vldb2012.pdf), the SSI cost in PostgreSQL 9.1: "our serializable mode has a performance cost of less than 7% relative to snapshot isolation, and outperforms two-phase locking significantly on some workloads." (l.23). "On this simple benchmark, tracking read dependencies has a CPU overhead of 10-20%." (l.573). "For the in-memory configuration (Figure 5a), SSI causes a 5% slowdown relative to snapshot isolation because of increased CPU usage." "SSI outperforms S2PL for all transaction mixes, and does so by a significant margin when the fraction of read-only transactions is high." On the disk-bound 150-warehouse run "the performance of SSI is indistinguishable from that of SI" and "the serialization failure rate was under 0.25%." (l.588.)
- QUOTED, why SSI aborts less than locking or OCC (Ports l.162-166): "it checks for a 'dangerous structure' of two adjacent rw-antidependency edges ... it may have false positives because not every dangerous structure is part of a cycle." "both S2PL and classic OCC prevent concurrent transactions from having rw-conflicts. SSI allows some rw-conflicts as long as they do not form a dangerous structure".
- Wins: read-heavy and read-only transactions. Loses: more aborts than SI; fine-grained locks give fewer false positives (InnoDB less than Berkeley DB, SUMMARY of a forum relay, not checked).
- Needs: tracking of reads (SIREAD marks) that outlive the reader; callers ready to retry aborts.
- Failure: false-positive aborts; a single lock on active-transaction state was a contention point in early PostgreSQL (SUMMARY).
- Ref: Cahill, Roehm, Fekete, "Serializable isolation for snapshot databases", SIGMOD 2008 (journal: ACM TODS 2009); Ports and Grittner, "Serializable snapshot isolation in PostgreSQL", PVLDB 5(12), 2012.
- Known: SUMMARY. https://arxiv.org/abs/1208.4179 ; https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/csep544/09sp/resources/fekete-sigmod2008.pdf (listed, not fetched).

### 9. Ordering transactions before they run (Calvin, deterministic execution)
- Problem: avoid distributed commit and distributed deadlock. How (TEXTBOOK, confirmed by l.89 below): a sequencing layer batches requests per epoch and fixes a global order; locks are granted in that order so execution is deterministic.
- Read in full: papers/calvin/plain.txt (349 lines; line numbers below). http://cs.yale.edu/homes/thomson/publications/calvin-sigmod12.pdf
- QUOTED, epochs (l.89): "Calvin divides time into 10-millisecond epochs during which every machine's sequencer component collects transaction requests from clients."
- QUOTED, TPC-C (l.200): "Without the agreement protocol, Calvin is able to achieve around 5000 transactions per second per node in clusters larger than 10 nodes, and scales linearly." "Our Calvin implementation is therefore able to achieve nearly half a million TPC-C transactions per second on a 100 node cluster." (New Order only; about 10 percent multi-warehouse, l.198.)
- QUOTED, needs known sets (l.120): "Transactions which must perform reads in order to determine their full read/write sets (which we term dependent transactions) are not natively supported in Calvin since Calvin's deterministic locking protocol requires advance knowledge of all transactions' read/write sets before transaction execution can begin." Fix: a reconnaissance query first, then "the read results must be rechecked, and the process [may] be (deterministically) restarted if the 'reconnoitered' read/write set is no longer valid."
- QUOTED, cost of ordering first, microbenchmark (l.247-262, l.296-300): "Under higher contention (contention index = 0.01, which is similar to TPC-C's contention level), we see a longer, more gradual per-node throughput degradation as machines are added". "under low contention, Calvin gets the same approximately 5x to 7x slowdown--from 27000 to about 5000 (4 nodes) or 4000 (8 nodes) transactions per second--as seen in the previous experiment going from 1 machine to 4 or 8." Cause named in the paper: "Execution progress skew", worse at high contention: "The higher the contention, the tighter this limit."
- QUOTED, the comparison is a model, not a measurement (l.298-304): "Therefore, the model does not establish a specific comparison point for our system, but a strong lower bound on the slowdown for such a system." The model assumes locks held "approximately 8ms on each distributed transaction" under two-phase commit and a bound of 1/(C x D_2PC) transactions per second at contention index C.
- QUOTED, replication latency (l.98-100): Figure 2 reports latency per replication mode; "Ping times between replicas ranged from 100 ms to 170 ms. Total transactional throughput was not affected by changing Calvin's replication mode." (Latency numbers are in the figure, not read.)
- Wins: contended, distributed workloads with known access sets. Loses: dependent transactions (the paper's own list above). Interactive transactions: the paper does not say (searched, no match). Harding et al. measured Calvin's losses (Sec B3: 36 percent drop with data-dependent aborts, flat 7k per second on PPS).
- Needs (TEXTBOOK): whole transaction submitted at once; declared or derivable read and write sets; deterministic code. Failure (TEXTBOOK): a wrong declared set; the epoch adds latency to every transaction (no figure read).
- Ref: Thomson, Diamond, Weng, Ren, Shao, Abadi, SIGMOD 2012.

### 10. Phantoms, predicate locks, index-range and next-key locking
- Problem: locking existing rows cannot stop another transaction inserting a row that matches a range just read.
- How: predicate locks (lock the condition; exact but costly to test) or, in practice, lock index key ranges. Next-key locking locks a key plus the gap before it, so inserts into a scanned range block (ARIES/KVL, Mohan 1990; ARIES/IM 1992).
- Guarantee: with range locks held to commit, no phantoms; serializable (TEXTBOOK). Figure not recalled.
- Wins: range scans under serializable. Loses: gap locks block inserts and raise deadlock rate; need an index on the predicate or the scan locks a whole table.
- Needs: an ordered index over the searched attribute.
- Failure: coarse locking when no index fits; deadlocks between inserters.
- Ref: Eswaran et al. 1976 (predicate locks); Mohan, "ARIES/KVL", VLDB 1990.
- Known: TEXTBOOK. No URL fetched.

### 11. Escrow transactions
- Problem: a hot aggregate (a balance, a stock count) serializes every writer under normal locking.
- How: instead of locking the value, a transaction reserves ("escrows") a quantity against a known lower or upper bound. The system keeps an interval [low, high] of possible values given uncommitted reservations. A request that keeps the bound true under all outcomes is granted at once; otherwise it waits.
- Guarantee: bound invariant (for example balance not below zero) holds under every commit or abort outcome, with concurrent updates, because operations commute within the bound (O'Neil 1986; TEXTBOOK).
- Wins: hot counters with commutative increment and decrement. Loses: reads of the exact value must wait or accept an interval; works only where the operation is a delta, not an overwrite.
- Needs: updates that are commutative deltas with a checkable bound; a log that can undo by inverse delta.
- Failure: non-commutative writes cannot use it; many outstanding reservations widen the interval and force waits (TEXTBOOK).
- Ref: O'Neil, "The escrow transactional method", ACM TODS 11(4), 1986.
- Known: TEXTBOOK. No URL fetched.

## Part B. Measured comparisons

### B1. Agrawal, Carey, Livny 1987
- Study: a closed queueing model comparing blocking (2PL), immediate restart, and optimistic control, varying resources and model assumptions. ACM TODS 12(4):609-654 (SIGMOD 1985 version also).
- Read in full: papers/agrawal/plain.txt (line numbers below). Three algorithms: dynamic 2PL ("blocking"), immediate-restart, optimistic. Simulated single-site database, closed queueing model; a "resource unit" is one CPU and two disks (l.108).
- QUOTED, aim (abstract): "rather than presenting 'yet another algorithm performance study,' we critically investigate the assumptions made in the models used in past studies and their implications." "We show that differences in the underlying assumptions explain the seemingly contradictory performance results."
- QUOTED, the crossover (conclusions, l.1195-1197): "Dynamic two-phase locking was found to outperform the immediate-restart and optimistic algorithms for medium to high levels of resource utilization. However, if resource utilizations are low enough so that a large amount of wasted resources can be tolerated, and in addition there are a large number of transactions available to execute, then a restart-oriented algorithm that allows a higher degree of concurrent execution is a better choice. We found the optimistic algorithm to perform the best of the three algorithms tested under these conditions."
- QUOTED, where the crossover fell (Exp 3, l.395-396): with 5 and 10 resource units "Blocking again provided the highest overall throughput", the maximum useful disk utilization for blocking, immediate-restart and optimistic being 72, 60, 58 percent (5 units) and 56, 45, 47 percent (10 units). "With 25 resource units, the maximum throughput obtained with the optimistic algorithm beats the maximum throughput obtained with blocking (although not by very much)." Disk use at that point: "34 and 30 percent" for blocking, "81 and 30 percent" for optimistic (total and useful). "with useful utilizations in the 30 percent range, the system begins to behave somewhat like it has infinite resources." At 50 units "maximum useful utilizations down in the range of 15 to 25 percent".
- QUOTED, interactive (l.650-652): "if utilizations are sufficiently low, a restart-oriented algorithm becomes a better choice. Such low resource utilizations arose in our experiments with large numbers of resource units and in our interactive workload experiments with large intratransaction think times. The optimistic algorithm provided the best performance in these cases." (think times of 1, 5, 10 s, 1 resource unit, l.646.)
- QUOTED, thrashing (l.74): "under low resource contention and a high level of multiprogramming, blocking may start thrashing before restarts do." Locking thrash is "due to the large increase in the number of times that a transaction is blocked".
- QUOTED, realism (l.1195-1197): "It is an open question whether or not such low utilizations will ever actually occur in real systems ... If not, blocking algorithms will remain the preferred method for database concurrency control." Also "the proper algorithm choice is strongly resource dependent."
- QUOTED, load control (l.1201-1203): "the level of multiprogramming in database systems should be carefully controlled"; "if the multiprogramming level is increased beyond a certain level, the blocking and optimistic concurrency control strategies start thrashing."
- Limit: 1987 simulation, 1 to 50 resource units; the paper gives no single conflict-rate number: the crossover is a resource level (useful disk use near 30 percent), not a conflict rate. URL: https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~jag/eecs584/papers/acl.pdf (read).

### B2. Yu, Bezerra, Pavlo, Devadas, Stonebraker 2014 ("Staring into the abyss")
- PVLDB 8(3):209-220. Seven schemes in a main-memory engine run in the Graphite simulator to 1024 cores: DL_DETECT, NO_WAIT, WAIT_DIE (2PL), TIMESTAMP, MVCC, OCC, H-STORE. Read in full: papers/yu/plain.txt. Line numbers below are of plain.txt (pdftotext without -layout; the -layout copy is two-column and unreadable).
- QUOTED (l.27): "Our analysis shows that all algorithms fail to scale to this magnitude but for different reasons."
- QUOTED (l.794): "we identified several bottlenecks to scalability: (1) lock thrashing, (2) preemptive aborts, (3) deadlocks, (4) timestamp allocation, and (5) memory-to-memory copying."
- QUOTED, read-only (l.326-328): "the T/O schemes ... timestamp allocation becomes the bottleneck with a large core count. OCC hits the bottleneck even earlier since it needs to allocate timestamps twice per transaction" and "Both OCC and TIMESTAMP have significantly worse performance than the other algorithms regardless of the number of cores. These algorithms waste cycles because they copy tuples to perform a read".
- QUOTED, thrashing, 2PL with fixed lock order (l.218): "When there is no skew in the workload (theta=0), the contention for locks is low and the throughput scales almost linearly." "With medium contention (theta=0.6), the throughput peaks at several hundred cores and then decreases due to thrashing. At the highest contention level (theta=0.8), the DBMS's throughput peaks at 16 cores and cannot scale beyond that."
- QUOTED, medium contention writes, theta 0.6 (l.330-383): "NO_WAIT and WAIT_DIE are the only 2PL schemes that scales past 512 cores. NO_WAIT scales better than WAIT_DIE." "DL_DETECT is inhibited by lock thrashing at 256 cores." "both NO_WAIT and WAIT_DIE have a high transaction abort rate." "OCC does not perform as well because it spends a large portion of its time aborting transactions; the overhead is worse since each transaction has to finish before the conflict is resolved."
- QUOTED, high contention, theta 0.8 (l.385): "almost all of the schemes are unable to scale to more than 64 cores." "NO_WAIT initially outperforms all the others, but then succumbs to lock thrashing". "Surprisingly, OCC performs the best on 1024 cores. This is because although a large number of transactions conflict and have to abort during the validation phase, one transaction is always allowed to commit."
- QUOTED, skew sweep at 64 cores (l.386): "for theta values less than 0.6, the contention has little effect on the performance. But for higher settings, there is a sudden drop in throughput that renders all algorithms nonscalable and approaches zero for values greater than 0.8."
- QUOTED, transaction length at 512 cores (l.472): "DL_DETECT and NO_WAIT have the best performance in this scenario [short transactions]"; "OCC performs the worst because it allocates double the number of timestamps as the other schemes for each transaction."
- QUOTED, read/write mix at 64 cores, theta 0.8 (l.517): "MVCC stand out as having the best performance when there are small number of write transactions." "TIMESTAMP and OCC do not perform well because they copy tuples for reading."
- QUOTED, partitioning (l.587, 635): "H-STORE outperforms all other schemes up to 800 cores" (single-partition only, uniform access); "The DBMS does not scale with transactions accessing four or more partitions because of the reduced parallelism and increased cross-core communication." (10 percent multi-partition, 1024 cores in Fig 15b).
- QUOTED, TPC-C with 4 warehouses (l.644): "all of the schemes fail to scale for TPC-C when there are fewer warehouses than cores." "they are able to scale up to 64 cores for the NewOrder transaction. TIMESTAMP, MVCC, and OCC have worse scalability due to high abort rates."
- Limit: simulation, in-memory, transactions without think time; no absolute throughput is stated in the text (it is in the figures, not read as numbers). PDF: https://people.csail.mit.edu/devadas/pubs/1000cores.pdf (read).

### B3. Harding, Van Aken, Pavlo, Stonebraker 2017
- PVLDB 10(5):553-564. Read in full: papers/harding/plain.txt (line numbers below). Deneva framework on EC2 (m4.2xlarge, 8 cores each, 1 ms RTT), six protocols: NO_WAIT, WAIT_DIE, TIMESTAMP, MVCC, OCC, CALVIN (deterministic); YCSB, TPC-C, PPS.
- QUOTED, contention, 16 servers (Sec 4.2, l.205-206): "the protocols' throughput is relatively unaffected by skew for theta values up to 0.5. After this point, most of them decline in performance but at different rates. Once theta reaches 0.8, all but one of them converge to the same low performance. CALVIN is the only protocol to maintain good performance despite high skew."
- QUOTED, OCC (l.206): "OCC performs worse than the other non-deterministic protocols under low contention due to the overheads of validation and copying during transaction execution [60]. At higher levels of contention, however, the benefit of tolerating more conflicts and thus avoiding unnecessary aborts outweighs these overheads."
- QUOTED, update rate at theta 0.6 (Sec 4.3, l.254-255): "MVCC and TIMESTAMP diverge, and by 100% update transactions perform at 33% of the throughput of NO_WAIT." "When 100% of the transactions are updates, NO_WAIT performs 54% better than the next-best non-deterministic algorithm (OCC)." "at a 10% update rate, there is an average of 1.4 aborts per transaction" (WAIT_DIE).
- QUOTED, conclusion on choosing (Sec 5.1, l.617-618): "when there were few updates using a protocol with minimal overhead, such as 2PL or a timestamp protocol, made the most sense. These protocols quickly degraded as the number of writes increased and past a certain threshold using an optimistic or deterministic protocol was a better choice." Also "Optimistic concurrency control is slightly more resilient to high skew, but once the skew reaches a critical point, CALVIN emerges as the most consistent protocol."
- QUOTED, multi-partition (Sec 4.4, l.296): "With the exception of CALVIN, the protocols' throughput plummets when transactions touch more than one partition. From two to four partitions, performance drops by 12-40%."
- QUOTED, scaling (Sec 4.5, l.315): "At 64 servers, the protocols all improve their single-server performance by 1.7-3.8x." "NO_WAIT has the best overall performance for all numbers of servers." On the medium-contention run OCC "outperforms all of the protocols with the exception of NO_WAIT and CALVIN when there is more than one server as the benefit of being able to tolerate more conflicts exceeds the costs of copying and validation."
- QUOTED, data-dependent aborts (Sec 4.7, l.596): "CALVIN experienced a 36% decrease in throughput when the workload was run on 16 servers with medium contention (theta=0.6, 50% update transactions)"; "CALVIN's throughput declines from 73k to 19k transactions per second" as theta goes 0.8 to 0.9.
- QUOTED, PPS (Sec 4.9, l.611): "CALVIN has a flat-lined throughput of about 7k transactions per second", because of reconnaissance queries. "OCC's abort rate is 15%, which is as high as NO_WAIT. Unlike NO_WAIT, however, OCC must roll back the entire completed transaction".
- QUOTED, conclusion (l.761): "distributed transactions on a cluster often only exceed the throughput of non-distributed transactions on a single machine by small amounts."
- Limit: EC2, in-memory, one datacenter. PDF: https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol10/p553-harding.pdf (read).

### B4. Silo, Tu, Zheng, Kohler, Liskov, Madden 2013
- SOSP 2013. Read in full: papers/silo/plain.txt (line numbers below).
- QUOTED, abstract (l.5): "Silo's key contribution is a commit protocol based on optimistic concurrency control that provides serializability while avoiding all shared-memory writes for records that were only read." "Silo achieves almost 700,000 transactions per second on a standard TPC-C workload mix on a 32-core machine, as well as near-linear scalability."
- QUOTED (l.19): "Per-core transaction throughput at 32 cores is 91% of that at 8 cores" and "benchmarks on our hardware with a commercial main-memory database system perform at most 3,000 transactions per second per core." (Silo: about 22,000 per core.)
- QUOTED, model limit (l.21): "Silo assumes a one-shot request model in which all parameters for each client request are available at the start ... If high-latency client communication were part of a transaction, the likelihood of abort due to concurrent updates would grow."
- QUOTED, cost of one shared counter (l.275): "MemSilo+GlobalTID ... Here we see a scalability collapse after 24 workers; Key-Value outperforms MemSilo+GlobalTID by 1.80x at 32 workers." Overhead of read/write tracking (l.274): "Key-Value outperforms MemSilo by a maximum of 1.07x."
- QUOTED, partitioned store against OCC (l.434-436): "Partitioned-Store outperforms MemSilo by 1.54x at no cross-partition transactions. However, performance suffers as soon as cross-partition transactions are introduced. At roughly 20%, the throughput of Partitioned-Store drops below MemSilo ... At roughly 60% cross-partition transactions, MemSilo outperforms Partitioned-Store by 2.98x." "While Silo's OCC protocol initially pays a nontrivial overhead for tracking record-level changes in low contention regimes, this work pays off as the contention increases."
- QUOTED, hot counter (l.439): with one hot counter per district, "As we add more workers, we increase the number of read-write conflicts over this shared counter, which increases the number of aborted transactions. Note that this is not unique to OCC; in 2PL, conflicting workers would serialize around write locks held on the shared counter." Result: "MemSilo outperforms Partitioned-Store by a maximum of 8.20x at 24 workers"; with the counter taken out of the transaction, "17.21x at 32 workers" (l.440).
- QUOTED, snapshots (l.451-458): "MemSilo outperforms MemSilo+NoSS by 1.19x. This is due to the larger number of aborts that occur when a large read-only transaction executes with concurrent modifications (recall that snapshot transactions never abort)." Space for old versions: "a 3.4% increase in memory for snapshots".
- Silo has no direct 2PL-versus-OCC curve under heavy write contention; the paper's contended test is against partition locks, not against record locking. URL: https://people.csail.mit.edu/stephentu/papers/silo.pdf (read).

### B5. Hekaton / main-memory MVCC, Larson et al. 2012 (PVLDB 5(4):298-309) and Diaconu et al. 2013
- Read in full: papers/larson/plain.txt (arXiv 1201.0228, the VLDB 2011/12 paper; line numbers below). Schemes: 1V (single-version locking), MV/O (optimistic multiversion), MV/L (pessimistic, locking multiversion). Server with 2 NUMA nodes, 12 cores, HyperThreading.
- QUOTED, abstract (l.7): "single-version locking works well when transactions are short and contention is low performance degrades under more demanding conditions. The multiversion schemes have higher overhead but are much less sensitive to hotspots and the presence of long-running transactions."
- QUOTED, low contention, 10M rows, 10 reads and 2 writes (l.425): for 1V "the system still continues to scale linearly, reaching a maximum of over 2M transactions/sec. The multiversion schemes have lower throughput because of the overhead of version management and garbage collection." "MV/L has 30% lower performance than MV/O."
- QUOTED, hotspot, 1,000 rows (l.427): "all schemes achieve a throughput of over a million transactions per second, with MV/O slightly ahead of both locking schemes. 1V achieves its highest throughput at six threads, then drops and stays flat after 8 threads."
- QUOTED, isolation level cost at 24 threads (Table 3 text, l.462): "The overhead for Repeatable Read for both locking schemes is very small, less than 2%. MV/O needs to repeat the reads at the end of the transaction, and this causes an 8% drop in throughput." "Both MV schemes achieve serializability at a cost of 10%- 19% lower throughput". 1V serializable cost 2 percent.
- QUOTED, read-only share (l.470): "The MVCC schemes have a clear advantage at high contention, as snapshot isolation prevents read-only transactions from interfering with writers. When 80% of the transactions are read-only, the MVCC schemes achieve 63% and 73% higher throughput than 1V." (1,000-row table.)
- QUOTED, long readers, 10 percent of a 10M-row table per reader (l.477-480): "1V is twice as fast as the MV schemes when all transactions are short update transactions ... At x=1, update throughput drops 75% for the single version engine. In contrast, update throughput drops only 5% for the MV schemes, making MV twice as fast as 1V." "When 50% of the active transactions are long readers, at x=12, MV has 80X higher update throughput than 1V."
- QUOTED, conclusions (l.512-514): "Single-version locking is fragile; it performs well when transactions are short and contention is low but suffers under more demanding conditions." "The optimistic MVCC scheme consistently achieves higher throughput than the pessimistic scheme." Related work (l.507): "Whether their approach [Cahill et al.] can be implemented efficiently for a main-memory DBMS is an open question."
- Diaconu et al. 2013 (Hekaton in SQL Server): not obtained. URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.0228 (read).

### B6. Where optimistic control wins and where locking wins (all QUOTED above; the rows to compare)
- Locking wins: Agrawal (resource-limited: blocking best at 1, 5 and 10 resource units, where its maximum useful disk use was 72 and 56 percent at 5 and 10 units); Yu at medium contention (NO_WAIT and WAIT_DIE scale past 512 cores, OCC "spends a large portion of its time aborting"); Harding under update load ("NO_WAIT performs 54% better than the next-best non-deterministic algorithm (OCC)" at 100 percent updates, theta 0.6); Larson at low contention (1V over 2M per second, MV/L 30 percent below MV/O).
- Optimistic wins: Agrawal (25 units: "beats ... (although not by very much)"; 50 units and 1 to 10 s think time: "The optimistic algorithm provided the best performance"); Yu at theta 0.8 on 1024 cores ("OCC performs the best", because "one transaction is always allowed to commit"); Harding under skew ("the benefit of tolerating more conflicts ... outweighs these overheads", OCC second only to NO_WAIT and CALVIN beyond one server at theta 0.6); Larson (MV/O "consistently achieves higher throughput than the pessimistic scheme"); Silo (OCC beats partition locks above 20 percent cross-partition transactions); Kung (conflict "highly unlikely").
- The papers disagree on a single crossover: Yu and Harding report OCC better under skew, Agrawal and Yu (medium) report locking better when resources are limited and conflicts frequent. Agrawal's explanation: the choice "is strongly resource dependent" (l.1197). No paper gives one conflict-rate number at which they cross; the nearest are Yu's theta 0.6 to 0.8 band at 64 cores (all schemes collapse above 0.8) and Agrawal's useful disk use near 30 percent.

## Corrections against the first pass
- Yu: the row said "best 2PL spends about 30 percent of cycles on useful work" and "detection thrashes beyond 16 cores". Neither is in the paper's text. What it says: DL_DETECT is "inhibited by lock thrashing at 256 cores" (medium contention); "peaks at 16 cores" is the fixed-order 2PL at theta 0.8 (l.218). Not found: any 30 percent figure.
- Yu: the row said "read-only: 2PL schemes scale better than TO-family". True, but the paper's reason is timestamp allocation and tuple copying (l.326-328). "None scales beyond about 64 cores" is right for high contention, but "OCC performs the best on 1024 cores" there is omitted in the first pass and reverses its OCC-loses line.
- Harding: the row listed "2PL (no-wait, wait-die), timestamp, MVCC, OCC and a deterministic one". Right. It lacked the finding that OCC beats the other non-deterministic protocols at higher contention and that CALVIN alone holds up at theta 0.8 and above (l.205-206).
- Agrawal: the row said "Blocking tends to win when resource use is high; optimistic wins when resources are plentiful". Right in outline; the paper adds three-way results (immediate-restart is the third scheme) and the figures: crossover between 10 and 25 resource units, useful disk use near 30 percent.
- Silo: no correction of the abstract figures (700,000 per second, 3,000 per core confirmed). The first pass said Silo showed OCC scaling "where conflicts are low"; the paper also tests contention, and OCC wins there against partition locks (l.434-440).
- Larson: first pass said "one secondary source says optimistic MVCC beat pessimistic". Confirmed by the paper (l.514). It also said "no figures": the paper has them (B5).
- Calvin: the first pass said interactive transactions are unsupported. The paper's text does not say so. Its own limit is dependent transactions (l.120). The 10 ms epoch is confirmed (l.89).
- SSI: the first pass gave "about 5 percent, 2 percent, one 20 percent case" from list posts. The paper says under 7 percent overall and 10 to 20 percent CPU on a simple benchmark (Ports l.23, l.573). Cahill 2008 still unread.
