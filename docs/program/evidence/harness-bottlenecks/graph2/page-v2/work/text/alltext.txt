This page shows the design of the second code-and-document graph as a system: what the agents actually look up (measured on 1,452 recorded transcripts: session logs, subagents included), the model derived from those questions, one lookup stepped through on real data, the loop that keeps cached conclusions honest, where each part comes from, what was cut, and how small the prize may be. The judge's rulings on the refuter's attack (R-1 to R-8) are applied throughout and shown as the current state.

Buys:
one node for things that join more than two parts (a change set, a rule with roles, a claim with its premises); re-checking a cached answer against the facts it rests on: 0 % of served answers wrong at fact level (judged against the extractor's own facts) against 6.5 % at file level, on 29,097 replayed lookups.

a graph database for speed (milliseconds at 5,464 nodes), reachability indexes, or embeddings and graph neural models (about 6.9 facts per entity here against about 37; measured on this graph (report of 2026-11-16): No arm adds to the simple ranker on this repository; the largest gain fused with the simple ranker, in top-ten precision inside the candidate pool, is +0.0023 [−0.0002, +0.0049] (RotatE), which "matches" it (within 0.02), where "adds" needed at least +0.02).

What this measurement could not measure (10)
Run-to-run drift: the untracked program documents are read at their live content when baseline.js.txt runs, and other sessions keep writing them, so a re-run can move a baseline cell by an instance or two (seen once: 29.6 to 29.5 %).
Containment of the whole needed span: a baseline "hit" means a result points into a truth span (overlap), not that the result covers all of it.
Which lines of a read the agent actually used: confirmation covers only later edits, file:line citations and verbatim quotes; about 9 % of model calls carry visible text (use-cases.md section 11), so "read but not confirmed" is not "unused".
The exact code version: transcripts carry no commit; the version is the branch tip at the episode time (main when the branch no longer exists), and uncommitted working-tree changes are invisible.
Program documents outside version control: they can only be indexed at their current content, which may hold text written after the session (counted per form as needs-current-docs, and per baseline as hits on current-only files).
Truth outside the repository (scratchpad files, transcripts, memory, web): no repository index can return it; these instances are counted, not scored.
Whether an agent given the index result would have stopped reading: the baselines score containment of the truth span among the first 10 results and its token cost, not the agent behaviour.
Program question forms are labelled by rules that a manual check found right on about 3 in 4 calls (use-cases.md section 2.4); "earlier lane findings" (the question “earlier conclusions”) is the noisiest label.
Confirmation-part numbers: the baselines were computed on the development part only, by design; nothing on the confirmation part has been scored.
Product work is 13 main sessions of one day (2026-11-13) and one plan; the development part holds 8 of them and the confirmation part 5. A per-form product number rests on few sessions.
What it shows.
Documents, rules, the plan and earlier conclusions carry most lookup cost; 55 % of product lookup cost is spent before the first edit, much of it the same orientation reads repeated. The form the first test scored is 1.8 % of product cost. Measured since: the test set holds 4,829 recorded lookups; 27.1 % have a span a later edit, citation or quote used, and 10.6 % replay against the frozen commit with no leak.

How to read it.
The loop is the mechanism. Below it, the replay: 250 deterministic questions looked up again at each later saved snapshot (29,097 lookups over 190 snapshots); an answer was reused only if the rule said it still held, and the true answer was recomputed every time to count wrong reuses. Which sense of “temporal knowledge graph”.
In the representation sense, facts carry validity over time, here over the branching commit history for code and over content hashes for documents; in that representation sense graph v2 counts as a temporal knowledge graph. It is not one in the forecasting or completion sense: it predicts no future or missing facts.

On the loop, step 1 → reuse: are recorded findings read again?
43 of 56 sampled findings that appear in the transcripts were shown again in another session, 27 in two or more; median gap 57 hours. 94 of 2,245 files were read in two or more sessions, carrying 9.9 % of the carried tokens. An exposure shows the text was shown, not that it was used.
On the loop, steps 2 to 4 → stale: did a changed premise matter?
Of 8 findings traced through the git history, 2 had gone stale and 1 drifted. The stale ones were shown again 6 times after the change, in 5 session-finding pairs. Times the stale sentence was written into a file afterwards: 0. Whether a decision rested on it was not established.
What this changes in the design
Can keep honest (about 12 to 20 % of these findings):
query-plus-answer findings about this repository (seed values, config defaults, build profiles, call sites), and summaries of tracked spans whose span hash can be re-checked (three of the sampled findings). Two of the seven query findings went stale within two weeks; a ledger re-running "default of the ping backdate ceiling setting" and "does login compare a PIN with a default" at the new commit would have flagged both, and both still sit unflagged in tracked documents.
Could keep honest if written differently (up to about half):
script results (M, 25 %) and findings citing untracked notes, scratch files or clinic files. With every cited file content-hashed at citation time, 53 % (41 to 65 %) would have only file or query premises (the change detected, not the conclusion re-derived).
Cannot:
findings resting on outside sources (25 %), on one-off measurements whose inputs are gone (22 %), and the judgement layer (63 % carry some bare judgement; 68 % are interpretation). For these a ledger can store the date, author and premises, and show their age; it cannot say the conclusion still holds.
What recording premises at writing time would have required:
about 3 to 4 premise records per finding (median 3, up to 10). In this sample a writer would have had to record, per 100 premises: 6 repository queries, 11 tracked spans with commit, 14 plus 10 plus 8 file spans needing a content hash (repository folder, scratch, clinic), 11 plus 1 clinic git facts, 2 pinned library spans, 12 outside-source citations with fetch date, 7 measurement inputs that were not kept, and 18 judgements to be marked as such.
What would have to change in how findings are written:
put the query in words or code and the commit beside each code claim; keep measurement inputs and outputs under content hashes (or commit them) instead of in scratch; commit or hash the evidence notes a finding cites; mark judgement as judgement; and give each finding an id the ledger can index. None of this was measured as a cost; it is what the sample says is missing.
Not claimed.
Not claimed here: any token saving, or that a ledger would have prevented a wrong action.

The state of an untracked file when a finding was written (judged by where a copy sits now).

What it shows.
Re-checking the facts an answer rests on, including the absence of other matches, served 87.6 % of lookups with no wrong answer in this replay, where file-level invalidation served wrong answers 6.5 % of the time. This holds for deterministic answers; for model-made conclusions it holds only as far as their premises were recorded. Measured for earlier conclusions: 67.8 % of their carried read tokens come from scratch folders outside the repository and 4.1 % from tracked files; 12 of 60 sampled findings have all their checkable premises tracked.

Do learned graph embeddings add to the simple ranker?
No arm adds to the simple ranker on this repository.
Four knowledge-graph embedding models and one graph-neural encoder were each fused with the simple ranker and compared with it on the 50 later tuning commits. On top-ten precision every fused arm "matches" it: the largest gain is +0.0023 inside the candidate pool and +0.0032 over all code files, where the protocol asked for at least +0.02 before it would say "adds". A real gain of up to about +0.006 in the pool, and about +0.007 over all code files, cannot be ruled out; that is about a third of the bar. Alone, RotatE and TransE match the simple ranker. ComplEx-N3 and CompGCN match inside the candidate pool and are lower over all code files. The routed specialists are lower in both.
Why, from the audit: the models learn well, but mostly they recover the co-change facts they were trained on, which the simple ranker already has. Below those they order the other candidates about as well as rooted PageRank does. Fused with the simple ranker they add no gain that the protocol's +0.02 bar could register; a small one, of about +0.006 in the pool at most, is not ruled out.

How to read it.
Each row is one arm: the line is its 95 % interval, the dot its difference to the simple ranker in top-ten precision; the shaded band is −0.02 to +0.02, the dashed line the bar of +0.02. An arm "adds" when its interval lies above zero and its gain reaches the bar; it "matches" when the whole interval lies inside the band, which means within 0.02, not "no difference"; "lower" when the interval lies wholly below zero.

Why: six reasons from the audit
Points 2 to 5 come from the audit's refuter, who set out to show that "matches" is an artifact. Point 1 comes from the auditors of stage A and of the arms' code, point 6 from the auditor of the routed specialists. A second agent re-tested points 2, 5 and 6 and corrected them; the corrected forms are given here. Point 1's figures are the lane's stage-A table, with a caveat from two auditors; points 3 and 4 rest on one agent's computation.
The models learn.
On stage A's held-out co-change pairs RotatE, ComplEx-N3, TransE and CompGCN reach a mean reciprocal rank of 0.68 to 0.75, against 0.28 for rooted PageRank. Rooted PageRank's graph has no co-change edges, so this shows that the models learned the co-change structure, not that they beat a ranker that has it.
Mostly they recover what they were trained on.
Alone, RotatE, ComplEx-N3, TransE and CompGCN separate a file's known co-change partners from the other candidates almost perfectly (AUC 0.996 to 1.000). Inside that block they do not follow co-change confidence's order (median rank correlation 0.07 to 0.29). Below it their order is moderately correlated with rooted PageRank's (0.49 to 0.63 in the pool) and about as accurate: top-ten precision on candidates without co-change history is 0.196 to 0.213, against rooted PageRank's 0.208. It is not the same order.
They find little that is new.
On those candidates no arm beats rooted PageRank by a margin this test can see. The refuter's own contrast, with the truth restricted in the same way, gives RotatE +0.004 [-0.005, +0.012] and TransE +0.001; ComplEx-N3, CompGCN and the routed specialists score below rooted PageRank. The data do not show that the embeddings carry no information of their own; they show that it is too little to move the top ten.
More weight does not help.
Outside the protocol's grid (weights 2, 4 and 8, and the arm alone) no arm's gain over the simple ranker exceeds +0.006 in the pool. The room was there: a perfect order would gain +0.119 in the pool.
In the pool the fused list can hardly differ from the simple ranker's.
There are two causes. The fusion the protocol fixed (k = 60) keeps a candidate with co-change history above one without: in the fused tables the co-change candidates hold the top places in 224 to 226 of the 226 tasks where that can be tested. And the selection gave low weights to arms that added nothing. So the fused top ten shares 9.1 to 9.3 of its 10 files with the simple ranker's, and the placebo moves the list as much as the real models do. "Adds" was still reachable: a perfect score put through the same selection takes weight 1 and gains +0.060 [+0.042, +0.079], and a half-informative one +0.036. But that is the most the primary contrast can show, so "matches" rules out a third to a half of the gain that could be reached, not all of it. The pool "matches" is a weak comparison; the arms alone, the unrestricted lists and the weights beyond the grid (points 2 to 4) carry the conclusion.
The routed specialists.
The routed specialists' shortfall alone lies entirely in the 192 tasks that were routed to two areas. On the 121 tasks routed to one area the routed specialists equals RotatE (0.2226 against 0.2219). About 60 % of the routed specialists' gap to RotatE on the two-area tasks comes from the merge the protocol wrote (item 13): a candidate that only the second specialist knows enters with that one percentile and takes top-ten places at about 5 % precision. The rest is not explained by this test. Candidates that no specialist knows explain at most 0.008 (the bound comes from the re-test of the arms auditor's finding).
The sentence the evidence supports:
on this repository, at this size, the trained embeddings mostly recover the co-change facts they were trained on; below those they order candidates about as well as rooted PageRank, though not in the same order; and fused with the simple ranker they add nothing this test's bar could register (at most about +0.006 in pool top-ten precision, where the protocol asked for +0.02).

How to read it.
The judge's eight rulings first: they are the current state of the design. Then every finding, grouped by kind, with the ruling that answers it beside it. The refuter also tested 52 claims of the design one by one (the bar; the list is folded).
R-1 · Honest source labels
The refuter is right that not every choice has a source yet. Each component is shown with one of four honest labels: "from a named source", "simplified from a named source" (say what was simplified), "our own choice, no source" (thresholds, the role vocabulary), or "measured here".
R-2 · Cut
Cut: the connect operation by Steiner tree (no recorded question asks for it); the kind-level relevance filter; the redundancy term as a component; the third tier of field contracts (a pass over untyped web JavaScript); the many-feature learned ranker and the calibration step unless the stop rule first survives a simpler test.
R-3 · Deferred, not built now
Deferred, not built now: the what-breaks ranker (needs test-failure ground truth), the depth-3 tier, the extraction audit with its indexer installs. Per-method confidence is folded into the quality card. Validity sets over the commit graph are infrastructure for stored conclusions and citations, not an agent feature by themselves.
R-4 · Added because the refuter showed they were missing
Added because the refuter showed they were missing: (a) the primary test set is real lookups taken from recorded sessions, weighted by the tokens they cost, not co-change; (b) an index-only arm (document outline, item-ID lookup, symbol and file index, keyword search, no relation edges) in every test, to isolate what the relations add; (c) a scripted-search arm; (d) a brief written by the orchestrator, and plain heading-and-ID extraction, as the strongest simple competitors for the session-start brief; (e) a task-context store built from what earlier sessions on the same phase read and then edited (the degree-of-interest model of Kersten and Murphy) as the documented competitor to a graph-derived brief; (f) an edit-time warning hook as its own component with a trigger, a cost and a false-alarm rate; (g) two histories: code by commit, documents by content hash, because most program documents are not in version control; (h) a leak rule: every document fact used at a commit is extracted as of that commit; (i) cut rules wherever a test had none.
R-5 · Build order changes
Build order changes: first the test set of real lookups and the index-only arm; then the document, item, rule and decision layer with the session-start brief (the largest measured cost); then the conclusion ledger, but only after measuring what share of earlier conclusions rest on things inside the repository; the ranking work comes after these because the question it serves (what changes together) is 1.8 % of product lookup cost.
R-6 · Embeddings and graph neural models
Embeddings and graph neural models: the design says "not at this size" from published evidence and a density argument (about 7 facts per entity here against about 37 in a standard benchmark), not from a measurement on this graph. The owner has trained such models himself. State it as an argued position that can be settled by a small measured trial if he approves the install (a knowledge-graph embedding library and a tensor library, CPU only), and say that the question it would improve is a small part of the token cost.
R-7 · The decisive test changes
The decisive test changes. An agent pilot of about 40 tasks can only see a saving of about 20 to 25 %, and the plausible effect is single-digit percent of context. So the evidence is built in two steps: a lookup-level replay (recorded lookups answered by each arm, scored on whether the needed span is in the answer and on tokens, which can see small effects), then a focused agent test on the session start, where the measured cost is largest (55 % of carried product lookup tokens are spent before the first edit) and an effect could be large enough to see.
R-8 · Corrections to what the owner was told earlier
Corrections to what the owner was told earlier, to be shown plainly: (a) the current graph does NOT link calls by name alone (0 of 4,886 call edges; they are resolved by scope, imports and the type checker), so "replace name matching" was wrong; what is true is that 11.2 % of changed definitions had no node (mostly variables and object members); (b) the first test scored "what changes together", which is 1.8 % of product lookup cost; (c) the 60 configurations were compared at a depth where every candidate fitted in the answer, so the ranking could not matter, and at the depth finally chosen the walk reached two thirds of the graph and selected nothing.

Sources cited for what they do not say
(5)
Hogan's definition of a knowledge graph is misquoted
The design defines a knowledge graph as "a graph with a schema, stable identity, provenance and quality measures [Hogan]". Hogan et al. (ACM CSUR 2021, arXiv 2003.02320, introduction, text extracted from the PDF) adopt an inclusive definition: a graph of data intended to accumulate and convey knowledge of the real world, whose nodes are entities and whose edges are relations. Schema, identity and context are topics the survey discusses, and quality is one of its methods; none is definitional. Consequence: by Hogan's own definition v1 was already a knowledge graph. v2 adds the aspects Hogan discusses. The answer to the owner's "strictly graphs or knowledge graphs or temporal knowledge graph" must say that.
Ruling:
R-1 (the knowledge-graph wording: v1 already was one in Hogan’s inclusive sense)
"Not a temporal knowledge graph" is too narrow, and so is "is a temporal knowledge graph"
A temporal knowledge graph in the representation sense is a set of facts annotated with time (quadruples or validity intervals). snapshots and validity sets's validity sets over commits are exactly that, with the difference that time here is a partial order (the commit graph branches), not a line. What v2 is not is a temporal knowledge graph in the completion or forecasting sense (TLogic, RE-Net). Round 2's temporal lane says it did not review the temporal knowledge graph literature (../widen/lane-temporal.md line 51), so neither round has a primary source for the representation sense either. This is a wording fix, but it is the owner's own question.
Ruling:
R-1 (say which sense of temporal knowledge graph is meant)
[deKleer] is cited for in-lists and out-lists
In-lists and out-lists are the support-list justifications of Doyle's justification-based truth maintenance system (1979). De Kleer's 1986 assumption-based system works with assumption sets and labels. The time lane got this right ("justification-based truth maintenance justifications with in and out lists (Doyle 1979)", lane-time-incremental.md line 302, with Doyle not opened and described through de Kleer). The design's citation should read Doyle (not opened), described in de Kleer 1986. Nothing in the conclusion ledger needs either system's machinery; what the conclusion ledger actually uses is the verifying trace of Build Systems a la Carte (confirmed, verify-b T-a).
Ruling:
R-1 (cite Doyle; the ledger uses build-system traces)

the question registry prune test: biased toward cutting, and measuring the wrong thing
Removing a kind and checking snapshots and validity sets recall plus 20 typed questions cannot show the value of a kind that serves a competency question with one or two questions (uses_fixture for the question “which tests exercise X”, for example). (6)
The ranking tests re-use the data that chose the arms
The pilot (lane-ranking.md §5) ran on exactly the 65 tuning commits and the late 50 rolling split. RRF with k = 60, restart 0.15, the 2-feature form and the floor were all seen there. the two-stage ranker's "keep F only if its paired interval over the best simpler arm is above zero and the gain is at least 0.02" will be computed on the same commits, where RRF already beat the graph-conditioned floor by about +0.022 (0.233 against 0.211). Ruling:

Rolling origin is sound for history features, and only for them
The pilot builds features from the parent snapshot (feat.py line 25, verify-b R-o). Training on at least 15 earlier commits and scoring the late 50 is a correct time-respecting split. Ridge with at most 8 features on about 15,000 candidate rows will not overfit in the classical sense, but the rows are clustered (388 seeds in 50 commits), so the effective sample is about 50, and 12 weighted changed-together counts variants chosen on an inner split then compared on the outer 50 still invite a winner's curse unless the comparison is nested and reported with the selection step inside the bootstrap.
Ruling:
R-4 (i) (nested selection inside the bootstrap)
Document text written after the fact is a leak path the design does not close
CLAUDE.md's invariants name later migrations and PRs (for example migration 0046 and PRs #162 and #163), so many of them did not exist at early tuning commits. the document layer's role anchors are "hand-assigned once for the 20 invariants", at the current text. If any the document layer fact (a rule anchor, a "same rule" feature, a brief section) is used when scoring a tuning commit, it carries knowledge of later defects into the past. The rule must be: every document fact used at commit C is extracted from the document as of C, and hand-assigned roles are dated and used only from their date.

Caveat.
These are sizes of index answers measured on recorded lookups. They are not a forecast of tokens saved: whether an agent would stop reading after such an answer needs an agent run.
What this measurement could not measure (10)
Run-to-run drift: the untracked program documents are read at their live content when baseline.js.txt runs, and other sessions keep writing them, so a re-run can move a baseline cell by an instance or two (seen once: 29.6 to 29.5 %).
Containment of the whole needed span: a baseline "hit" means a result points into a truth span (overlap), not that the result covers all of it.
Which lines of a read the agent actually used: confirmation covers only later edits, file:line citations and verbatim quotes; about 9 % of model calls carry visible text (use-cases.md section 11), so "read but not confirmed" is not "unused".
The exact code version: transcripts carry no commit; the version is the branch tip at the episode time (main when the branch no longer exists), and uncommitted working-tree changes are invisible.
Program documents outside version control: they can only be indexed at their current content, which may hold text written after the session (counted per form as needs-current-docs, and per baseline as hits on current-only files).
Truth outside the repository (scratchpad files, transcripts, memory, web): no repository index can return it; these instances are counted, not scored.
Whether an agent given the index result would have stopped reading: the baselines score containment of the truth span among the first 10 results and its token cost, not the agent behaviour.
Program question forms are labelled by rules that a manual check found right on about 3 in 4 calls (use-cases.md section 2.4); "earlier lane findings" (the question “earlier conclusions”) is the noisiest label.
Confirmation-part numbers: the baselines were computed on the development part only, by design; nothing on the confirmation part has been scored.
Product work is 13 main sessions of one day (2026-11-13) and one plan; the development part holds 8 of them and the confirmation part 5. A per-form product number rests on few sessions.
The two larger levers (for scale)
measured: TOKEN-BOTTLENECK-2026-11.md
Capping the orchestrator window at 150–200k (one phase per session, earlier compaction): 39 % (FixFlow) and 74 % (Clinic) of cache-read tokens sit in calls past 200k. Stripping the fixed prefix: About 60k tokens per agent window, 27 % of all tokens, 47 % of FixFlow's. Fetched content is 23 % of FixFlow context tokens.
Ruling R-7: the two-step test
The decisive test changes. An agent pilot of about 40 tasks can only see a saving of about 20 to 25 %, and the plausible effect is single-digit percent of context. So the evidence is built in two steps: a lookup-level replay (recorded lookups answered by each arm, scored on whether the needed span is in the answer and on tokens, which can see small effects), then a focused agent test on the session start, where the measured cost is largest (55 % of carried product lookup tokens are spent before the first edit) and an effect could be large enough to see.
What it shows.
The share that moves is a fraction of the exposure. At the starting settings it sits well below what an agent pilot of 40 tasks can detect; only settings near the extremes (the agent uses the tool almost always, and answers are a small fraction of the reads they replace) reach that range. A lookup-level replay is the test that can see effects of this size. The arm a graph must beat is already measured: when the agent started from a search, keyword search over names, paths and headings returned a result overlapping the span the agent read in its top 10 for 42.1 % of scorable product lookups and 44 % of program ones (55.3 % and 55.3 % with path seeds included, where the agent already had the file). A hit is an overlap with the read span, not the whole needed span.
