## Papers

### P1. Tsafrir, "Modeling, Evaluating, and Improving the Performance of Supercomputer Scheduling", PhD thesis, Hebrew University 2006. Chapter 4 is "largely based on" Tsafrir, Etsion, Feitelson, "Backfilling using system-generated predictions rather than user runtime estimates", TPDS 2007 (the paper pdf would not download). Read from: https://dants.github.io/papers/Tsafrir06PhD.pdf. Saved: P:tsafrir/paper.txt. Read: chapter 1 backfilling and results summary, chapter 2 log table, chapter 4 sections 4.2 to 4.2.4, Tables 4.3 and 4.7. Not read: chapters 3, 5, 6 line by line.
- Problem: batch scheduling of parallel jobs; first-come first-served leaves processors idle while a wide job waits; backfilling starts later jobs early without delaying the head job's reservation.
- Mechanism: EASY reserves the earliest start (the shadow time) for the first queued job and starts any later job that ends before it or uses spare processors. The improvement: predict a job's runtime as the mean of the same user's last two finished jobs, raise a prediction that proves too short to the user's estimate (the user's estimate stays the kill limit), backfill shortest first (SJBF).
- Result: "EASY required users to provide a runtime estimate for all submitted jobs" (txt line 688). "jobs that exceed their estimates are killed, so as not to violate subsequent commitments (the reservation)" (txt line 689-690). "consistent figures of 40-60% average utilization have gone up to around 70%, once backfilling was introduced" (txt line 667-668; five supercomputers, eleven years, cited from another study). "backfilling was shown to be a close second to more sophisticated algorithms that involve preemption (time slicing), migration, and dynamic partitioning" (txt line 674-676). Predictions without correction hurt: "performance is worsened by up to an order of magnitude!" (txt line 1978), because "the reservation is made for the present time effectively stopping all backfilling" (txt line 1982). "The predictors we use are as simple as e.g. averaging the runtime of the two most recently submitted (and already terminated) jobs by the same user." (txt line 1865-1866). "the more history we exploit when making a prediction, the worse the resulting performance becomes" (txt line 1879-1880).
- Measured gain (simulation replaying real logs; not a live system): Table 4.3 (txt lines 4143-4146), average wait in minutes, EASY / SJBF only / EASY++ / PERFECT++ (prediction exactly right): SDSC 363 / 361 / 327 (-10 %) / 278 (-23 %); CTC 21 / 19 / 14 (-33 %) / 19 (-10 %); KTH 114 / 102 / 95 (-17 %) / 91 (-20 %); BLUE 130 / 102 / 87 (-33 %) / 87 (-33 %). Bounded slowdown EASY / EASY++ / PERFECT++: SDSC 99 / 70 (-29 %) / 58 (-42 %); CTC 4.6 / 2.9 (-37 %) / 2.8 (-39 %); KTH 90 / 57 (-36 %) / 50 (-44 %); BLUE 35 / 19 (-47 %) / 13 (-64 %). Accuracy (runtime over estimate) with user estimates 31-47 %, with EASY++ about 60 % (txt lines 4143-4146, 4181). Text: SJBF alone "typical improvements of 10-20%, and up to 42% (BLUE's bounded slowdown)" (txt line 4127-4128); EASY+ "typical improvement over vanilla EASY is around 25%" (txt line 2001-2002). "In both cases making estimates less accurate by doubling ("X2") tends to help." (txt line 839). "EASY++ outperforms X2 by a wide margin for all traces and both metrics. It is also rather close to SJF scheduling in all cases" (txt line 4401-4403). Logs (txt lines 2213-2235): SDSC SP2 128 processors, 24 months, load 84 %, 54,053 clean jobs; CTC 512 processors, 11 months, load 56 %; KTH 100 processors, 11 months, load 69 %; BLUE 1,152 processors, 32 months, load 76 %; mean run time 73 to 188 minutes.
  (Mu'alem and Feitelson's original paper was not opened; the thesis cites it and repeats the finding that less accurate estimates can help.)
- Assumes: 100 to 1,152 processors; jobs of tens of minutes to hours; tens of thousands of jobs per log; a user submits streams of similar jobs (the last two predict the next); the user's estimate stays a kill limit; a job needs a fixed number of processors for its whole run.
- With model sessions (reasoned): the gains are ratios of wait and slowdown, and the log sizes (tens of thousands of jobs, 100+ processors) are far above ours (tens to hundreds of units, 3 to 16 workers): not shown to hold at our size. The "wide job waits, small jobs fill the gap" shape arises only if some units need several workers at once or a shared resource (a refilling window, a shared check); if every unit takes one worker, backfilling becomes shortest-first among waiting units, and SJBF alone gave 10 to 20 %. A prediction from the last two units of the same kind and model is the only form that needs no new instrument; a rerun is not the same run, so a prediction can be wrong in both directions and needs the correction step. A kill limit (turn or token cap) exists for a session.
- Needs to know before use: each unit's length (at least an upper limit), the workers it needs, the unit's kind and model (to predict from earlier units of that kind).
- Status: quoted (thesis, not the TPDS paper).

### P2. Kolisch and Hartmann, "Experimental investigation of heuristics for resource-constrained project scheduling: an update", EJOR 174:23-37, 2006. Read from: https://www.hsba.de/fileadmin/user_upload/bereiche/_dokumente/6-forschung/profs-publikationen/Hartmann_2006_Experimental_investigation_of_Heuristics.pdf. Saved: P:kolisch06/paper.txt. Read: sections 1, 3.1 to 3.2.2, the head rows of Tables 1 to 3.
- Problem: activities need units of several limited resources for fixed times with precedence between them; minimise the project finish (makespan). NP-hard.
- Mechanism: a schedule generation scheme (serial: place activities one at a time at the earliest feasible time; parallel: step through time and start what fits) driven by a priority rule, by random sampling, or by a search over activity lists (genetic, tabu, annealing); forward-backward improvement (FBI) shifts a schedule back and forward.
- Result: "The parameters pj, rj,k, and Rk are assumed to be nonnegative and deterministic." (txt line 36). "Again, the best performing methods are metaheuristics." (txt line 428-429). "adding FBI to random sampling leads to much better results than adding a priority rule" (txt lines 715-716). "Since neither the serial nor the parallel SGS is consistently superior ... it appears to be a good idea to employ both." (txt lines 448-450).
- Measured gain: 30 activities (J30), gap to the optimum, best method (Kochetov and Stolyar) 0.10 / 0.04 / 0.00 % at 1,000 / 5,000 / 50,000 schedules (txt line 461); a simple sampling rule 0.30-0.54 % at 1,000 (txt lines 477-487). 120 activities (J120), gap to the critical-path lower bound (not the optimum): best 11.73 / 11.10 / 10.71 % (Debels et al., txt line 545); the benchmark of the earlier survey 12.75 / 11.90 / 11.54 % (txt line 557-558). Single-pass priority rules alone are not in this paper's tables.
- Assumes: 30 to 120 activities, 4 resource types, durations known exactly, one project, up to 50,000 schedules computed.
- With model sessions (reasoned): the instance sizes bracket our tens to hundreds of units, and the gap between the best search and a plain rule is about 1 to 5 points of makespan; a search is cheap next to a unit (computer seconds against model minutes). But the paper assumes exact durations; it offers no result for rough lengths, which is our case. Not comparable by one number with P1, P4 (different problems and baselines).
- Needs to know before use: each unit's duration, precedence, per-unit use of each limited resource.
- Status: quoted. The 1996 paper with the single-pass priority-rule gaps was not opened.

### P3. Dean and Ghemawat, "MapReduce: Simplified Data Processing on Large Clusters", OSDI 2004. Read from: https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/mapreduce-osdi04.pdf. Saved: P:mapreduce/paper.txt. Read: 3.6, 5.3, 5.4, related work. Self-reported (Google).
- Problem: one slow machine on one of the last tasks delays the whole operation (a straggler).
- Mechanism: near the end of the operation, start backup executions of the remaining in-progress tasks; the first copy to finish counts.
- Result: "When a MapReduce operation is close to completion, the master schedules backup executions of the remaining in-progress tasks." (txt line 158). "We have tuned this mechanism so that it typically increases the computational resources used by the operation by no more than a few percent." (txt line 158). Causes named: a bad disk (30 MB/s to 1 MB/s), competing tasks, a cache bug slowing work "by over a factor of one hundred" (txt line 157).
- Measured gain (self-reported, one benchmark): "the sort program ... takes 44% longer to complete when the backup task mechanism is disabled" (txt line 158); "After 960 seconds, all except 5 of the reduce tasks are completed. However these last few stragglers don't finish until 300 seconds later. The entire computation takes 1283 seconds, an increase of 44% in elapsed time." (txt line 344).
- Assumes: hundreds to thousands of machines; tasks of seconds to a minute; a task is repeatable and a duplicate result can be dropped; only the few remaining tasks are copied.
- With model sessions (reasoned): the few-percent cost comes from copying only the tail; a copied unit of ours costs its whole token count again, and a copy is not the same run. The causes (bad disk, noisy neighbour) have no counterpart for a session; a slow session is a long reasoning run or a rate delay. Whether our unit lengths have a heavy tail is not measured: it is a measurement to take.
- Needs to know before use: which units remain; that a duplicate is allowed and discarded.
- Status: quoted.

### P4. Zaharia, Konwinski, Joseph, Katz, Stoica, "Improving MapReduce Performance in Heterogeneous Environments" (LATE), OSDI 2008. Read from: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi08/tech/full_papers/zaharia/zaharia.pdf. Saved: P:late/paper.txt. Read: sections 1 to 5.4.
- Problem: Hadoop copies a task whose progress is below the average, which fails when machines differ in speed.
- Mechanism: rank running tasks by estimated time left, (1 - progress) over progress rate; copy the longest first, only on fast nodes, with copies capped at 10 % of slots.
- Result: "We always speculatively execute the task that we think will finish farthest into the future, because this task provides the greatest opportunity for a speculative copy to overtake the original" (txt line 171). Time left is "(1 - ProgressScore)/ProgressRate. This assumes that tasks make progress at a roughly constant rate." (txt line 172). "set the SpeculativeCap to 10% of available task slots and set the SlowNodeThreshold and SlowTaskThreshold to the 25th percentile" (txt line 191); sensitivity test on a sleep workload found 20 % best (txt line 588).
- Measured gain: "LATE can improve Hadoop response times by a factor of 2 in clusters of 200 virtual machines on EC2" (txt line 7). Sort, 30 GB, 486 map and 437 reduce tasks on virtual machines sharing hosts, 6 runs: "LATE finished jobs 27% faster than Hadoop's native scheduler and 31% faster than no speculation" (txt line 414). With induced stragglers, 4 experiments: "58% faster than Hadoop's native scheduler and 220% faster than Hadoop with speculative execution disabled ... as high as 93%" (txt line 452). "Google has noted that speculative execution can improve job response times by 44% [1]" (txt line 12).
- Assumes: roughly constant progress rate; a progress score exists; nodes differ 2 to 3 times in speed; jobs of minutes on 40 to 200 machines.
- With model sessions (reasoned): a model session has no progress score, so time left needs its own estimate (a measurement). What carries is the ranking (copy the unit that will finish last, not any slow one) and a cap by share of workers: 10 % of 3 to 16 workers is 0.3 to 1.6, so in whole workers the cap is 0 or 1.
- Needs to know before use: a time-left estimate for each running unit.
- Status: quoted.

### P5. Ananthanarayanan, Kandula, Greenberg, Stoica, Lu, Saha, Harris, "Reining in the Outliers in Map-Reduce Clusters using Mantri", OSDI 2010. Read from: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi10/tech/full_papers/Ananthanarayanan.pdf. Saved: P:mantri/paper.txt (some digits are lost in the extraction; only figures that print are quoted). Read: abstract, introduction, section 2, evaluation opening. Self-reported (Microsoft Bing, production clusters).
- Problem: slow tasks lengthen jobs; blind duplication wastes resources.
- Mechanism: cause- and cost-aware: restart an outlier only if a new copy is expected to finish sooner than the remaining time and resource use justify, place tasks by network use, replicate the output of valuable tasks, act early on progress reports.
- Result: "outliers inflate the completion time of jobs by 34% at median" (txt line 22). "A task that runs for long because it has more work to do will not be restarted" (txt line 30).
- Measured gain (self-reported; deployment on a production cluster of thousands of servers plus trace replay): "Mantri reduces the completion time of jobs by 32% on" the production cluster (txt line 32; the rest of the line is cut in the extraction). The other figures (resource use, simulation percentiles) did not extract.
- Assumes: thousands of servers; phases separated by barriers; many jobs at once; real-time progress reports.
- With model sessions (reasoned): what carries is "do not restart a unit that is long because it has more work" which needs a size estimate before a unit runs. Not measured for sessions.
- Needs to know before use: expected work per task, progress reports.
- Status: quoted in part (evaluation figures beyond line 32 not recovered).

### P6. Ananthanarayanan, Ghodsi, Shenker, Stoica, "Effective Straggler Mitigation: Attack of the Clones" (Dolly), NSDI 2013. Read from: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi13/nsdi13-final231.pdf. Saved: P:dolly/paper.txt. Read: abstract, introduction, section 2 (traces), section 3 up to the pseudocode.
- Problem: for small jobs (a few tasks started at once) waiting to detect stragglers is too late.
- Mechanism: launch clones of every task of a small job at the start, use the first to finish; a hard cloning budget as a fraction of cluster capacity; clone a job only if all its tasks fit.
- Result: "stragglers that are on average 8 times slower than the median task in that job. Such stragglers increase the average job duration by 47%" (txt line 6). "Speculative execution waits to observe the progress of the tasks of a job and launches duplicates of those tasks that are slower." (txt line 14). "Dolly operates within an allotted resource budget. This budget is a configurable fraction (beta) of the total capacity" (txt line 258).
- Measured gain: "Dolly improves the average completion time of the small jobs by 34% to 46%, respectively, with LATE and Mantri as baselines. These improvements come with a resource budget of merely 5% ... Dolly effectively reduces the slowest task from running 8x slower on average to 1.06x" (txt line 26). 150-node cluster, replaying Facebook (Hadoop) and Bing (Dryad) production workloads.
- Assumes: most jobs are small and use a small share of resources (heavy-tailed job sizes); clones run independently; spare capacity exists.
- With model sessions (reasoned): the 5 % budget holds because small jobs are a small share of work; whether short units are a small share of our tokens is not measured. If every unit is minutes long and tokens are the scarce thing, a clone costs a whole unit. What carries is the form: a hard cap as a fraction of capacity.
- Needs to know before use: the share of work in short jobs, the probability that a task straggles.
- Status: quoted.

### P7. Lifka, "The ANL/IBM SP Scheduling System", JSSPP 1995 (LNCS 949). Read from: http://web.archive.org/web/2017im_/http://www.cs.huji.ac.il/~feit/parsched/jsspp95/p-95-16.pdf (author-hosted copy found through the archived JSSPP 1995 index). Saved: P:lifka95/paper.txt (`-layout`, 347 lines). Read: whole paper.
- Problem: first-come first-served on a 128-node SP leaves nodes idle while a wide job waits.
- Mechanism: FIFO with backfilling: when the first queued job cannot start, compute from the running jobs' stated minutes how long it is blocked, then start a later job that fits the idle nodes and ends within that time, or that leaves enough nodes when the blocked job starts.
- Result: "It quickly became apparent to all that a FIFO queue was extremely inefficient." (txt line 180-181; the text has "ine cient" through the ligature loss). "Backfilling provides a way to fill in the idle nodes caused by the situation just described with other jobs further down the queue, provided that they do not cause the first job in the queue to wait any longer for the nodes they require." (txt line 186-189). Worked example: User D "should have to wait for 60 minutes at the longest" and a later 4-node, 30-minute job starts at once (txt lines 244-251); a longer 8-node, 480-minute job may also start because enough nodes remain when the blocked job starts (txt lines 270-276).
- Measured gain: none. The paper has no utilisation or wait figures; it is a system description. The gain figures are in P1 (thesis, Table 4.3) and the Mu'alem paper (not readable, below).
- Assumes: users state a runtime for each job; a fixed node count per job; FIFO as the base policy.
- With model sessions (reasoned): only the rule is useful (start a later unit if it cannot delay the first waiting unit); no number from this paper.
- Needs to know before use: each unit's stated length and worker need.
- Status: quoted (mechanism only).
