## Gate summary

- P1 Lehman-Yao 1981: split is two steps (half-split by link pointer, then post to parent); at most 3 locks, searches take none; no merge, "batch reorganization ... locks the entire tree" for underflow. No throughput figure (line 487). quoted, lines 359, 478-482, 487.
- P2 Bw-tree 2013: any thread that meets a half-done split or merge completes it first; speedup 18.7x (Xbox), 8.6x (dedup), 5.8x (synthetic) against BerkeleyDB, 8 threads. quoted, lines 70, 247, 357.
- P3 Wang 2018: the same Bw-tree, rebuilt, is 1.5-4.5x SLOWER than lock-based trees with OLC on 20 threads. quoted, line 90, line 810.
- P4 ART sync 2016: lock coupling 418 cycles per lookup at 1 thread, 2787 at 20 (6.7x worse); OLC 348 and 418 (1.2x). quoted, line 348.
- P5 OLC 2019: B+tree, 100M keys, 20 threads: OLC lookup 3.9x faster than lock coupling; B-link holds one lock at a time but "has the same scalability issues as lock coupling" in memory. quoted, lines 29, 347.
- P6 ARIES/IM 1992: structure changes ARE serialised, one per tree, by an X tree latch held "very small" time, no I/O inside; concurrent ones need a lock, with deadlock risk. quoted, lines 263, 603, 612.
- P7 Srinivasan and Carey 1993: simulation; B-link best, lock-coupling with exclusive locks worst, gap grows with resources. No one ratio in the text. quoted, lines 10, 471, 600.
- P8 Graefe 2010: survey; the second half-split step "can be made a side effect of any future root-to-leaf traversal" and may be delayed "beyond a system reboot". No measurements. quoted, line 110-111.
- Merge: Lehman-Yao, ARIES/IM (page deleted only when empty), Srinivasan-Carey ("merges only when pages become empty") all avoid merge; only Bw-tree and Lanin-Shasha (not opened) do a concurrent merge. quoted.

## Papers

### P1. Lehman and Yao, "Efficient Locking for Concurrent Operations on B-Trees", ACM TODS 6(4), 1981. Read from: https://www.csd.uoc.gr/~hy460/pdf/p650-lehman.pdf. Folder: R:papers/tree/lehmanyao/. Pages read: all (text of sections 1-8).
- Problem: let many processes search, insert and delete in one B-tree on disk without locking whole paths.
- Mechanism: every node gets a high key and a link pointer to its right twin. A split makes two twins joined by the link and only later adds the parent pointer; a search that finds its key above the high key follows the link. Locks only stop two updaters writing one node.
- Measured gain: none. quoted: "A simulation would enable us to verify ... " (line 470); "The behavior of this system could be quantified by simulation ... Such a simulation would also be useful for comparison with other concurrency schemes." (line 487). x03-0201 stands.
- Operations:
  - Search: "this procedure does no locking of any kind" (line 233).
  - Split: "When a node is split because of data overflow, a single node is replaced by two new nodes. The link pointer of the first new node points to the second node" (line 162). The legal intermediate state: "it is legal to have a node in the tree that has no parent, but has a left twin" (line 163), but "the pointer from the father must be added quickly for good search time" (line 163).
  - Who completes it: the inserter that split the node, backing up one level at a time (line 359); "at most three nodes are ever locked simultaneously, and this occurs relatively infrequently: only when it is necessary to follow a link pointer while inserting a pointer to a split node" (line 359).
  - How long: not stated as a time; "Three nodes are locked only for the duration of one operation" (line 485).
  - Merge: "A simple way of handling deletions is to allow fewer than K entries in a leaf node." (line 478). "In situations where excessive deletions cause the storage utilization of tree nodes to be unacceptably low, a batch reorganization or an underflow operation which locks the entire tree can be performed." (line 482). So merge is a whole-tree, rare, offline-style step.
  - Root change: not described beyond a stack of rightmost nodes kept per level (line 236); not found as a separate section.
  - Livelock: "our algorithms do not prevent the possibility of livelock" (line 466); reasoned away by comparable speeds and few node creations.
- Assumes: node reads and writes are atomic; processes of comparable speed (line 469); splits rare ("the splitting of a node is an exceptional case", line 164); inserts more common than deletes (line 482); "extremely small collision probability ... unless there are many concurrent processes" for large node capacity (line 486, quoted).
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the idea carries over cleanly in one way: a half-finished split is a valid state, so a worker that has been handed a range need not wait for the parent to be updated; its questions reach the new half by the link. It breaks in another: "processes of comparable speed" and "atomic node write" are false for sessions (minutes per unit, a rerun differs), so the livelock note applies to a slow session chasing links. No merge means a tree that only grows and is rebuilt rarely, which fits a job that ends. Small job: no need. Very large: yes, a link plus high key is the cheapest way to let a range be split without stopping its neighbours. Ratio law: collision chance falls with node capacity (quoted, no number).
- Status: quoted.

### P2. Levandoski, Lomet, Sengupta, "The Bw-Tree: A B-tree for New Hardware Platforms", ICDE 2013. Read from: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/bw-tree-icde2013-final.pdf. Folder: R:papers/tree/bwtree2013/. Pages read: all.
- Problem: a B-tree that never blocks on a latch, built for many cores and flash.
- Mechanism: pages are never updated in place; an update is a delta record put in front of the page by one compare-and-swap on a mapping table. Splits and merges are broken into steps that each leave the tree valid; any thread that finds a half-done step finishes it first.
- Measured gain: "For the Xbox workload, the Bw-tree exhibits a throughput of 10.4M operations/second, while BerkeleyDB has a throughput of 555K operations/second, representing a speedup of 18.7x. ... the Bw-Tree maintains a 8.6x speedup over BerkeleyDB. ... (5.8x Bw-tree speedup)" for synthetic (line 357). Baseline BerkeleyDB B-tree mode, 8K pages; 4 cores hyperthreaded to 8 logical, 8 worker threads, in memory (lines 317, 322, quoted). Against a latch-free skip list: x03-0338 and x03-0339 (3.7x synthetic, 4.4x read-only), line 369 not re-read in full. The paper gives no curve of throughput against threads (x03-0203 stands). Wang 2018 (P3) contradicts the ranking on other baselines.
- Operations:
  - Update: "A page is never updated in place" by a delta (line 63); failed CAS is retried.
  - Consolidation: "We trigger consolidation if an accessor thread, during a page search, notices a delta chain length has exceeded a system threshold" (line 120). Chain length 4 (Xbox, 100-byte records) to about 8 (synthetic, 8-byte keys) before search slows (line 325).
  - Split: "Splits are triggered by an accessor thread that notices a page size has grown beyond a system threshold" (line 183). Step 1: new right sibling Q built with "the records from P with keys greater than KP", then "We atomically install the split by prepending a split delta record to P" (lines 187-188) = half split. Step 2: "we prepend an index term delta record to the parent" (line 189).
  - Who completes it: "a thread that sees a partial SMO will complete it before proceeding with its own operation. This ensures that no thread needs to wait for an SMO to complete" (line 70); "it must complete the split SMO by posting the new index term delta to the parent. Only then can it continue" (lines 246-247).
  - Merge: three atomic steps (remove-node delta on R, merge delta on left sibling L, index-term delete in parent); "Merges are decidedly more complicated than splits, and we need more atomic actions to accomplish them" (line 195). Two merges meeting: "the thread deleting R needs to first complete the delete of L ... This can lead to the processing of a 'stack' of SMOs, but given the rarity of this situation, it should not occur often" (line 248).
  - Failure rates, Table I (lines 280-290): record updates 0.0013%, 0.0171%, 0.0003%; splits 0.25%, 1.27%, 8.88% (dedup, Xbox, synthetic); the text puts the split failure at "8.88% for the synthetic workload ... a worst-case scenario" (line 354).
  - Root change: not described in the passages read.
- Assumes: 64-bit CAS; short operations (a split competes with record updates and loses when records are tiny: line 354); epoch-based memory reclamation; the mapping table.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the rule "whoever meets an unfinished structure change finishes it first" is the part that carries over; it removes any wait on a coordinator. Costs that do not carry over: a failed CAS costs nanoseconds, a failed split by a session costs a unit of minutes of tokens, so the 1.25% to 8.88% failure rates would be far dearer; helping needs a session to be able to act on a node it does not own. Small job: not needed. Very large: the help rule is a candidate. Law as a ratio: failure rate of the structure change rises as the competing operation gets shorter (8.88% when updates are the fastest; quoted, line 354).
- Status: quoted.

### P3. Wang, Pavlo, Lim, Leis, Zhang, Kaminsky, Andersen, "Building a Bw-Tree Takes More Than Just Buzz Words", SIGMOD 2018. Read from: https://db.cs.cmu.edu/papers/2018/mod342-wangA.pdf. Folder: R:papers/tree/wang2018/. Pages read: all text extracted; sections 1, 2 (background, SMOs), 5 (evaluation) and the closing read in detail, the optimisation sections skimmed.
- Problem: is the Bw-Tree really faster than lock-based in-memory indexes?
- Mechanism: rebuild the Bw-Tree with fixes (pre-allocated delta space, decentralised memory reclamation, fast consolidation, search shortcuts) and compare with SkipList, Masstree, a B+tree with optimistic lock coupling, and ART with OLC.
- Measured gain: "despite previous claims of lock-free indexes being superior to lock-based indexes on multi-core CPUs, the overhead of the Bw-Tree's indirection layer and delta records causes it to under-perform the lock-based indexes by 1.5-4.5x" (line 90); "the ART is more than 4x faster than the OpenBw-Tree for point lookups ... The OpenBw-Tree is also slower than the Masstree and the B+Tree, often by a factor of 2x" (line 810). Setup: two Xeon E5-2680 v2, 20 threads pinned to one socket, about 52M keys, YCSB A/C/E (lines 387-389). Own optimisations speed the Bw-Tree by 27-35% (line 720). "after disabling these lock-free features, the Bw-Tree is still 15%-19% slower than the B+Tree with OLC" (line 1222).
- Operations: SMOs in "a logical phase which appends special deltas to notify other threads of an ongoing SMO, and a physical phase" done by another thread; "Threads must help complete an unfinished SMO before the corresponding virtual node can be traversed" (lines 161-164). A failed CAS aborts the operation and restarts it from the root (line 154). "Although the Bw-Tree is lock-free, threads are not guaranteed to make progress because of failed CaS" (line 162).
- Assumes: in-memory, 20 threads on one socket.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the result is a warning about the cost of the machinery, not a result about sessions. For sessions the per-operation overhead (cycles) is irrelevant next to minutes of work, so the ranking by instruction count does not decide; what does carry is "help-along gives no progress guarantee" and "restart from the root". Not tested for 3 to 16 writers.
- Status: quoted.

### P4. Leis, Scheibner, Kemper, Neumann, "The ART of Practical Synchronization", DaMoN 2016. Read from: https://db.in.tum.de/~leis/papers/artsync.pdf. Folder: R:papers/tree/artsync/. Pages read: all.
- Problem: synchronise a radix tree (ART) on many cores.
- Mechanism: Optimistic Lock Coupling (a version counter per node; readers take no lock, validate the version, restart on change) and ROWEX (writers lock, readers never block).
- Measured gain: lookups at 1 / 20 threads, cycles per lookup: no sync 211 / 381; lock coupling 418 / 2787; OLC 348 / 418; ROWEX 375 / 427; Masstree 982 / 1231 (line 348). Setup: 50M random 8-byte integers, one Xeon E5-2687W v3, 10 cores, 20 hyper-threads (lines 338, 342). "Lock coupling does not scale well, even in the lookup experiment and although we use read-write locks. OLC and ROWEX ... scale very well ... with OLC slightly (around 7%) faster for lookup" (line 342). "the CPU cycles increase by a factor of 6.6x" (line 356). OLC overhead against unsynchronised: "around 65%" at one thread, "only 10%" at 20 (line 356). Derived (reasoned, from line 348): lock coupling 2787/418 = 6.7 times its own single-thread cost, OLC 418/348 = 1.2 times.
- Operations: lock coupling "holding at most 2 locks at a time during traversal, is the standard method for synchronizing B-trees" (line 132); the cause of failure: "Each time a core acquires a read lock for a node (by writing to that node), all copies of that cache line are invalidated" (line 136); restart: "If a change occurred, the lookup operation is restarted from the root of tree" (line 140); "limit the number of optimistic restarts. After this limit has been reached, the lookup operation can acquire write locks instead" (line 215). A change touches at most two nodes in ART; "In contrast to B-trees, a modification will never propagate up to more than 1 level" (lines 132-134).
- Assumes: shared memory with cache coherence, nodes of nanosecond access, short critical regions.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the failing resource, a lock word whose cache line moves between cores, does not exist when a node is a session; the lock-coupling collapse is a cache effect. What transfers is the shape: a per-node version, work done without a lock, a check at the end, redo on change, and a cap on redo with a fall-back to exclusive. For a session a redo costs minutes of tokens, so the restart probability must be small. Small job: no tree sync needed. Very large: version check plus a redo cap is a plausible rule.
- Status: quoted.

### P5. Leis, Haubenschild, Neumann, "Optimistic Lock Coupling: A Scalable and Efficient General-Purpose Synchronization Method", IEEE Data Eng. Bull. 42(1), March 2019. Read from: http://sites.computer.org/debull/A19mar/p73.pdf. Folder: R:papers/tree/olc2019/. Pages read: all.
- Problem: a general synchronisation method for B-trees and other trees that is simple and scales.
- Mechanism: as P4, applied to an in-memory B+tree.
- Measured gain: "With 20 threads, lookup with OLC is 3.9x faster than traditional lock coupling" (line 347, quoted; B+tree with 100M keys, 4096-byte nodes, 8-byte keys, Xeon E5-2687W v3 10 cores / 20 threads, lines 302-307). Single-threaded all three approaches "have very similar performance" and locking adds "up to 30% of additional instructions" (line 308). OLC lookup scaling "near-linear up to 20 threads" (line 309). It also says an OLC B-tree is simpler than the Bw-tree: OpenBw-Tree "requires an order of magnitude more code" (line 91), and cites another study where OLC "outperformed the Bw-tree" (line 33, summary of a citation).
- Operations: "Even the vast majority of update operations (with the exception of splits and merges), only modify a single leaf node" (line 39). On B-link: "the only 'dangerous' thing that could have happened is the split of a child node (assuming one does not implement merge operations) ... the B-link tree has the same scalability issues as lock coupling (it acquires just as many locks)" for in-memory work; "Releasing the parent lock early is highly beneficial when the child node needs to be fetched from disk" (line 29, all quoted). Fall-back: "limiting the number of optimistic retry operations and falling back to pessimistic locking in cases of very heavy contention ... a major advantage ... in terms of robustness" (line 244). Root: the code re-checks "make sure the root is still the root" (line 117).
- Assumes: in-memory nodes, version counter, epoch-based or hazard-pointer reclamation of removed nodes (line 246).
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): same as P4. The statement about B-link is the important one for the lane: B-link's single-lock advantage is greatest when a node access is slow (disk), which is nearer to the session case than to RAM. A session node is far slower than a disk read. So the 2019 ranking (OLC over B-link) is a statement about in-memory trees and does not apply. Small and very large: not tested by the paper.
- Status: quoted.

### P6. Mohan and Levine, "ARIES/IM: An Efficient and High Concurrency Index Management Method Using Write-Ahead Logging", SIGMOD 1992. Read from: https://ics.uci.edu/~cs223/papers/p371-mohan.pdf. Folder: R:papers/tree/ariesim/. Pages read: all.
- Problem: concurrency and recovery of B+-trees with transactions that can roll back, including undo of structure changes.
- Mechanism: a structure modification (SMO: split, page delete) is logged as a nested top action that commits by itself; SMOs in one tree are serialised by an X tree latch; a bit on each page marks an unfinished SMO.
- Measured gain: none in this paper (no performance section found).
- Operations: "SMOs within a single index tree are serialized" by an "X tree latch that is specific to this index" (line 263). "The tree latch is acquired in the X mode by a transaction just before it performs the SMO at the leaf level and is held until ALL the effects of that SMO are propagated up the tree" (line 267). SMO done "by the same transaction that encountered a need for it" (line 261); bottom up; "When a page is split by a transaction, other transactions are not prevented from reading that page or even modifying that page before the transaction which performed the split commits" (line 261). "The SMO is performed as a nested top action" (line 493). Time held: "the holder of the tree latch will not do any I/Os while holding the tree latch and hence the time interval for which the latch is held should be very small" (line 603); the latch is taken "only after all the affected pages have been brought into the buffer pool ... to minimize the serialization delays" (line 416). Half-split state: the SM_Bit on an affected page warns others (line 271); a traverser can see "an inconsistent tree" (line 261). Page delete only when the page is empty (line 419 and 512). Concurrent SMOs: "Serialization of SMOs via X latches on the tree was specified earlier only to make the presentation simple. Concurrent SMOs can be easily permitted by changing the tree latch into a lock ... deadlocks may occur if multiple SMOs are permitted concurrently" (line 612). The nested top action means that, if a transaction later rolls back, the SMO is not undone: a dummy log record lets undo skip over it (line 513).
- Assumes: a log, a buffer pool, short latch hold, one tree latch per index.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): two things carry over. One: structure changes in a tree can be serialised (one at a time) without harming readers, because the latch is held a short time and readers are not blocked; this answers the question "are they serialised": in ARIES/IM yes, by default. Two: a structure change that must not be undone when the unit of work that caused it is redone is the nested top action; for sessions, a split recorded as its own committed step, not undone when the work that triggered it is rerun. The "very small" latch time depends on no I/O inside; a hand-over between sessions takes minutes, so serialising it would be a long wait unless the split is recorded cheaply as a file step and the new session starts later.
- Status: quoted.

### P7. Srinivasan and Carey, "Performance of B+ Tree Concurrency Control Algorithms", VLDB Journal 2(4), 1993 (the longer version of SIGMOD 1991 "Performance of B-tree concurrency control algorithms"). Read from: https://www.csd.uoc.gr/~hy460/pdf/P361.pdf. Folder: R:papers/tree/srinivasan-carey/. Pages read: all text; sections 1-4.1 in detail, 4.2-4.3 and the conclusion by search.
- Problem: which of the many concurrent B+-tree algorithms performs best, with realistic resources.
- Mechanism: a closed queuing simulation (CPUs, disks, buffer pool, lock manager) running Bayer-Schkolnick (lock coupling, X or SIX locks), top-down, optimistic-descent and B-link variants.
- Measured gain: summary quoted from the abstract: "algorithms with updaters that lock-couple using exclusive locks perform poorly as compared to those that permit more optimistic index descents. In particular, the B-link algorithms are seen to provide the most concurrency and the best overall performance" (line 10). Setup: simulation; tree of 40,000 initial keys, fan-out 200 (3 levels) or 8 (6 levels); workloads 80% search / 10% insert / 10% delete, 20/40/40, and 50% appends with 50% search; 1 CPU 1 disk, 1 CPU 8 disks, and infinite resources (lines 283-293, 322, 601, 833). Findings quoted: with one CPU and one disk the disk is the bottleneck and "the only performance difference visible" is minor (lines 323-324); with 8 disks "the pessimistic algorithms level off at around the same maximum throughput" while "the optimistic and B-link algorithms make excellent gains in throughput with increasing MPL" (line 471); the update-heavy 20/40/40 workload made lock coupling "even worse" (line 601). No single speed-up ratio is printed in the text I extracted; the curves are in figures that I did not read as numbers. So no figure is given here.
- Operations: "B+-trees in real database systems usually perform page merges only when pages become empty; nodes are not actually required to contain at least d entries, because this simplifies implementation and for practical workloads is not found to decrease occupancy by much (Johnson and Shasha, 1989)" (line 64, quoted). B-link half-split then parent insert, "operations therefore lock a maximum of one node at a time" (line 133). Lanin-Shasha: "Merges can also be done in two steps ... via a half-merge followed by an entry deletion at the next higher tree level" (line 132, a citation). Link chases stay rare because splits are rare when the fan-out is high and operations rarely hit a leaf being split when it is low (line 806).
- Assumes: disk-resident tree, locks with a lock manager, node-level operations of milliseconds.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): this is the study whose time scale is closest to ours (milliseconds of disk against a lock of microseconds), and it still ranks B-link first; the cost of holding a lock across a slow step is the whole difference, and a session is a slower step still. The finding "pessimistic locking of the root caps throughput however many resources are added" is a ratio-type law: throughput is bounded by what is serialised at the root. Small job: with one resource the disk dominates and algorithms differ little (line 323, quoted); this is the closest thing to a "small project makes no difference" statement in the lane. Very large: B-link or optimistic.
- Status: quoted (the abstract and the passages cited). No ratio.

### P8. Graefe, "A Survey of B-Tree Locking Techniques", ACM TODS 35(3), article 16, 2010. Read from: https://users.cs.utah.edu/~pandey/courses/cs6530/fall24/papers/trees/a16-graefe.pdf (same bytes as the Georgia Tech copy, 408047 bytes). Folder: R:papers/tree/graefe2010/. Pages read: sections 1-4 on structure latching and the B-link passage in full; the key-range locking sections skimmed.
- Problem: sort out the terms and techniques for concurrency control in B-trees.
- Mechanism: separate latches (threads, short, protect structure) from locks (transactions, long, protect contents).
- Measured gain: none; a survey.
- Operations: lock coupling: "the latch on the parent node should be released while reading the child node from disk" and "restarting the root-to-leaf traversal ... it is often possible to resume the prior search after verifying, for example, based on the log sequence number on each page, that ancestor pages have not been modified" (line 97, quoted). B-link split: "The first step (splitting a node) creates the high fence key and a new right neighbor. The second, independent step posts the high fence key in the parent. The second step can be made a side effect of any future root-to-leaf traversal, should happen as soon as possible, yet may be delayed beyond a system reboot or even a crash and its recovery without data loss or inconsistency" (lines 110-111). "The advantage ... is that allocation of a new node and its initial introduction into the B-tree is a local step, affecting only one preexisting node. The disadvantages are that search may be a bit less efficient, a solution is needed to prevent long linked lists among neighbor nodes during periods of high insertion rates, and verification of a B-tree's structural consistency is more complex" (line 111). Merge: "This process can be reversed in order to enable removal of B-tree nodes [Lomet 2004]. The first step creates a neighbor pointer, the second step erases the child pointer in the parent node, and the third step merges the removal victim with its neighbor node" (line 117). Pointer to Comer 1979 as an alternative (merge with a sibling before splitting): line 39.
- Assumes: pages in a buffer pool, short latches.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): it answers how long a half-split lasts: unbounded, and any later traversal may finish it; correctness does not need the parent step, only search efficiency does. The warning "long linked lists among neighbor nodes during high insertion rates" is the risk for a job that grows fast. Small job: none. Very large: the step-by-step reversible merge (Lomet 2004) is the one named merge recipe not needing a global stop; not opened.
- Status: quoted.

## What the eight papers answer (summary of the questions in the brief)

- How a split happens while others work: in two steps, local then parent (P1 line 162-163; P2 lines 183-189; P8 line 110). Who completes it: the splitter (P1 line 359; P6 line 261), or whoever meets the half state first (P2 line 70; P3 line 164), or any later traversal (P8 line 111). quoted.
- How long a half-split state lasts: not given as a time anywhere in the papers read. P1: "must be added quickly" (line 163). P8: "may be delayed beyond a system reboot". P6: held inside the tree latch, which is short. quoted; "not in the papers read", not "no paper".
- Merge: Lehman-Yao (P1 line 478), ARIES/IM (P6 lines 419, 512) and the simulation study (P7 line 64) do not merge until a node is empty; Bw-tree does merge (P2 lines 195-198) with more steps and a "stack" of structure changes; Lanin-Shasha 1986 (concurrent merge, called "half-merge" in P7 line 132) was not opened. quoted.
- Serialised or not: ARIES/IM serialises one structure change per tree with a tree latch (P6 line 263) and says concurrent ones are possible with a lock but risk deadlock (line 612). Lehman-Yao, Bw-tree allow several at once. quoted.
- Measured gains against lock coupling, with workload and cores: P4 (6.7x cost growth, 20 threads, 50M keys), P5 (3.9x, 20 threads, 100M keys), P7 (simulation, no ratio in the text). Bw-tree against BerkeleyDB (P2) is not against lock coupling alone; Wang 2018 (P3) shows the Bw-tree is slower than OLC trees. quoted.
- Root change: P5 gives the check that the root has not changed (line 117); no paper read described a root split step in words. Not found in the text read, which is not "no paper".

## Notes for the coordinator (reasoned)

- On this PC the university hosts throttled downloads (about 10 to 30 KB per second); a 2 MB paper takes 2 to 8 minutes. A download cut at `--max-time` yields a damaged PDF, so a size check that looks plausible is not enough: `pdftotext` must read it.
- The lane's question "which kind fits small, growing and very large" is answered by these papers only for in-memory or disk trees with microsecond to millisecond operations. None of the eight measured a number of writers between 3 and 16, or minute-long units. The finding that stands across scales: pessimistic locking of the root caps throughput (P7 line 471); everything that takes the lock off the upper levels (link pointers, version checks) wins once there are several writers and loses nothing with one (P5 line 308 "very similar performance" at one thread).
- No paper read states a law in ratios for the number of structure changes per insert; Lehman-Yao state "splits are exceptional" and "collision probability small for large node capacity" (P1 lines 164, 486) without a figure.
