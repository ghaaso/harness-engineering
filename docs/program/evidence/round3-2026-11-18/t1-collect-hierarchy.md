## Gate summary
1. Kim 2025: one orchestrator over 3 agents (depth 1, a star) gave trace-level error amplification 4.4 against 17.2 for 3 independent agents (quoted, :158-159, Table 5 at :1153-1159), but the paper says error amplification is not a significant predictor (:978-983).
2. Kim 2025: turns grow as 2.72 x (n+0.5)^1.724 in agent count; "beyond 3-4 agents" per-agent capacity is thin at a fixed budget; centralized costs 285% extra tokens, 21.5 against 67.7 successes per 1K tokens for one agent (quoted, :1175-1189, :1162).
3. Huang 2025: "hierarchical" (-5.51) against "linear" (-23.72) is MAD and AgentVerse (a judge over 2-3 debaters; 3-4 agents) against MetaGPT and Self-collab, one faulty agent at a time (quoted, :287-303, :313). It is depth 1, different systems, not a controlled depth test.
4. Huang 2025: adding one inspector agent that reviews all messages restored 43.3 to 75.0 (Self-collab, code; baseline 76.2) (quoted, :510-513, :548-553).
5. Armstrong 2003 + OTP: a supervisor that restarts a child more than MaxR times in MaxT seconds fails itself and its parent decides; defaults 1 and 5; the sustained rate is MaxR/MaxT (quoted, thesis :5933, OTP sup.txt:139-143, :153-155).
6. Dremel 2010: a 1:2900 two-level tree is "off the chart" for an aggregation with many groups; 1:100:2900 gave a 3 s query, and an extra level halved the other query (quoted, :447-458).
7. Astrolabe 2003: members keep levels x branching summaries (8 x 5 = 40 at 390,625 members, branching 5); branching 5 to 50 advised; a reorganisation touches only the leaf zone (quoted, :1588-1589, :1539-1550).
8. TAG 2002: sending partial results up the tree cut bytes by an order of magnitude against sending everything, equal to centralized in a flat one-hop net or for MEDIAN (a result needing all inputs) (quoted, :631-632, :625-626, :589-613).
9. MacNet 2024: in a DAG of agents, wider star topologies beat deeper trees at equal density; divergent beats convergent; chain 0.6078, star 0.6267, tree 0.6015 (quoted, :358-361, :379-385, Table 1 :291-293).
10. Anthropic blog 2025: lead agent plus 3-5 parallel subagents; about 15x the tokens of chat, 4x for a single agent; outputs to files with references passed up; a lead waiting synchronously is a bottleneck (quoted, :56-60, :93, :119-120, :133).
All numbers above hold only for the sizes tested; none of these papers tested depth 2 or more of language-model sessions (F4).

## Findings (cross-paper, each with the mark)
- F1 (quoted). The figure "4.4 against 17.2" compares a star of 3 agents plus an orchestrator with 3 agents that never talk, in a study of 260 configurations; the
  tree was never deeper than one level. The paper says: "Centralized ... A single orchestrator coordinates ... Sequential depth equals ..." (P:kim-scaling/paper.txt:381-383) and
  "multi-agent systems deploy 3 agents" (:2227). Its own caveat: error amplification main effect "not significant (beta 0.014, p=0.658)" (:978-980); efficiency and overhead explain
  performance better (:981-984). So the figure is a mechanism hint (a checking parent contains errors), not evidence for depth or fan-out.
- F2 (quoted). "-5.51 against -23.72" are average drops in points under one injected faulty agent, hierarchical against linear. The paper's "hierarchical" systems are MAD
  (two debaters, one judge) and AgentVerse (four agents) (P:huang-faulty/paper.txt:298-303); "linear" is MetaGPT (5) and Self-collab (3) (:208-211, :287-293). The paper itself gives
  a second pair of numbers for the same result (12.1% and 9.2%, :584-585) and a third reading in the abstract (5.5% against 10.5% and 23.7%, :23-25). Different systems, tasks, and GPT-3.5/4o; one fault at a time.
  Its explanation: a higher-level agent "is always presented with various versions of the answer by multiple agents performing the same sub-task" (:327-330). Inference (reasoned): the gain is the
  parent comparing parallel answers, not the shape of the tree.
- F3 (quoted + reasoned). The two numbers the attack record used support the claim "a checking parent over parallel children beats a chain and beats unchecked peers" at 3 to 5 agents. They do not support: a tree of
  several levels, a fan-out of 10 or 16, or a coordinator that rearranges its children. For a tree of sessions at three to sixteen leaves, the measured size range was 2 to 25 agents (Huang, Kim up to 9, MacNet up to 1,275 with different task type).
- F4 (reasoned, a gap). Not found in the eight sources read: any controlled study of depth 2 or more, or of fan-out above 9, for model sessions with token counts. MacNet is the only one varying depth (chain, star, tree, layered), with small differences (0.5629 to 0.6522) and
  no confidence intervals in Table 1 text. "Not found in these papers" is not "no paper": other names worth a search are "agent hierarchy depth", "recursive agents", "tree of agents", "hierarchical planner executor", "map-reduce agents".
- F5 (quoted). Ratio-form laws that came out: (a) Astrolabe state per member = levels x branching, levels = log_b(N) (P:astrolabe/paper.txt:1588-1589; :1832 limits work per round to levels x (branching-1)); (b) OTP sustained restart rate = intensity / period (sup.txt:153-155), escalate when exceeded;
  (c) Kim turns = 2.72 (n+0.5)^1.724, a super-linear law in agent count (:1175-1177); (d) TAG: the gain from a tree is the ratio of partial-record size to raw inputs: "In a flat, single-hop environment ... TAG is no better than the centralized approach" (:622-626) and MEDIAN costs the same as centralized (:589-590).
- F6 (quoted). What flows in what volume. Dremel and TAG: a small instruction goes down; fixed-size partial results come up and are combined at each level ("On the way up, intermediate servers perform a parallel aggregation of partial results", dremel:350-351). The up-flow shrinks at each level only when the combine step is cheap and the result size is bounded by the summary,
  not by the inputs. Anthropic: subagents "pass lightweight references back to the coordinator" (anthropic-research/paper.txt:133). For a research tree whose up-flow is a report, this is the reason a coordinator's window does not grow with leaf count (reasoned).

## Papers

### P1. Armstrong, "Making reliable distributed systems in the presence of software errors", PhD thesis, KTH 2003. Read from: https://erlang.org/download/armstrong_thesis_2003.pdf. Folder: P:armstrong/. Pages read: chapters 4.3-4.4 (workers and supervisors), 5.1-5.2 (supervision hierarchies), 6.5 (supervisor API and example); not the whole thesis (11,226 lines).
- Problem: errors in a process must be handled without the worker containing error code, and the system must survive hardware loss.
- Mechanism: a tree of supervisors over workers; a supervisor restarts a failed child (OR) or stops and restarts all children (AND); a supervisor that restarts too often fails itself and its parent decides.
- Measured gain: none; it is a design thesis. Nothing quantitative about token or time cost.
- Operations: "One process, the worker process, does the job. Another process, the supervisor process. observes the worker. If an error occurs in the worker, the supervisor takes actions to correct the error." (armstrong/paper.txt:4363-4367). "If the worker process fails with a non-normal exit then the supervisor will assume that the task has failed and will initiate some error recovery procedure. The error recovery procedure might be to restart the worker or failing this try to do something simpler." (:4768-4771). "If any child dies and I am an AND supervisor stop all my children and restart all my children. If any child dies and I am an OR supervisor restart the child that died." (:4912-4916). "if the supervisor has to restart the processes which it is monitoring more than 5 times in 1000 seconds then it itself will fail." (:5932-5934). "Supervisors can supervise an arbitrary number of workers or supervisors." (:4842). Each supervisor except the top has exactly one parent (:4849-4851). No split or merge of the tree: its shape is fixed by a static specification (SSRS, :4843-4846).
- Assumes: processes are cheap, a restart costs microseconds, state is small or recoverable, failures are mostly transient; restart intensity is read from experience.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the supervision rules transfer (OR for independent leaves, AND for dependent ones, count restarts, escalate). The cost assumption does not: a restart of a session costs a full brief and a window of tokens (library x19-0408 states this; not re-measured here), and a rerun is not the same run, so "restart" is a new draw and a deterministic fault will repeat (Gray 1985 via library x19-0446, summary). Intensity must therefore be small (OTP default is 1 in 5 s).
- Status: quoted (sections read), design thesis.

### P2. Erlang/OTP documentation, "Supervisor Behaviour" (design principles, supervisor), erlang.org. Read from: https://www.erlang.org/doc/system/sup_princ.html (HTML fetched with curl and stripped of tags by me, not a model summary). Folder: P:otp/. Pages read: whole page (406 lines of text).
- Problem: bound the damage of a restart loop and decide who gives up.
- Mechanism: strategies one_for_one, one_for_all, rest_for_one, simple_one_for_one; a maximum restart intensity (MaxR in MaxT seconds); on overrun the supervisor kills its children and itself, and the next supervisor above restarts it or stops.
- Measured gain: none (documentation).
- Operations: "If more than MaxR number of restarts occur in the last MaxT seconds, the supervisor terminates all the child processes and then itself." (otp/sup.txt:139-141). "When the supervisor terminates, then the next higher-level supervisor takes some action. It either restarts the terminated supervisor or terminates itself." (:141-142). Defaults: "they default to 1 and 5, respectively." (:143-144). Ratio rule: "if an intensity value of 5 is chosen, setting the period to 30 seconds will give you at most one restart per 6 seconds for any longer period of time" (:153-155); "same sustained error rate as 5/30 or 10/60" (:163-164, burst versus sustained). Advice: with no live monitoring, "at most one restart per minute before the supervisor should give up and escalate to the next level" (:158-160).
- Assumes: restarts are fast and the cause is transient; the sustained rate is the quantity to bound.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): a law in ratio form (restarts per unit time) is exactly the kind the owner prefers, but a session unit is minutes, so the period must be hours and the intensity 1 or 2; "escalate" means the parent coordinator takes a decision with its own window and tokens. Small work: one child, the parent is the top; very large: escalation climbs levels and each level must have a window with room for it.
- Status: quoted.

### P3. Kim, Gu, Park, Park, Schmidgall, Heydari, Yan, Zhang, Zhuang, Liu, Malhotra, Liang, Park, Yang, Xu, Du, Patel, Althoff, McDuff, Liu, "Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems", arXiv 2512.08296v3, April 2026. Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.08296. Folder: P:kim-scaling/. Pages read: abstract, sections 1-4 (architectures, metrics, Table 2, Table 5, scaling fits), limitations, appendix setup lines 2220-2310; not every appendix table.
- Problem: when does adding agents help; which of single, independent, centralized, decentralized, hybrid fits which task.
- Mechanism: 260 configurations, 6 benchmarks, 3 model families, matched total reasoning tokens (mean 4,800 per trial), a regression on measured coordination metrics.
- Measured gain: "Relative performance change compared to single-agent baseline ranges from +80.8% on decomposable financial reasoning to -70.0% on sequential planning" (kim:28-30). Error amplification (Table 5, :1153-1159): single 1.0, independent 17.2, decentralized 7.8, centralized 4.4, hybrid 5.1; success 0.466, 0.370, 0.477, 0.463, 0.452; turns 7.2, 11.4, 26.1, 27.7, 44.3; overhead 0, 58, 263, 285, 515 per cent; success per 1K tokens 67.7, 42.4, 23.9, 21.5, 13.6 (the table is flattened by the extractor; row order checked against :1166 and :1382-1384: "Centralized drops to 21.5 (3.1x worse) ... Hybrid to 13.6 (5.0x worse)"). Capability ceiling: coordination "yields diminishing returns" above about 45% single-agent accuracy (:155, :1070). Mixed capability: "sub-agent capability matters more than orchestrator capability" (:1260).
- Operations: centralized = orchestrator only talks to agents, hierarchical aggregation, rounds r, sequential depth r with a parallel factor n (:381-383); an orchestrator can override sub-agent outputs (:356-358). No split, merge or rebalancing: the number of agents is fixed per run (n = 3, :2227-2229; exploration "up to nine", :1449-1450).
- Assumes: matched token budget (so per-agent budget shrinks as agents are added: "per-agent reasoning capacity becomes prohibitively thin beyond 3-4 agents", :1186-1188), tool-using agentic tasks, a fresh run each trial.
- Turn law: "T = 2.72 x (n + 0.5)^1.724, R^2 = 0.974, 95% CI on exponent: [1.685, 1.763]" (:1175-1177); the power law "predicts 69 turns at n=6 and 157 turns at n=10" (:1370-1372). Optimal overhead band "200% < overhead < 300%" (:1345).
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the budget is not matched in our tree (each leaf has its own window), so the "thin per-agent capacity" result does not carry over directly; the super-linear turn law and the up-front-overhead percentages do (they are per-trial token multiples at 3 agents). Depth is 1, so nothing on inner levels. Small jobs: centralized gave +80.8% on decomposable tasks and -50.3% on sequential ones, which says the choice of shape depends on whether the job divides (a ratio is not stated; the paper uses a regression on task properties). Very large: the authors say scaling beyond moderate team size "remains an open question" (:1450-1455).
- Caveat that bears on our use: after controls, error amplification is not significant (:978-983), so the explanation "a checking parent contains errors" is partly a hypothesis of the authors.
- Status: quoted.

### P4. Huang, Zhou, Jin, Zhou, Chen, Wang, Yuan, Lyu, Sap, "On the Resilience of LLM-Based Multi-Agent Collaboration with Faulty Agents", ICML 2025 (arXiv 2408.00989v4). Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.00989. Folder: P:huang-faulty/. Pages read: abstract, sections 1-5 and conclusion; appendix not read in full.
- Problem: how much does one clumsy or malicious agent cost, by system structure, and can it be repaired.
- Mechanism: six existing systems in three structures (linear, flat, hierarchical); one faulty agent injected by rewriting its profile (AutoTransform) or its messages (AutoInject); remedies are Challenger (each agent may challenge others) and Inspector (an extra agent that reviews and corrects all messages).
- Measured gain: "the 'hierarchical' structure, i.e., A(BC), exhibits superior resilience with the lowest performance drop of 5.5%, compared to 10.5% and 23.7% of other two structures" (huang:20-27, abstract); body: hierarchical -5.51, flat -10.54, linear -23.72 (:293-297). Repair: Self-collab on HumanEval-type code, no defense under AutoTransform 43.3, Challenger 70.7, Inspector 74.4, both 75.0, baseline without errors 76.2 (:508-513); "recovers up to 96.4% of the lost performance" (:551-553). Camel: 32.5 to 48.7 with both, baseline 62.2 (:517-519).
- Operations: hierarchical = "rooted directed trees, where there exists a unique root agent" with a strict top-down hierarchy (:103-106 and the paragraph beside it); the systems tested are MAD (two debaters and a judge) and AgentVerse (four agents) (:298-303); linear is MetaGPT (5) and Self-collab (3); flat is Camel (2) and SPP (25 personas of one model) (:208-211). One faulty agent at a time, normal agents "remain unaware of the faulty agent's presence" (:313-315). Other findings: "the frequency of erroneous messages impacts resilience more than the number of errors within a single message" (:585-587 area, conclusion); more rounds or agents did not help ("the effect of the number of agents or rounds is limited", :515-516).
- Assumes: temperature 0, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o, one fault, tasks of code, math, translation, evaluation; no token cost reported in the parts read.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the parent's job here, comparing several versions of one answer, is what a research coordinator does with parallel leaves; the repair with an inspector agent is a second checker, equal in cost to one more child. The study shows nothing about a parent whose child overflows its window or is slow. It cannot support a depth claim: all three structures have 2 to 5 agents (25 for SPP). A faulty rate of 1 in 3 to 1 in 5 agents was the fault density tested; ratio of faulty to total is a variable the paper itself says matters (:104-107 "Increasing the ratio of Faulty Messages ... greater performance drop").
- Status: quoted. Library x21-0318's second figure is read here exactly; its wording in the attack record ("hierarchy lost -5.51 against -23.72 for a chain") is a fair reading only with the F2 limits.

### P5. Melnik, Gubarev, Long, Romer, Shivakumar, Tolton, Vassilakis, "Dremel: Interactive Analysis of Web-Scale Datasets", VLDB 2010. Read from: https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/36632.pdf. Folder: P:dremel/. Pages read: sections 1, 5-8 (serving tree, query dispatcher, experiments); not the column-striping sections in detail.
- Problem: interactive queries over trillions of rows on thousands of machines, with stragglers and failures.
- Mechanism: a multi-level serving tree. The root rewrites a query and sends parts to the next level; leaves scan tablets; intermediate servers aggregate partial results upward. A dispatcher balances load and reschedules slow tablets.
- Measured gain: the tree-depth experiment (Figure 11, text :445-458): leaves fixed at 2,900; "2-level topology (1:2900)", "3 levels ... 1:100:2900", "4-level ... 1:10:100:2900". "Query Q2 runs in 3 seconds when 3 levels are used in the serving tree and does not benefit much from an extra level. In contrast, the execution time of Q3 is halved due to increased parallelism. At 2 levels, Q3 is off the chart, as the root server needs to aggregate near-sequentially the results received from thousands of nodes." (dremel:452-457, interleaved columns). The second table of times is in the figure, not in the text; only these statements were read. Also "an order of magnitude" from columnar storage and another from the engine (:464-468), which is not a tree result.
- Operations: "Dremel uses a multi-level serving tree" (:297); queries are rewritten per level ("Each serving level performs a similar rewriting", :343-345); "On the way up, intermediate servers perform a parallel aggregation of partial results" (:350-351); each leaf holds about 5 tablets per slot (100,000 tablets over 24,000 slots, :372-375). Stragglers: "If a tablet takes a disproportionately long time to process, it reschedules it on another server. Some tablets may need to be redispatched multiple times." (:377-380 region). Partial scan: the dispatcher honours "the minimum percentage of tablets that must be scanned before returning a result" (98% instead of 100% speeds up, :388-392 region). Parent failure: not dealt with in the text read.
- Assumes: partial results are small and combinable (aggregations with small or medium outputs), nodes are fast processes, leaves hold replicated data so a repeat is cheap; "Large aggregations and other classes of queries may need to rely on execution mechanisms known from parallel DBMSs and MR" (:356-358).
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the lesson is the ratio at the root: the root's work grows with the number of children whose results it must combine, so insert a level when the fan-in is in the thousands; with 10 to 16 sessions no extra level is needed (a root with 16 children is far below the 1:100 scale where Dremel added a level). The "redispatch slow tablets" idea corresponds to a rerun of a leaf, but a rerun is a different run and costs a window. The "accept 98%" rule corresponds to stopping with some leaves unfinished, which Dremel allows because a miss is statistical; a research tree has no such tolerance (reasoned).
- Status: quoted for the statements above; the actual times by depth are in a figure, not read.

### P6. van Renesse, Birman, Vogels, "Astrolabe: A Robust and Scalable Technology for Distributed System Monitoring, Management, and Data Mining", ACM TOCS 21(2) 2003. Read from: https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/quicksilver/public_pdfs/Astrolabe.pdf. Folder: P:astrolabe/. Pages read: abstract, section 1-2 principles, 4.5, 7 (performance; the figures were not read as numbers).
- Problem: keep a summary of the state of thousands to millions of nodes, with changing membership.
- Mechanism: a zone tree; each zone summarises its children with SQL aggregation queries; members gossip with siblings, so no node holds more than levels x branching summaries.
- Measured gain: "these confirm that Astrolabe could scale to thousands and perhaps millions of nodes, with information propagation delays in the tens of seconds." (astrolabe:24-26). Simulation up to 5^8 = 390,625 members, branching 5, 25, 125 (:1564-1573). "at 390,625 members and branching factor 5, there are 8 levels in the tree. Thus each member has to maintain and gossip only 8 x 5 = 40 MIBs." (:1588-1589). "Flat gossip provides the lowest dissemination latency" and "Hierarchical gossip ... is significantly slower"; "Latency improves when the branching factor is increased, but doing so also increases overhead." (:1583-1586). Round counts are in Figure 6 (not read).
- Operations: "the number of child zones should be approximately between 5 to 50" (:1539-1545). "Messages ... grow in size approximately as a function of the branching factor" and about 200 to 300 bytes per MIB, so 25 to 40 MIBs per 8 KB message (:1157-1164). "As the membership grows, it may be necessary to create more zones. Initially, new machines can be added simply to leaf zones, but at some point it becomes necessary to divide the leaf zones into smaller zones. Note that this re-configuration only involves the machines in that leaf zone." (:1546-1551). That is the split operation, local to one leaf. The check work per round "is at most k x (bf - 1), where k is the number of levels and bf the branching factor" (:1832-1833).
- Assumes: summaries bounded in size ("Given bounds on the size of information in a zone", :175-176), eventual consistency, node count up to millions, cheap messages.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the ratio law (levels = log_b N, per-node state = levels x b) transfers as a design rule for the coordinator's window: its state is b summaries per level, not N reports. For N = 16 leaves and b between 5 and 16 the tree has 2 levels; a third level is needed only above b^2. Here a "node" is a session whose window is bounded, so "summary bounded in size" is the assumption to preserve: a child's report must be capped. A split of a leaf touching only that leaf is the property to borrow; whether a new session can be started without pausing the parent is a property of the app, not of the paper.
- Status: quoted (text); the latency figure was not read.

### P7. Madden, Franklin, Hellerstein, Hong, "TAG: a Tiny AGgregation Service for Ad-Hoc Sensor Networks", OSDI 2002. Read from: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi02/tech/full_papers/madden/madden.pdf. Folder: P:tag/. Pages read: sections 1-5 and 7 (loss); optimisation sections skimmed.
- Problem: compute aggregates (count, min, average, median) over hundreds of battery sensors without sending every reading to the root.
- Mechanism: a routing tree built by flooding a request; each node sends its parent a partial state record; interior nodes merge their children's records with their own, one message per epoch.
- Measured gain: "in network aggregation can reduce communication costs by an order of magnitude over centralized approaches, and that, even in the worst case (such as with MEDIAN), it provides performance equal to the centralized approach." (tag:631-635). Numbers in the text: MEDIAN about 90,000 bytes per epoch, COUNT DISTINCT 73,000, HISTOGRAM about 9,000 messages, diameter 50, no loss (:589-613). Loss: with a plain tree "at diameter 10, about 40% of the partial state records are reflected in the aggregate at the root; by diameter 50, this percentage has fallen to less than 10%" (:815-818); a child cache raises it (section 7.4, :828-840).
- Operations: "each leaf node in the tree reports a count of 1 to their parent; interior nodes sum the count of their children, add 1 to it, and report that value to their parent." (:84-88). Parent selection by level; a child that no longer hears its parent "assumes its parent has failed or moved away", resets its level and picks a new parent (:777-780, :795 region). Aggregate classes by size of the partial state record (distributive, algebraic, holistic, unique, content-sensitive; classification at :288-300 area, summary versus exemplary).
- Assumes: aggregates whose partial record is small and mergeable; high loss; the cost measured is radio bytes.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): the gain exists only when the merge shrinks the data; a report that must be read whole by the parent behaves like MEDIAN (no gain). "In a flat, single-hop environment ... TAG is no better than the centralized approach." (:622-626): with 3 to 16 leaves the tree is flat, so the benefit of an inner level is nil, which is the lower-bound direction for the small project. Loss compounds per hop (:821-822): in a research tree, an unusable report at an inner level loses the whole subtree, so inner levels should be few.
- Status: quoted.

### P8. Chen Qian and others (Tsinghua), "Scaling Large-Language-Model-based Multi-Agent Collaboration" (MacNet), arXiv 2406.07155v1, June 2024. Read from: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.07155v1. Folder: P:macnet/. Pages read: abstract, sections 2-5 (about 650 lines; evaluation tables, scaling figure by text only).
- Problem: does quality scale with the number of agents, and does topology matter.
- Mechanism: agents are nodes of a directed acyclic graph; solutions flow in topological order; topologies chain, star, tree, mesh, layered, random; nodes increase from 1 to 50 (up to 1,275 agents for mesh).
- Measured gain: average scores over four datasets (MMLU, HumanEval, SRDD, CommonGen), Table 1 (macnet:291-296): chain 0.6078, star 0.6267, tree 0.6015, mesh 0.6316, layered 0.5629, random 0.6522; single CoT 0.5757; AgentVerse 0.5805. "optimal outcomes are achieved in high-density networks" (:429-431); "Typically, divergent structures (those with more child nodes than parent nodes) significantly outperform convergent counterparts" (:379-382); "given the same density, 'wider' star-shaped topologies generally outperform 'deeper' tree-shaped ones" (:357-359); deeper "may cause agents to lose sight of farther contexts ... version rollback" (:361-366). Scaling: quality "can be approximated by a sigmoid-shaped function", saturation "around a scale of 2^4 to 2^5" agents (:440-452); some settings degrade 2.27% to 6.24% (:433-435).
- Operations: topological order; no inner rebalancing; no cost per node in the parts read beyond "associated time and economic costs" (:423-424).
- Assumes: fixed topology, short tasks (one answer per node), a single model; the data show no confidence intervals in the text of Table 1.
- With sessions as nodes (reasoned): it is the only source here varying depth, and it favours wide over deep and divergent over convergent, the opposite of a tree that gathers many reports into one parent. Saturation around 16 to 32 agents lines up with the 3 to 16 sessions that are run, but the unit (one short answer per agent) is far from a session of minutes.
