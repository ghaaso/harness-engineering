What this measurement could not measure (10)
  • Run-to-run drift: the untracked program documents are read at their live content when baseline.js.txt runs, and other sessions keep writing them, so a re-run can move a baseline cell by an instance or two (seen once: 29.6 to 29.5 %).
  • Containment of the whole needed span: a baseline "hit" means a result points into a truth span (overlap), not that the result covers all of it.
  • Which lines of a read the agent actually used: confirmation covers only later edits, file:line citations and verbatim quotes; about 9 % of model calls carry visible text (use-cases.md section 11), so "read but not confirmed" is not "unused".
  • The exact code version: transcripts carry no commit; the version is the branch tip at the episode time (main when the branch no longer exists), and uncommitted working-tree changes are invisible.
  • Program documents outside version control: they can only be indexed at their current content, which may hold text written after the session (counted per form as needs-current-docs, and per baseline as hits on current-only files).
  • Truth outside the repository (scratchpad files, transcripts, memory, web): no repository index can return it; these instances are counted, not scored.
  • Whether an agent given the index result would have stopped reading: the baselines score containment of the truth span among the first 10 results and its token cost, not the agent behaviour.
  • Program question forms are labelled by rules that a manual check found right on about 3 in 4 calls (use-cases.md section 2.4); "earlier lane findings" (the question “earlier conclusions”) is the noisiest label.
  • Confirmation-part numbers: the baselines were computed on the development part only, by design; nothing on the confirmation part has been scored.
  • Product work is 13 main sessions of one day (2026-11-13) and one plan; the development part holds 8 of them and the confirmation part 5. A per-form product number rests on few sessions.

Where a relation joins more than two things, a pairwise edge loses information

Commit or PR change set

Joins: k files and the entities in them, message, PR, date

Pairwise edges lose: Which pairs were two of thirty; the commit's reason (message) and PR; the order and time of the set. k(k-1)/2 edges per commit instead of k

Measured: 49 % of changed-together edges (930 of 1,896) rest only on commits of 10 or more files; commits of 16 to 30 files hold 55.1 % of pair incidences (4,635 of 8,411). measured: lane-modelling.md

Rule with several anchors

Joins: statement, conditions, exemptions, anchors in different roles (server guard, client half, dependent rule), rationale

Pairwise edges lose: The roles. v1's constrains edge says "this invariant mentions this symbol"; "the other half of this rule" (the question “the other half of a contract” and the question “which rule applies”, 2 to 3 hops in the schema lane) has no way to tell the guard from the example

The current graph's “constrains” edge says only that a rule mentions a symbol; it cannot tell the server guard from the client half or an example. measured: v1-audit.md

Claim with its dependency set

Joins: statement, and the set of spans, hashes and negative facts ("no other caller") it rests on

Pairwise edges lose: The set as a unit: a pairwise rests_on is fine as long as all members are present; the point of the node is that the Claim has a status and a producer

Replay of cached answers: 0 % of served answers wrong on 29,097 lookups with fact-level premises, against 6.5 % with file-level ones. measured: cache replay, widen/k0

  • Cannot: findings resting on outside sources (25 %), on one-off measurements whose inputs are gone (22 %), and the judgement layer (63 % carry some bare judgement; 68 % are interpretation).   • What recording premises at writing time would have required: about 3 to 4 premise records per finding (median 3, up to 10). Do learned graph embeddings add to the simple ranker?

No arm adds to the simple ranker on this repository.

Four knowledge-graph embedding models and one graph-neural encoder were each fused with the simple ranker and compared with it on the 50 later tuning commits. On top-ten precision every fused arm "matches" it: the largest gain is +0.0023 inside the candidate pool and +0.0032 over all code files, where the protocol asked for at least +0.02 before it would say "adds". A real gain of up to about +0.006 in the pool, and about +0.007 over all code files, cannot be ruled out; that is about a third of the bar. Alone, RotatE and TransE match the simple ranker. ComplEx-N3 and CompGCN match inside the candidate pool and are lower over all code files. The routed specialists are lower in both.

Why, from the audit: the models learn well, but mostly they recover the co-change facts they were trained on, which the simple ranker already has. Below those they order the other candidates about as well as rooted PageRank does. Fused with the simple ranker they add no gain that the protocol's +0.02 bar could register; a small one, of about +0.006 in the pool at most, is not ruled out.

Why: six reasons from the audit

Points 2 to 5 come from the audit's refuter, who set out to show that "matches" is an artifact. Point 1 comes from the auditors of stage A and of the arms' code, point 6 from the auditor of the routed specialists. A second agent re-tested points 2, 5 and 6 and corrected them; the corrected forms are given here. Point 1's figures are the lane's stage-A table, with a caveat from two auditors; points 3 and 4 rest on one agent's computation.

  1. The models learn. On stage A's held-out co-change pairs RotatE, ComplEx-N3, TransE and CompGCN reach a mean reciprocal rank of 0.68 to 0.75, against 0.28 for rooted PageRank. Rooted PageRank's graph has no co-change edges, so this shows that the models learned the co-change structure, not that they beat a ranker that has it.
  2. Mostly they recover what they were trained on. Alone, RotatE, ComplEx-N3, TransE and CompGCN separate a file's known co-change partners from the other candidates almost perfectly (AUC 0.996 to 1.000). Inside that block they do not follow co-change confidence's order (median rank correlation 0.07 to 0.29). Below it their order is moderately correlated with rooted PageRank's (0.49 to 0.63 in the pool) and about as accurate: top-ten precision on candidates without co-change history is 0.196 to 0.213, against rooted PageRank's 0.208. It is not the same order.
  3. They find little that is new. On those candidates no arm beats rooted PageRank by a margin this test can see. The refuter's own contrast, with the truth restricted in the same way, gives RotatE +0.004 [-0.005, +0.012] and TransE +0.001; ComplEx-N3, CompGCN and the routed specialists score below rooted PageRank. The data do not show that the embeddings carry no information of their own; they show that it is too little to move the top ten.
  4. More weight does not help. Outside the protocol's grid (weights 2, 4 and 8, and the arm alone) no arm's gain over the simple ranker exceeds +0.006 in the pool. The room was there: a perfect order would gain +0.119 in the pool.
  5. In the pool the fused list can hardly differ from the simple ranker's. There are two causes. The fusion the protocol fixed (k = 60) keeps a candidate with co-change history above one without: in the fused tables the co-change candidates hold the top places in 224 to 226 of the 226 tasks where that can be tested. And the selection gave low weights to arms that added nothing. So the fused top ten shares 9.1 to 9.3 of its 10 files with the simple ranker's, and the placebo moves the list as much as the real models do. "Adds" was still reachable: a perfect score put through the same selection takes weight 1 and gains +0.060 [+0.042, +0.079], and a half-informative one +0.036. But that is the most the primary contrast can show, so "matches" rules out a third to a half of the gain that could be reached, not all of it. The pool "matches" is a weak comparison; the arms alone, the unrestricted lists and the weights beyond the grid (points 2 to 4) carry the conclusion.
  6. The routed specialists. The routed specialists' shortfall alone lies entirely in the 192 tasks that were routed to two areas. On the 121 tasks routed to one area the routed specialists equals RotatE (0.2226 against 0.2219). About 60 % of the routed specialists' gap to RotatE on the two-area tasks comes from the merge the protocol wrote (item 13): a candidate that only the second specialist knows enters with that one percentile and takes top-ten places at about 5 % precision. The rest is not explained by this test. Candidates that no specialist knows explain at most 0.008 (the bound comes from the re-test of the arms auditor's finding).

The sentence the evidence supports: on this repository, at this size, the trained embeddings mostly recover the co-change facts they were trained on; below those they order candidates about as well as rooted PageRank, though not in the same order; and fused with the simple ranker they add nothing this test's bar could register (at most about +0.006 in pool top-ten precision, where the protocol asked for +0.02).

What this measurement could not measure (10)
  • Run-to-run drift: the untracked program documents are read at their live content when baseline.js.txt runs, and other sessions keep writing them, so a re-run can move a baseline cell by an instance or two (seen once: 29.6 to 29.5 %).
  • Containment of the whole needed span: a baseline "hit" means a result points into a truth span (overlap), not that the result covers all of it.
  • Which lines of a read the agent actually used: confirmation covers only later edits, file:line citations and verbatim quotes; about 9 % of model calls carry visible text (use-cases.md section 11), so "read but not confirmed" is not "unused".
  • The exact code version: transcripts carry no commit; the version is the branch tip at the episode time (main when the branch no longer exists), and uncommitted working-tree changes are invisible.
  • Program documents outside version control: they can only be indexed at their current content, which may hold text written after the session (counted per form as needs-current-docs, and per baseline as hits on current-only files).
  • Truth outside the repository (scratchpad files, transcripts, memory, web): no repository index can return it; these instances are counted, not scored.
  • Whether an agent given the index result would have stopped reading: the baselines score containment of the truth span among the first 10 results and its token cost, not the agent behaviour.
  • Program question forms are labelled by rules that a manual check found right on about 3 in 4 calls (use-cases.md section 2.4); "earlier lane findings" (the question “earlier conclusions”) is the noisiest label.
  • Confirmation-part numbers: the baselines were computed on the development part only, by design; nothing on the confirmation part has been scored.
  • Product work is 13 main sessions of one day (2026-11-13) and one plan; the development part holds 8 of them and the confirmation part 5. A per-form product number rests on few sessions.

Ruling R-7: the two-step test

The decisive test changes. An agent pilot of about 40 tasks can only see a saving of about 20 to 25 %, and the plausible effect is single-digit percent of context. So the evidence is built in two steps: a lookup-level replay (recorded lookups answered by each arm, scored on whether the needed span is in the answer and on tokens, which can see small effects), then a focused agent test on the session start, where the measured cost is largest (55 % of carried product lookup tokens are spent before the first edit) and an effect could be large enough to see.

What it shows. The share that moves is a fraction of the exposure. At the starting settings it sits well below what an agent pilot of 40 tasks can detect; only settings near the extremes (the agent uses the tool almost always, and answers are a small fraction of the reads they replace) reach that range. A lookup-level replay is the test that can see effects of this size. The arm a graph must beat is already measured: when the agent started from a search, keyword search over names, paths and headings returned a result overlapping the span the agent read in its top 10 for 42.1 % of scorable product lookups and 44 % of program ones (55.3 % and 55.3 % with path seeds included, where the agent already had the file).
