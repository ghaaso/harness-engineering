Rows hold what the fetch tool returned (a small model's summary of the page, not the page). Quotation marks mark sentences the fetched text gave as the paper's own. "figure not read" where it did not. Nothing here was run.

Generic task for the carry-over notes: from a relational history of timed events, predict which items appear together in the next event (recommendation / link prediction on a heterogeneous temporal graph, a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of events per database).

What this does and does not carry over to: RelGT's token design (type, hop, time, subgraph PE, K=300 neighbours) is a concrete recipe for a heterogeneous temporal graph and its sampling is time-valid, which fits timed events. But it was tested on entity classification and regression only, never on recommendation or link prediction, with training sets from 1.3K up; gains are task-dependent (some tasks lose, many tie). The pretrained models (RT, KumoRFM-2, Griffin) report zero-shot or few-shot gains on classification and regression; none of the fetched text reports next-event co-occurrence (set) prediction, so their value for "which items appear together" is untested in what was read.

What this does and does not carry over to: for item-set prediction the strongest published evidence is that a model that sees the pair (user context with candidate) beats plain two-tower embeddings by a wide margin, and that a "what was seen before" heuristic is a hard baseline (Past Visit beat LightGBM and GraphSAGE on average). It does not say how the models do on a few thousand events, since RelBench link tables are far larger, and the locality figures show that on several datasets most true next items are not in the 1-hop history, which sets a ceiling for any history-only rule. The numbers come from different papers and splits; compare only within one table.

What this does and does not carry over to: the failure that matters here is stated clearly in the literature: a model that gives each item one embedding cannot see that two items share a neighbour (co-occurred before), and the cheap fixes are common-neighbour pooling (NCN), a per-pair attention (LPFormer) or heuristic features next to the model. Scoring against hard negatives (items that already share neighbours with the query) lowers GNN scores a lot and lets heuristics look strong, so a co-occurrence task should be scored with such negatives. All of these were tested on homogeneous, static, citation or collaboration graphs, with hundreds of thousands of edges or more at the top end; no heterogeneous temporal event data was in these rows, and temporal leakage was not part of them.
