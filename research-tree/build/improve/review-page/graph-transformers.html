Graph transformers for our work: what fits, and what held our models back

25 November. Built from your slides, the published work (read at summary level, not the full papers) and our own code and logs. Nothing was run on the card, downloaded or rented for it.

A relational graph transformer is worth one fair test: in a pair-aware form, on richer tables and more data. It is not a swap on today's inputs, where published work and our own runs both say it would hit the same wall. And you were right that some of our choices were flawed.

Known failure modes, matched against our own runs

Present

One vector per file cannot hold pair facts. "These two changed together seven times" is lost. The graph alone scored below counting.

Present

The time-aware models looked through too short a window. Only each file's last 19 or 31 events. Our setting, not the idea.

Present

Big commits flood the graph network. A third to a half of all pair events come from commits of 20 or more files. Counting down-weights them; the network did not.

Present

One time-aware model trained with a shortcut it does not have when scored.

Present

Two of the three repositories are too small. A few hundred training cases each; runs swing widely.

Not the cause

Over-squashing, or too few layers. The needed fact is one step away; two layers scored the same as one.

Not found

Looking ahead in time. Every feature and graph is built only from before the change being predicted.

What published work says

  • Relational graph transformers were never tested on our kind of task. Only on classifying or scoring single rows. There: better on 10 of 21 tasks, level on 9, worse on 2.
  • On the closest benchmark, the pair-aware model wins. It scores about four times the best model that keeps one vector per item. A plain "seen before" rule beats an ordinary graph network there too, as in our runs.
  • With equal tuning, plain graph networks match graph transformers on 17 of 18 benchmarks in one reassessment. In another, the transformers' long-range lead vanished after basic tuning.
  • Over-squashing shows from about four steps away. Its own authors say local tasks are less affected.
  • Time-aware graph models: memorising rules match or beat them when most links were seen before. Easy training examples can leave a model unable to rank at all.
  • For "where else to edit", the models that work are given the edit and are trained across hundreds of projects. Nobody has shown history alone beating counting.
  • So a pair-aware relational transformer on this task would be new. A real test with a real chance of insight, and a bet rather than a likely win.

Not found in the search: any study of how much training data graph transformers need, or of what position encodings cost on a graph that grows over time.

The form that would fit us

Tablescommits, files, changes; then the edit's content and the code's structure
→
Sample around the paira few hundred neighbours of both files, only from before the change
→
Tokenscontent, table type, steps to each file, time gap, local structure
→
Attentionlocal over the sample, global over cluster centres
→
Pair scorejoined with the counts
DirectionOur links have direction (imports, calls, time), and a plain Laplacian ignores it, as you noted. Direction-aware encodings exist and were shown on code graphs.
PositionNo whole-graph Laplacian. Our graph grows with every commit, so it would have to be rebuilt at each point in time or it sees the future. Steps, type, time and a local encoding on the sample do the job, as in your screenshot.
CostAttention runs over a few hundred sampled tokens, so the n-squared cost over the whole graph never arises. At our size, cost is not the constraint.
TimeTime enters as a token. That keeps what the time-aware models know, without the memory part that trained wrongly.
Text to graphNot needed for code: a parser gives the graph exactly. The research we recorded found model-written graphs cost twenty to forty-five times more and skip files. Text enters as features.
What it must beatThe learned ranker and a tuned pair-aware graph network, on the same inputs and the same budget. Otherwise a win proves nothing.

Data

  • The large project is not short of data for the forms we tried. The two small repositories are, and no transformer will train on a few hundred cases.
  • What would extend it: the histories of many public projects. Two public collections hold about 11 million and 58 million commits with file lists and edits. The published "where else to edit" models trained on about 180,000 commits from 471 projects.
  • This is where rented cards would earn their keep: training one model across many projects, then applying it to ours.
  • Quality: very large commits blur which files truly belong together, and our test never asks about brand-new files.
